Vespasian — the general who became emperor

Researched 2026-11-19 for James's search list (item 3: "Who was Vespasian?"). Sources: Suetonius (Life of Vespasian), Tacitus (Histories), Josephus (Jewish War 3–4), Cassius Dio; the Colosseum inscription as reconstructed by Géza Alföldy (1995).

Where he came from — a "new man"

  • Titus Flavius Vespasianus, born AD 9 at Falacrinae, a village in the Sabine hills northeast of Rome. No senators in his family tree: his grandfather was said to have been a tax contractor's agent, his father a tax collector in Asia and a moneylender among the Helvetii (in modern Switzerland).
  • The sneer followed him all his life: Suetonius says he was nicknamed the "mule-driver" (mulio) because he once dealt in mules. In Roman terms he was a novus homo — a new man, the first of his family to reach the top.
  • He climbed the hard way: army service in Thrace, a successful command in Britain under Claudius (he fought some thirty battles there, per Suetonius), consul in AD 51, then governor of Africa — where he was so strapped for money, Suetonius says, that the mule-dealing that earned him the nickname happened.

Why Nero gave him the Jewish war (AD 66/67)

  • When Judea exploded in 66 and Cestius Gallus's legion was mauled, Nero needed a first-rate soldier. Suetonius and Josephus agree on the logic of the choice: Vespasian was able, experienced, in his late fifties — and his low birth made him seem safe. Nobody feared a tax collector's son would use a big army to grab the throne. It is one of history's great miscalculations.
  • He arrived in 67 with three legions and a host of auxiliaries and allied troops — about 60,000 men — and methodically crushed Galilee: Jotapata, Tarichaeae, Gamala, Gischala (see the-war-66-70.md).

Josephus's prophecy

  • Captured at Jotapata in 67, the rebel commander Josephus asked for a private word and told Vespasian: "You will become Caesar, Vespasian, you will become emperor" (Josephus, War 3.399–402). Vespasian kept him prisoner but alive — half hostage, half good-luck charm. When the prophecy came true, Josephus was freed and took the family name Flavius. It is Josephus's own account of himself, so read it with that in mind — but Vespasian's later favour to him is a fact.

Emperor (AD 69–79)

  • Nero died in 68; Rome burned through Galba, Otho and Vitellius in a year. On 1 July 69 the legions of Egypt proclaimed Vespasian emperor; his own army in Judea swore allegiance two days later, and the Danube legions won Italy for him. He left the Jewish war to his son Titus — with Agrippa II and Berenice bankrolling his bid (see berenice-deep-dive.md).
  • What kind of emperor: a repairman. He inherited an empty treasury and a burned Capitol. Suetonius says he declared he needed an enormous sum to set the state upright (the figure given, forty thousand millions of sesterces, is usually read as rhetorical, not literal). He raised taxes, sold offices, and was famously unembarrassed about money: when Titus objected to a tax on public urinals, Vespasian held a coin from the first takings to his son's nose and asked if it smelled — "pecunia non olet," money doesn't stink.
  • He was also a builder and, by the standards of the office, decent: hardworking, tolerant of criticism, witty, and he restored stability after civil war. He founded the Flavian dynasty — Titus and Domitian after him.
  • The Colosseum is his. He began the Flavian Amphitheatre (c. 72) on the drained lake of Nero's Golden House — giving the land back to the public. Its dedicatory inscription, its bronze letters long ago pried out, was reconstructed by Géza Alföldy in 1995 from the pattern of the letter-holes: "…amphitheatrum novum ex manubiis fieri iussit" — "ordered the new amphitheatre to be built from the spoils of war." The spoils in question were Jerusalem's: the Colosseum was paid for with the plunder of the Temple his son had destroyed.

His death — the last joke (AD 79)

  • Dying in June 79, he kept working — Suetonius: "An emperor ought to die standing," and he tried to rise. Then, feeling the end, he joked about the Roman custom of declaring dead emperors gods: "Vae, puto deus fio" — "Oh dear, I think I'm becoming a god."
  • He died on 23 June 79, aged 69, and was succeeded by Titus — the man who had burned the Temple, whose triumph Vespasian had shared in 71.
