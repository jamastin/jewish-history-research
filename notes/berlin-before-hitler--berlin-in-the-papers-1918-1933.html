Berlin in the papers, 1918–1933 — deeper newspaper pass

The search (2026-11-19): what was going on in Berlin before Hitler, all the way up till Hitler. The arc is in README-first-overview.md. This file is the era's own voices — an eyewitness, and the newspapers as they printed it.

June 24, 1922 — the Rathenau murder, in the morning papers' own words

Walther Rathenau — German-Jewish industrialist (son of Emil Rathenau, founder of AEG), foreign minister since February 1922, the man who negotiated the Treaty of Rapallo with Soviet Russia in April 1922 — was murdered on his way to work from his villa in Berlin-Grunewald.

  • How it happened (Richard J. Evans, London Review of Books, "Prophet in a Tuxedo": https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v34/n22/richard-j.-evans/prophet-in-a-tuxedo): on the morning of June 24, 1922, two men in long leather coats and leather driving helmets drew level with his limousine; one opened fire with a long-barrelled machine-pistol, the other lobbed a hand grenade into the back of the car. The killers were Erwin Kern and Hermann Fischer, driven by Ernst Techow, 21. All were members of the ultra-nationalist secret Organisation Consul, ex-Freikorps men; Kern was killed in a shoot-out and Fischer shot himself on July 17, 1922.
  • The trial, as the papers printed it — Manawatu Standard, October 11, 1922 (Papers Past: https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/MS19221011.2.25): "RATHENAU'S MURDER. ATTEMPT TO POISON PRISONER. SENSATION AT THE TRIAL… Dr. Walter Rathenau, German Minister for Foreign Affairs, was assassinated on June 24 near his home in Grunewald, a suburb of Berlin… two men in a car following fired ten shots with revolvers at Dr. Rathenau and threw a grenade. The grenade blew off the Minister's left leg and he expired immediately." Twelve members of the Bavarian secret society were arrested; the manhunt involved "hundreds of armed men in motor-cars and bicycles."
  • What it meant: Rathenau's murder was one of nearly 400 political assassinations in the Weimar years, mostly by rightists (Britannica). His death brought national mourning and huge demonstrations against counter-revolutionary terror, and briefly strengthened the republic — but after the Nazis took power in 1933, all commemorations of Rathenau were banned. He had been hated by the right both for the Versailles system and, openly, for being a Jew: Nazi leaders claimed he was part of a Jewish-Communist conspiracy.
  • Source: Britannica, "Walther Rathenau" (http://www.britannica.com/biography/Walther-Rathenau); Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Rathenau).

1923 — the hyperinflation, in an eyewitness's words

Stefan Zweig, writing in 1943 about living through it (Alpha History: https://alphahistory.com/weimarrepublic/stefan-zweig-1923-hyperinflation-1943/):

"I have known days when I had to pay fifty thousand marks for a newspaper in the morning and a hundred thousand in the evening; whoever had foreign currency to exchange did so from hour to hour, because at four o'clock he would get a better rate than at three… On street cars one paid in millions, trucks carried the paper money from the Reichsbank to the other banks, and a fortnight later one found hundred thousand mark notes in the gutter; a beggar had thrown them away contemptuously… For a hundred dollars one could buy rows of six-story houses on the Kurfürstendamm."

The numbers behind it:

  • June 9, 1923: an egg in Berlin cost 800 marks. December 2, 1923: the same egg cost 320 billion marks.
  • By November 1923, one U.S. dollar = 4.2 trillion marks (from 4.2 marks to the dollar in 1914). Workers were paid twice a day and ran to spend it; housewives hauled cash in wheelbarrows — the famous story is of thieves who stole the wheelbarrow and left the money on the ground. Burning banknotes was cheaper than buying firewood.
  • November 15, 1923: the Rentenmark was issued and the printing stopped; the nightmare ended almost overnight.

January 30, 1933 — the day Hitler became Chancellor, as the papers printed it

  • The New York Times, January 31, 1933: "HITLER MADE CHANCELLOR OF GERMANY BUT COALITION CABINET LIMITS POWER; CENTRISTS HOLD BALANCE IN REICHSTAG" — "Hitler Pledges Fight in Cabinet," dateline Berlin, Jan. 30. The cabinet "does not fully represent Hitlerism," Hitler said, and he pledged to keep fighting inside the government.
  • Otago Daily Times, February 1, 1933, page 7 (Papers Past: https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/ODT19330201.2.52): "COALITION IN GERMANY — HERR HITLER CHANCELLOR — TUMULTUOUSLY ACCLAIMED — EXCITEMENT IN BERLIN. BERLIN, January 30. Herr Hitler has been appointed Chancellor, and Herr von Papen Vice-Chancellor… The news of Herr Hitler's Chancellorship spread like wildfire through the city." His spokesman said Hitler "has taken the oath of allegiance to the Republican Constitution, and will rule legally."
  • L'Osservatore Romano, January 30–31, 1933, page one (translated at https://galebachlaw.com/iet1933.html): "Hitler Appointed Chancellor: Has Formed a Presidential Ministry" — Chancellor, Hitler; Vice Chancellor and Reich Commissar for Prussia, von Papen; Interior, Frick; Foreign Affairs, von Neurath; Reich Army, von Blomberg; Commerce and Agriculture, Hugenberg; Labour, Seldte; Aviation, Goering.
  • He was sworn in at about 11 a.m. on January 30, 1933, in Hindenburg's office — Hindenburg had said as late as January 26 that he would never appoint "this Austrian corporal" Chancellor; von Papen spent the last two weeks of January talking him round. One of Hitler's first conditions: dissolve the Reichstag and call new elections.
  • Source: David Cesarani, Final Solution (prologue); Chronicles magazine, "Götterdämmerung, Eight Decades Later" (https://chroniclesmagazine.org/web/gotterdammerung-eight-decades-later/).
