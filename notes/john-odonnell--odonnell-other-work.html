John O'Donnell — His Other Work and Controversies

Researched and saved 2026-11-20, at James's request ("look up his other work"). This file covers everything beyond the October 3, 1945 Patton column, which lives in the General Patton subfolder (general-patton/odonnell-original-column-1945-10-03-quoted-words.md). Items drawn from Matthew Pressman's 2021 article are marked [Pressman] and attributed to him; everything else is from the source named with it. Exact words are quoted wherever a free source gives them.

The short version: the Patton column was not a one-off. O'Donnell's career was a string of fights — a 1941 story the President himself denounced as "a deliberate lie," a libel trial in which five fellow Washington correspondents testified under oath about his private talk on Hitler and the Jews, a presidential Iron Cross handed out as an insult, and a 1943 column about women soldiers that the Army's own official history says did "incalculable damage." He kept the same column, in the same vein, until 1961.

1. The 1941 Navy-escort story — FDR calls it "a deliberate lie"

On April 17, 1941 — eight months before Pearl Harbor — O'Donnell published a story in the Daily News and the Washington Times-Herald claiming the U.S. Navy and Coast Guard were secretly escorting British merchant ships. Its opening, as preserved in the court record of his libel case (below):

"Charges that battle craft of the U. S. Navy and the Coast Guard are now giving armed escort to munition-laden British merchantmen leaving Atlantic ports for the European battlefront exploded in the Capitol last night. Detailed information that President Roosevelt himself is permitting the Coast Guard to accompany the ships of foreign belligerents from the harbors of Baltimore, Philadelphia and New York to a point in the Atlantic where they would be picked up by a U. S. Navy 'neutrality patrol' and thence escorted to a rendezvous determined by the British Admiralty, has already been placed in the hands of Senators."

The same day, President Roosevelt had his secretary tell newsmen the article was "a deliberate lie." (Both the story text and Roosevelt's words are quoted in the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's opinion in O'Donnell v. Philadelphia Record Co., cited below.)

2. The "Naziphile" editorial and the libel trial (1941–1947)

The next day, April 18, 1941, the Philadelphia Record — an interventionist paper owned by J. David Stern — answered with an editorial about O'Donnell. The paragraph printed in heavy type, later held libelous in itself:

"John O'Donnell is a Naziphile. He makes no secret of it. On numerous occasions, to all friends and bar-flies within hearing, he has broadcast his sympathy with most of Hitler's aims—such as destruction of the British Empire, suppression of labor unions and liquidation of Jews."

Other parts of the editorial, as quoted in the court record:

"But when opposition to the Administration resorts to what the President feels necessary to denounce as 'a deliberate lie', then it also becomes right and proper that the public know the kind of man who is writing such news. Mr. O'Donnell feels so intensely on this subject that he may believe himself justified in resorting to any means to block the Administration. At any rate, he is not an unbiased reporter."

"Perhaps they are right, but at times like these it is just as important for newspaper readers to know the character of Washington correspondents as it is to know the ownership of newspapers, which is required by law."

The editorial was written by Stern together with Col. Robert S. Allen, the Record's Washington bureau chief and co-author of the "Washington Merry-Go-Round" column. The Record repeated the charge on its front page on August 8, 1942, after the United States was at war and after O'Donnell had sued:

"On April 18, 1941, we published an editorial stating that John O'Donnell, head of Joe Patterson's Washington Bureau, and Joe's close friend and adviser, was a great admirer of the Nazis, a hater of the British Empire, an advocate of the destruction of trade unions, and quite pleased by the liquidation of Jews by Hitler."

The lawsuit. O'Donnell sued for libel. Two juries found for him — the first verdict was $50,000; the second, $25,000, reduced by the court to $8,000. On March 29, 1947, the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania affirmed the judgment: O'Donnell v. Philadelphia Record Company, 356 Pa. 307, 51 A.2d 775 (1947). The Record then petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court to take the case; the petition survives in full at archive.org (its disposition was not verified in this research).

What the trial put on the record. The Record's defense was that it had reasonable cause to believe the charge, based on what other Washington journalists had heard O'Donnell say in 1940–41 and reported to Stern. Five newspapermen testified. From the court's summary of their sworn testimony (the dissent's review of the record; the majority agreed the jury was entitled to disbelieve it, and the verdict for O'Donnell stood):

  • Thomas L. McNamara (an aide to columnist Drew Pearson) testified he told Allen that O'Donnell, at the Press Club bar, had stated "that in his opinion Hitler was right in his treatment of the Jews in order to build up Germany. O'Donnell was talking in a very loud voice, and as a matter of fact he was striking the table for emphasis occasionally during the time." McNamara also reported overhearing O'Donnell "sounding off about the Nazis and his anti-Semitic views," saying "that the Nazis had to get rid of the Jews in order to build up Germany."
  • Kenneth Crawford, a newspaperman, testified that when he told O'Donnell "the Nazi treatment of the Jews was sufficient to convince me that we had to help the enemies of Hitler," O'Donnell disagreed and explained the Nazi method with a figure of speech: when the Nazis build a road "they build the road straight through, regardless of anything that got in its way. Anything there was wiped out… and that in their treatment of the minorities that the same technique prevailed. If a minority got in its way, it was brushed aside." Crawford said O'Donnell expressed no disagreement with the Nazi treatment of the Jews and that he "was shocked because he did not."
  • Col. Robert S. Allen testified to a conversation at the Bellevue-Stratford Hotel bar in Philadelphia on June 24, 1940, during the Republican National Convention. Allen said to him, "You talk like a Nazi. That is the kind of stuff that Goebbels is putting out." O'Donnell replied: "Well, the Nazi have done a lot of good things in Germany. They cleaned up Germany, wiped out unemployment and they have restored the honor and standing of Germany as a nation in the world." When Allen confronted him with "the atrocities that they perpetrated on the Jews, thousands of old people, women and children who have been killed for no other reason than they were born Jews," O'Donnell answered — by his own later admission under cross-examination, Allen's account was right — "Well, there probably have been some excesses, but certain elements needed cleaning up in Germany."
  • Gardner Jackson (a former Agriculture Department official) testified to a three-to-four-hour Press Club bar conversation in 1940: O'Donnell "had defended the Nazi theories and practices regarding the Jews and minorities… and had defended them on the ground that the Nazis had been obliged to remove the Jews because many of them had become dominant in German affairs, government, business, and the professions, and that in order to enforce and put into practice the Nazi policies both at home and abroad, it had become necessary for the Nazis to eliminate the Jews and to get rid of them."
  • Ulric Bell (a Washington correspondent) testified he had heard O'Donnell say "that he admired the way the Germans were carrying out their program," indicating "his sympathy for the whole program the Germans were carrying out, which included the liquidation of the Jews," and that "O'Donnell expressed himself on this subject quite violently sometimes."

O'Donnell's own testimony. He denied expressing sympathy with the Nazi treatment of the Jews — to Crawford, he said, he had called the treatment "cruel." But under cross-examination he confirmed pieces of the others' accounts: he agreed he had used Crawford's road simile ("I think I did… Yes, he is correct"); he agreed that Allen had said "John, you talk like a Nazi"; and asked whether he had said the Nazis, to carry out their announced program, would have to sweep the Jews aside with the other minorities, he answered: "That is a fact. That is true… Yes [I said that]."

The judge's private view. The trial judge let the verdict stand only because two juries had reached it. In the words quoted by the dissenting chief justice: "The trial judge was impressed by the plaintiff's obvious quibbling, evasions, and his manifest want of sincerity and veracity, and was utterly unable to credit his professed freedom from violent and odious anti-Semitic sentiments and predilections." Chief Justice Maxey's dissent argued the verdict should have been thrown out. The majority affirmed anyway, on the rule that judging witnesses' credibility belongs to the jury.

Full text of the opinion (free): https://static.case.law/pa/356/case-pdfs/0307-01.pdf — the testimony summaries above are from the dissenting opinion's review of the trial record (pages 310–330 of the report). The U.S. Supreme Court certiorari petition, with the April 17, 1941 story, is at archive.org: https://archive.org/download/micro_IA40386416_1263/micro_IA40386416_1263%201.%20Petition%20for%20a%20Writ%20of%20Certiorari.pdf

3. FDR's Iron Cross (December 18, 1942)

At his Friday press conference on December 18, 1942, President Roosevelt publicly humiliated O'Donnell. The Medford Mail Tribune (Medford, Oregon) reported it the same day under the headline "Roosevelt Gives Nazi Iron Cross To Offending Newspaper Writer":

"Washington, Dec. 18 (U.P.) — President Roosevelt today sent a German Iron Cross to a Washington newspaperman who had written a column which displeased him. Mr. Roosevelt after his Friday press conference handed the German military medal to Earl Godwin, a radio commentator who was standing near him, and asked him to present it to John O'Donnell, Washington correspondent of the New York Daily News. O'Donnell had attended the press conference, but had filed out of the room along with most other reporters when this occurred. The President told Godwin he wanted O'Donnell to have the decoration for something he had written about George E. Durno, former White House correspondent for the International News Service who is now a captain in the army. Mr. Roosevelt apparently thought that column by O'Donnell pictured Durno, an old friend of the President, in an unfair light."

(Medford Mail Tribune, Dec. 18, 1942, p. 1, via Historic Oregon Newspapers: https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn97071090/1942-12-18/ed-1/seq-1/ocr/ — the same Medford paper this project already uses for the Patton death reports.)

Ruth Montgomery, a fellow Washington correspondent, remembered it in a 1989 oral history for the Washington Press Club Foundation: Roosevelt "gave to someone else an iron cross that had been taken off of a German soldier, and said to award that to John O'Donnell, which was really a pretty mean thing to do. That was very unpresidential. He had a lot of criticism about it. But he was furious because John O'Donnell had also been such a good friend of theirs until they didn't like what Roosevelt was doing." She also recalled that O'Donnell and the Chicago Tribune's Tex Ervin "used to go up every morning and have a drink at the bar and exchange notes against the Roosevelts." (https://wpcf.org/interview/group1)

Note on the date: Wikipedia's article cites a New York Times item dated December 14, 1942 for this incident; the contemporary wire reports (Medford Mail Tribune, Dec. 18; Daily Iowan, Dec. 19, 1942) place the press conference on Friday, December 18, 1942. The December 18 date is the one supported by the contemporary papers checked.

4. The WAAC column (June 1943) — the other great retraction

In June 1943, with the Women's Army Auxiliary Corps struggling to recruit, O'Donnell's "Capitol Stuff" turned on the women in uniform. His June 7, 1943 column first retailed the gossip it pretended to deplore, writing of efforts by women journalists and congresswomen to dispel "the gaudy stories of the gay and careless way in which the young ladies in uniform … disport themselves." Then, in the column of June 9, 1943, he printed as fact:

"Contraceptives and prophylactic equipment will be furnished to members of the WAACS, according to a super secret agreement reached by the high ranking officers of the War Department and the WAAC chieftain, Mrs. William Pettus Hobby."

There was no such agreement. The War Department and Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson denounced the story ("WAC Gossip Lie, Says Stimson," Daily News, June 11, 1943), and the column was retracted — the first of O'Donnell's two famous forced retractions, two years before the Patton one. The damage was already done: the Army's official history of the Women's Army Corps (Mattie E. Treadwell, The Women's Army Corps, U.S. Army Center of Military History, 1954) records that the morals of the WAACs became a topic of general discussion, that denials were ineffectual, and that three years later "religious publications were still to be found reprinting the story, and actually attributing the columnist's lines to Director Hobby. Director Hobby's picture was labeled 'Astounding Degeneracy.'" The Army's commemorative history (Judith A. Bellafaire, The Women's Army Corps: A Commemoration of World War II Service, CMH, 1972) judged that the column did "incalculable damage" to the Corps and thwarted wartime recruiting.

Historian Ann Pfau adds a sequel (Miss Yourlovin: GIs, Gender, and Domesticity during World War II, Columbia University Press, 2008): "Forced to retract his allegations, O'Donnell and his publisher remained determined to discredit the corps. Soon after this incident, O'Donnell was discovered 'canvassing Army general hospitals.' He sought [to] ascertain the number of WAACs hospitalized for pregnancy and thus defend his reputation with undeniable proof of promiscuity."

(The column quotes and the Stimson headline are as cited in Wikipedia's O'Donnell article from the Daily News of June 9 and June 11, 1943, and the Shreveport Times of June 9, 1943 — the Daily News itself is paywalled on Newspapers.com; Treadwell's official history is free in full text from the U.S. Army Center of Military History.)

5. New details on the Patton column's aftermath (October 1945)

The Patton folder holds the main file; these verified details from the Detroit Jewish News of October 26, 1945 (free at the Bentley Historical Library's digital archive) add names to the advertiser boycott:

  • "Retaliation against the New York Daily News for its failure to apologize for Columnist John O'Donnell's slur against Jews in connection with Gen. Patton's removal, was launched by three advertisers here who cancelled their accounts with the paper. They are Sachs Furniture store, and Ohrback's and May's department stores."
  • "Dr. Stephen S. Wise, president of the American Jewish Congress, called for pressure from readers and advertisers, against the anti-Semitic character of the Daily News."
  • Rep. Helen Gahagan Douglas, speaking on the House floor, declared that O'Donnell's allegation that a Jewish "plot" caused Patton's removal "is an insult to the General as well as to the Jews who served in the U. S. armed forces during the war."
  • The paper also noted: "Ten days after the O'Donnell column appeared, the Daily News made its first editorial comment."

Source: https://digital.bentley.umich.edu/djnews/djn.1945.10.26.001/20

And from [Pressman]: the protest letter from Jewish leaders that the Daily News printed on October 6, 1945 ("Voice of the People—O'Donnell Column Denied," p. 11) called the column "as flagrantly anti-Semitic as anything which has ever appeared in a metropolitan newspaper." Pressman also records that B'nai B'rith passed a resolution condemning the News and joined the Anti-Defamation League in producing flyers mock-titled "Daily Noose," with an open letter warning publisher Patterson not to "become a cheap imitation of Hitler and attempt to foment racial and religious strife." Per Pressman, the October 19 correction acknowledged the slapped soldier was Christian and printed the denials of Niles, Hillman, and Morgenthau, but carried no apology for the cabal insinuation itself.

6. The March 27, 1950 column — O'Donnell and the Lavender Scare [Pressman]

Pressman, following historians Michael Kazin and Stephen J. Whitfield, identifies one March 1950 "Capitol Stuff" column as a driver of the national furor over gay men in government (the "Lavender Scare"). In the column of March 27, 1950 (Daily News, p. 4), O'Donnell wrote that U.S. foreign policy was:

"dominated by an all-powerful, supersecret inner circle of highly educated, socially high-placed sexual misfits in the State Department, all easy to blackmail, all susceptible to blandishments by homosexuals in foreign nations."

Pressman's footnote adds: "This was only the most inflammatory of several columns O'Donnell wrote about gays in the State Department." (Citations: Kazin, The Populist Persuasion, pp. 170–1; Whitfield, The Culture of the Cold War, p. 44; and David K. Johnson, The Lavender Scare, on the press's role.)

7. The August 14, 1953 column — against foreign aid [Pressman]

Pressman quotes O'Donnell's column of August 14, 1953 (Daily News, p. 4), which seized on a general strike in France as proof that foreign aid was wasted: O'Donnell called the strike a "sobering cold shower" for the "bleeding hearts" and "global do-gooders," wrote that France was so "Commie-dominated" it could never be a reliable ally, and concluded that "our hard cash [aid money] and golden oratory have been wasted" and "we've been played for a sucker." (Bracketed words are Pressman's clarifications.) It is the same isolationist line he had taken since before the war.

8. Smaller columns that survive in quotation

  • April 21, 1944 — one of the earliest printings anywhere of the famous wisecrack about American GIs abroad. From "Capitol Stuff," Daily News, Friday, April 21, 1944 (quoted in full by the wordhistories.net phrase study): "American troops haven't worn out their welcome in Australia and New Zealand, but the hospitality of earlier days—when the Jap thrust came dangerously close to the heart of the English-speaking domain down-under—has become a bit threadbare. We asked an Australian the reason the other day and he explained: 'Your troops are over-paid. Furthermore, they're over-decorated and they're over-dressed. They're over-sexed, and damn it, they're over there.'" (https://wordhistories.net/2020/11/06/overpaid-overdressed-oversexed-over-here/)
  • June 27, 1951 — on Truman, Stalin, and games, from "Capitol Stuff," Daily News, p. 4 (quoted by Edward Winter, Chess History, from his long-running "Chess Notes"): "'Remember Truman and his intimates are poker players. Stalin, Malik and the Kremlin crowd are all chess players. A good chess player can always beat a good poker player. As a matter of fact, this country hasn't got a good chess player in public life, with the possible exception of New York's Tom Dewey.'" (https://www.chesshistory.com/winter/extra/einsteinstalin.html)
  • February 15, 1955 — a clipping of "Capitol Stuff" titled "The Answers To The Big Questions," about what the Republican Party must do to preserve the republic and itself, is cataloged in the University of Notre Dame Archives (CRJO 5/10): https://archivesspace.library.nd.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/1753776
  • March 1959 — a "Capitol Stuff" clipping from March 1959 survives in Henry A. Kissinger's papers at Yale (MS 1981, Box 290, Folder 9), fully digitized: https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/11787237 — the clipping is image-only online; its text was not read for this file, so its subject is not stated here. It does show the column still running, and still being clipped and kept, two years before O'Donnell's retirement.

9. Books

No book by John Parsons O'Donnell was found in the catalog searches run. Unlike his ex-wife Doris Fleeson (a syndicated columnist in her own right) or his libel-case opponent Robert S. Allen, O'Donnell left no memoir or book that the sources checked record — his output was the daily column, from the early 1930s to 1961.
