The four colonies, daily life 1891–1900 — extended search by colony name

The search (2026-11-19): an extended search on anything extra about the four Argentine colonies — what daily life was like, 1891–1900. Searched by colony name (Moïseville, Mauricio, Clara, Lucienville), not just by "Baron Hirsch."

Honest limit first: no new American newspaper piece on the colonies by name turned up in this pass — the May 15, 1895 Bill Barlow's Budget piece is still the only U.S. newspaper article on the colonies found so far. What follows is from the Jewish Encyclopedia (1901–1906), JewishGen colony records, and Javier Sinay's book The Murders of Moisés Ville — all named below, so you can see what is a newspaper and what is a later history.

Moïseville (Moisés Ville), Santa Fe — the oldest

  • First colonists arrived on the steamship Weser, dropping anchor in Buenos Aires August 14, 1889 — families from Kamenetz-Podolsk in Podolia, fleeing the pogroms.
  • It stayed the only Jewish colony in Santa Fe province for more than twenty years, until Montefiore was founded in 1912. At its peak, 1920s–1950s, about 6,000 people.
  • The hard side: 22 settlers were murdered between 1889 and 1906 — the subject of Javier Sinay's book The Murders of Moisés Ville (2013 in Spanish; English 2022). His great-grandfather Mijl Hacohen Sinay wrote about the first murders in a 1947 article; the killings started in 1889, when the new arrivals were starving and begging for biscuits.
  • The Sinay family itself arrived December 27, 1894 — a month and a half at sea from Libau — and were taken by train to Palacios station, then walked the 18 km to the colony on December 30, 1894, guided by Michel Cohan, the JCA administrator who ran Moïseville from 1893 to 1902 with what Sinay calls "iron discipline."
  • Mijl Hacohen Sinay became a teacher at the colony's first school; in 1898 he moved to Buenos Aires and founded Der Viderkol, the first Yiddish-language newspaper in Argentina.
  • Sources: Javier Sinay, The Murders of Moisés Ville (Asymptote excerpt; https://www.asymptotejournal.com/nonfiction/the-murders-of-moises-ville-javier-sinay/ and https://sheldonkirshner.com/moises-ville-the-jerusalem-of-south-america/).

Clara, Entre Ríos — the biggest group, 19 villages

  • The steamship Pampa arrived in Buenos Aires in December 1891 carrying Jewish families from the Russian Empire; after a summer at Mar del Sud they were settled in the fields of Villaguay and Uruguay, around the Basavilbaso and Gobernador Domínguez train stations.
  • What life looked like: houses facing each other the way they did in Russian villages; families from Kherson and Bessarabia; farms of wheat and flax; wells thirty meters deep; the Novibuco synagogue-ranch; schools run by teacher Josep Sabah; a cemetery at Colony Number One. The Jewish Encyclopedia even printed a photograph captioned "Clara Colony — Children on Horseback Starting for School."
  • Dr. Noé Yarcho arrived in the early years and faced the typhus epidemic of 1894; he went on to found the first Jewish hospital in South America.
  • August 12, 1900: the colonists formed an agricultural society — one of the first agricultural cooperatives in Argentina — later tied to Miguel Sajaroff's Community Fund.
  • By January 1899 the colony counted 19 villages; Basavilbaso alone had 429 people (71 families). In all of Entre Ríos the JCA owned 381,779 acres, 195,545 of them colonized.
  • Sources: Jewish Encyclopedia, "Agricultural Colonies in the Argentine Republic" (https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/905) and "Entre Rios" (https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5785-entre-rios); "Los Gauchos Judíos de Entre Ríos" documentary history (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iM31VN4lKKc — note: its visual reconstructions are AI-made illustrations, not archival footage; the facts cited are from the records listed in its description).

Lucienville (Basavilbaso), Entre Ríos

  • Founded October 1893; first groups Novabuco and Aquerman; named for the Baron's son Lucien. The hamlets grew up around the Basavilbaso train station.
  • A JewishGen study of five families who settled in Basavilbaso, traced for 115 years, records the first generation's life: their language was Yiddish, their food was kosher, the Jewish festivals and traditions were observed, and weddings were between Jewish people — in five generations, no first-generation marriage involved a non-Jewish person. Children born in the colony mostly married inside the Jewish people too.
  • Source: JewishGen KehilaLinks, "The Descendants of the Colonists in Lucienville" (https://kehilalinks.jewishgen.org/basavilbaso/pdf/Lucienville%20Descendants.pdf).

Mauricio, Buenos Aires province

  • One of the first two tracts the JCA set apart in 1891, named for the Baron himself; about 62,000 acres (25,000 hectares). Less daily-life detail survives for Mauricio in this pass than for the other three — the Jewish Encyclopedia treats Mauricio and Clara together, and Clara is where the 1894 settlers went.
  • The land-buying scale: in August 1891, by Hirsch's direction, some 3,000 square leagues of land were bought across Argentina for $1,300,000 (£260,000).
  • Source: Jewish Encyclopedia, "Agricultural Colonies in the Argentine Republic."

Where the colonies stood when Hirsch died

  • At his death in 1896 there were 6,757 colonists across the Argentine settlements (Wikipedia, "Baron Maurice de Hirsch's Agricultural Settlements in Argentina": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Maurice_de_Hirsch%27s_Agricultural_Settlements_in_Argentina).
