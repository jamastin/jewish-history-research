Bavaria in Hirsch's Time — Banking, the Jewish Community, Wealth, and Unrest

Rundown prepared for James, 2026-11-19. Covers roughly the 1800s up to Baron Maurice de Hirsch's death in 1896, and a little before. Hirsch ("Hershey" in voice) was born in Munich in 1831.

The setting

  • Bavaria was a kingdom in southern Germany, strongly Catholic, mostly farmland and small towns.
  • Munich, the capital, grew fast: about 40,000 people in 1812, around 90,000 by mid-century, 169,478 by 1871.
  • It was not an industrial powerhouse like Berlin or the Ruhr. Wealth sat with the king, the court, the landed aristocracy, and a small circle of private bankers. Most ordinary people were peasants, craftsmen, or small traders.

The banking community — the court Jews

  • There were no big modern banks at first. The Bavarian state financed itself through private "court Jews" (Hoffaktoren / court bankers) attached to the court.
  • Aron Elias Seligmann was appointed Bavarian court banker in 1804 and ennobled in 1814 as Baron von Eichthal, the first Jew in Bavaria so honored.
  • The Hirsch family was the leading example:
  • Jacob von Hirsch (1764/65–1840) — Maurice's grandfather; began as a cattle trader, became banker to King Maximilian I; ennobled in 1818 ("auf Gereuth"), the first Jew permitted to own land in Bavaria.
  • Joseph von Hirsch (1805–1885) — Maurice's father; banker to three kings of Bavaria; involved in early railway construction and the rise of industry in Bavaria; made a hereditary Freiherr (baron) in 1869.
  • The banking house of Hirsch at Promenadeplatz, Munich, existed from 1824 until 1885; Jacob was appointed banker to the royal court on February 1, 1824.
  • So a tiny number of Jewish banking families stood very close to real power and money — and the Hirschs were at the top of that short list.

The Jewish community — the other side of the picture

  • Most Bavarian Jews were not bankers. Population: 29,756 counted in Bavaria east of the Rhine in 1813; 53,402 Jewish souls in the statistics quoted by the Jewish Encyclopedia for the emancipation debates.
  • Most lived in the countryside, especially Franconia, working as cattle dealers, peddlers, and small traders; of the families counted in the 1820s, only 252 lived by agriculture, 169 were artisans, 839 were factory hands — the rest mostly trade.
  • Munich's own community was tiny for most of the century:
  • 1781: 53 Jews; 1790: 127; 1814/1818: ~451–479
  • 1825: 607; 1848: 842; 1860: 1,200; 1867: 2,067; 1880: 4,144; 1900: 8,739; 1910: 11,083 (about 20% of all Bavarian Jews)
  • The Munich congregation was officially established in 1815; its first synagogue, on Westenriederstrasse, opened April 21, 1826 (dedicated 1827), with King Ludwig I and his wife attending the opening. A much larger synagogue (Herzog-Max-Strasse, ~1,800 seats, third-largest in Germany) replaced it in 1887.

The law — half-citizens

  • The Jewish Edict of June 10, 1813 made Jews full citizens as to duties, but only half-citizens as to rights.
  • The worst piece was the Matrikelparagraph (matriculation clause): a registration cap on how many Jewish families could live in each place. Whoever had no Matrikel slot could not legally found a family — in practice, could not marry and settle.
  • That rule, plus residence and occupation restrictions, pushed large numbers of young Bavarian Jews to emigrate to the United States — one wave early in the century, a second after 1848.
  • Timeline to equality: Matrikel clause abolished 1861; remaining immigration restrictions lifted 1868; full legal equality only in 1871, when Bavaria joined the German Empire and imperial law took over. Maurice de Hirsch was already 40.

Protests and unrest in the period

  • 1819 — the Hep-Hep riots. Beginning in Würzburg (Franconia), anti-Jewish riots spread across Bavaria and the German Confederation: looting and attacks on Jews and their businesses, with authorities slow to step in. Further unrest in 1822.
  • 1819–1822 — the petition fight. When the first Bavarian Landtag (parliament) met in 1819, the larger Jewish congregations sent delegates to Munich (led by Rabbi Samson Wolff Rosenfeld) to petition for complete enfranchisement. The Diet at first promised a revision; influenced by the Hep-Hep agitation, it declared on May 13, 1822 that "the time for the emancipation of the Jews had not yet arrived."
  • 1831 — after the July Revolution in France, the Bavarian Diet unanimously called for unrestricted emancipation, but the Abel ministry let the matter die in the reaction that followed.
  • 1844 — the Munich Beer Riots, over the price of beer.
  • 1848 — revolution. Students and the middle classes protested; on March 4 a large crowd assaulted the Munich Armory and moved on the Residenz. The scandal over King Ludwig I's mistress Lola Montez (made Countess of Landsfeld, given Bavarian citizenship in February 1847) detonated his rule: after renewed unrest when she returned, Ludwig abdicated on March 20, 1848 in favor of his son Maximilian II. The 1848 revolution was also accompanied by anti-Jewish riots, notably in rural Franconia.
  • Later in Hirsch's lifetime, the 1873 stock-market crash (Gründerkrach) fed a new, organized antisemitic agitation across Germany in the late 1870s–1880s — the climate in which Hirsch turned his fortune toward getting Jews out of Russia.

The point of it

Hirsch was born into one of the very few Jewish families in Bavaria with wealth, a title, and the king's ear — while most Jews around him were capped in number by law, barred from settling or marrying freely, and periodically attacked in the streets. That contrast is the world he came out of.
