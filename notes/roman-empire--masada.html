Masada — the last holdout

Researched 2026-11-19 for James's search list (item 6: "Masada"). Sources: Josephus (Jewish War 2 and 7.252–406) — the only literary source; Yigael Yadin's excavation reports (1963–65) and the scholarly debate since (e.g., Shaye J. D. Cohen; Nachman Ben-Yehuda, The Masada Myth).

The rock and Herod's palace

  • Masada is a flat-topped rock mesa in the Judean desert, rising some 400 m above the Dead Sea shore — sheer cliffs on every side, one narrow winding approach Josephus calls "the Snake."
  • Josephus says a Hasmonean high priest (usually identified as Alexander Jannaeus) first fortified it. Herod the Great made it what it became (c. 37–31 BC): a fortress-palace built as his refuge of last resort — casemate walls around the summit, a palace on the northern cliff in three terraces, a second palace, storehouses for grain, oil and weapons, and plastered cisterns holding floodwater enough, Josephus says, to outlast any siege. Herod stocked it against two fears: a Jewish revolt, and Cleopatra persuading Antony to hand her his kingdom.

Who held it in the war

  • At the outbreak of revolt in AD 66, a band of Sicarii under Menahem son of Judas seized Masada from its Roman garrison by treachery and slaughtered it. Menahem was soon killed in Jerusalem's infighting, and his nephew Eleazar ben Ya'ir took command of the survivors on the rock.
  • From Masada the Sicarii raided the countryside — Josephus accuses them of atrocities against fellow Jews, including the massacre of some 700 women and children at Ein Gedi on Passover. They took no part in Jerusalem's defence; when the city fell in 70, Masada, Herodium and Machaerus were all that remained. The others fell by 72. Masada stood alone.

The Roman siege (AD 72/73 or 73/74)

  • Rome sent the governor Lucius Flavius Silva with the Tenth Legion (Legio X Fretensis), auxiliaries and thousands of prisoners as labour — Josephus says about 8,000–10,000 men in all (his figure; treat as approximate).
  • Silva's work is still on the ground today, the most complete Roman siege system surviving anywhere:
  • a wall of circumvallation ringing the entire rock, with towers, so no one could slip away;
  • eight siege camps, their stone outlines still clearly visible from the summit and from the air;
  • and on the western side, the great assault ramp — an enormous bank of earth and timber built up against the cliff on a natural rock spur, up which a siege tower and battering ram were hauled to the wall. The ramp is still there, the single most famous siege work of the ancient world.
  • The defenders countered the ram with an inner wall of timber and earth that absorbed the blows — until the Romans set it on fire, and a change of wind blew the flames back toward the defenders.
  • Dating note, honestly flagged: Josephus's chronology is usually read as spring AD 73, but a strong scholarly case puts the fall in 74. The debate turns on Silva's career dates; write it 73/74.

The last night — as Josephus tells it

  • That evening, Josephus says, Eleazar ben Ya'ir gathered his people and made two great speeches: slavery under Rome would be worse than death; the soul is immortal and death sets it free; God himself had decreed the nation's defeat. (Speeches in ancient historians are the author's compositions — these are Josephus's words for Eleazar, not a transcript.)
  • The men killed their own wives and children. Then they cast lots and chose ten men to kill all the rest. The ten then drew lots again: one killed the other nine, checked that all were dead, set the palace on fire, and fell on his own sword.
  • Josephus's total: 960 dead, men, women and children.
  • Two women and five children survived — an older woman, a relative of Eleazar "superior in sagacity and training," and five children — hidden in the underground water passages. They came out to the Romans in the morning and told the story. Everything we "know" of that night rests, by Josephus's own framing, on their testimony carried through Roman reports to him.

What the archaeology actually found

  • Yigael Yadin's excavations (1963–65), with thousands of volunteers, uncovered Herod's palaces with frescoes and mosaics, storerooms still holding burnt food remains, a synagogue (among the oldest known), ritual baths, coins of the revolt, scroll fragments (including Deuteronomy and Ezekiel), and over 700 inscribed potsherds (ostraca).
  • The "lots": near the storerooms Yadin found eleven ostraca, each bearing a single name — one of them "ben Ya'ir." Yadin identified these as the very lots of Josephus's story, the names of the final ten and their commander. It is a striking fit — but flag it honestly: ostraca with names were everyday administrative tags at Masada, and other scholars read these as ration or duty labels. The identification is Yadin's interpretation, not a certainty.
  • The bodies: excavators found the remains of only about 28 people (in the northern palace and a cave below the southern cliff) — nowhere near 960. Yadin held state funerals for them as the defenders; later forensic work has questioned even that (among the cave bones were pig bones, suggesting Roman burials). Where 960 bodies went is unexplained — the Romans may have cleared and buried them, but no mass grave has been found.
  • The fires, the ramp, the camps, the wall: all real, exactly where Josephus put them. The siege is archaeology. The last night is literature resting on two women's reported words.

The honest bottom line

  • Josephus is the only source for the mass suicide, and modern scholars are divided: some accept a core event (a group suicide rather than capture is plausible, and the survivors' story had to come from somewhere); others (notably Shaye Cohen) argue the story is heavily shaped — Josephus had told a strikingly similar lot-and-suicide story about himself at Jotapata, where he alone conveniently survived. Nachman Ben-Yehuda documented how the Masada story was built up in the 20th century into a national myth ("Masada shall not fall again").
  • What is not in doubt: Sicarii held Herod's fortress for years after Jerusalem fell; Rome spent a legion, eight camps and a mountain of earth to take it; and when the Romans broke in, the fortress was burned and its defenders did not come out alive in any number. How exactly they died, Josephus alone tells us — and he was not there.
