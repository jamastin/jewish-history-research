Berlin before Hitler — first overview (started 2026-11-19)

The search: "what was going on in Berlin before Hitler — all the way up till Hitler."

The arc, 1918–1933

  • November 9, 1918: Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicates as WWI ends in defeat; the republic (later called the Weimar Republic) is proclaimed. Sailors' mutiny at Kiel and revolution in the streets of Berlin.
  • Berlin the city: after the Greater Berlin Act of 1920 it held about 4 million people — one of the largest, loudest cities in the world, center of Germany's press, film, theater, and science.
  • Jewish Berlin: about 173,000 Jews in 1925 — roughly a third of all Jews in Germany, about 4% of the city. Sixteen synagogues (the New Synagogue on Oranienburger Strasse, 1866, the famous one), Jewish newspapers and periodicals (the communal paper alone circulated 60,000 copies), B'nai B'rith and the national German-Jewish organizations headquartered there. Jews prominent in publishing, banking, department stores, medicine, law, theater, and film. University faculties opened fully to Jewish scholars in 1918; Einstein was in Berlin and won the Nobel Prize in 1921. Of nine German Nobel winners in the Weimar years, five were Jewish scientists.
  • 1923: the hyperinflation. The mark collapsed; savings wiped out; the famous wheelbarrow-of-money year. It poisoned trust in the republic and in saving itself — a generation decided to spend and enjoy instead.
  • The mid-1920s "Golden Twenties": cabarets, nightclubs, revue theaters, film (Fritz Lang's Dr. Mabuse, 1922, caught the mood: slums, stock exchange, cabarets — "everywhere chaos reigns"). Berlin's reputation as the freest, wildest city in Europe — the world Christopher Isherwood later wrote up in Goodbye to Berlin (the basis of Cabaret).
  • The other side, all along: political street violence — Nazis and Communists fighting, assassinations early on (Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau, himself Jewish, murdered in Berlin in 1922), and Berlin was also a center of antisemitic agitation precisely because Jewish life there was so visible and successful.
  • 1929: the crash. American loans stopped, mass unemployment (6 million jobless in Germany by 1932), and the Nazi vote climbed: 2.6% in 1928 → 18% in 1930 → largest party by July 1932.
  • January 30, 1933: Hitler becomes Chancellor. The Berlin that had been the golden age of German Jewry — about 160,000–172,000 Jews at that moment — began to be dismantled.
