Rome and Judea — where this folder starts

Started 2026-11-19 at James's request: Rome just before it entered Israel/Palestine, the emperors around Jesus's time, and the story he read about an emperor and a Jewish woman. Sources: Josephus; Suetonius, Life of Titus; Cassius Dio; standard chronology of Roman Judea.

How Rome got into Judea — before there were emperors

  • 63 BC — Pompey takes Jerusalem. Rome was still a republic; there was no emperor yet. Judea was tearing itself apart in a civil war between two Hasmonean brothers, Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II. Pompey intervened, besieged Jerusalem for three months, stormed the Temple Mount, and walked into the Holy of Holies — the room only the High Priest could enter, once a year. He left the Temple treasury untouched and ordered the sanctuary purified, but Judea's independence was over: Hyrcanus kept the high priesthood, lost the kingship, and Judea paid tribute to Rome as a client state.
  • 40–37 BC — Herod the Great. After the Parthians invaded and installed a Hasmonean rival, Herod fled to Rome and talked the Senate into naming him "King of the Jews." He fought his way back with Roman legions and took Jerusalem in 37 BC. For 33 years he was Rome's model client king: absolute loyalty, heavy taxes, massive building — including rebuilding the Temple into one of the wonders of the ancient world.
  • 27 BC — the first emperor. Octavian, Julius Caesar's adopted heir, took the name Augustus. The republic became the empire. Herod switched his loyalty from the losing Antony to Augustus smoothly and kept his throne.

The emperors of Jesus's lifetime

  1. Augustus (27 BC – AD 14) — first emperor. Jesus was born under him, late in Herod's reign (c. 4 BC, the year Herod died). Luke's Gospel ties the birth to a census; the census of Quirinius that historians can date belongs to AD 6 (see below), a known timeline tension in the sources.
  2. On Herod's death (4 BC) his kingdom was split three ways: Archelaus in Judea, Herod Antipas in Galilee (he rules all through Jesus's life — the Herod of the Gospels' ministry years), and Philip in the north.
  3. AD 6 — Judea becomes a Roman province. Archelaus ruled so badly that his own subjects petitioned Rome to remove him. Augustus deposed him, and Judea passed to direct Roman rule under prefects answerable to the governor of Syria; the capital moved from Jerusalem to Caesarea on the coast. The census of Quirinius was taken at this annexation — the tax registration that made "Judea is now Rome's" real on the ground.
  4. Tiberius (AD 14–37) — the second emperor. Jesus's ministry and crucifixion fall in his reign. His prefect in Judea, Pontius Pilate (AD 26–36), is the Roman who condemned Jesus. The coin in the "render unto Caesar" story is Tiberius's denarius, with Tiberius's face on it. Tiberius himself had no Jewish wife — his marriages were Vipsania and then Augustus's daughter Julia. In AD 19 he expelled the Jews from the city of Rome itself.
  5. After Tiberius: Caligula (37–41), whose order to install his statue in the Jerusalem Temple nearly started a war (he was assassinated before it could be carried out); Claudius (41–54), who briefly made Herod's grandson Agrippa I king again (41–44); Nero (54–68), under whom the great revolt broke out in AD 66; then Vespasian (69–79), the general sent to crush the revolt, and his son Titus, who finished it — Jerusalem and the Temple destroyed in AD 70.

The story James read: an emperor and a Jewish woman — Titus and Berenice

The story matches Titus and Queen Berenice — with two corrections to the way it is usually retold:

  • Titus was the second emperor of the Flavian house (after his father Vespasian), not the second emperor overall. He reigned AD 79–81.
  • Berenice (b. AD 28) was a Jewish queen: daughter of Agrippa I, great-granddaughter of Herod the Great, sister of Agrippa II. She appears in the New Testament — she sits beside her brother in Acts 25–26 when Paul makes his defence before Festus at Caesarea. Twice widowed young (her second husband was her uncle, Herod of Chalcis, by whom she had two sons), she later married Polemon, king of Cilicia, who accepted circumcision to marry her — and she left that marriage too.
  • During the Jewish war she backed the Flavians, and she and Titus fell in love. In AD 75 she came to Rome and lived in the imperial palace "to all appearance as his wife" (Cassius Dio). Suetonius records that Titus had promised to marry her. No Roman emperor had ever married a foreign — let alone Jewish — queen, and Rome remembered Cleopatra: the opposition was fierce.
  • When Vespasian died in AD 79 and Titus became emperor, he sent her away at once. Suetonius's exact phrase: invitus invitam — "he, unwilling, dismissed her, unwilling." Dio says she later returned to Rome, but Titus did not take her back. He died in AD 81; Berenice disappears from the record.
  • The "adopting her children" part: no source says Titus adopted Berenice's sons (Berenicianus and Hyrcanus, by Herod of Chalcis). That detail appears to be a garble added in retellings. What the sources do support: the promised marriage, the palace life as his wife in all but name, and the forced parting.
  • The other real "Jewish woman at the top of Rome" of the era: Poppaea Sabina, Nero's wife. Josephus calls her "god-fearing" (theosebēs) and credits her with interceding to free Jewish priests he had come to Rome to plead for; some later traditions go further and call her a proselyte. That may be the other version of the story in circulation.
