The Black Death Accusations Against the Jews, 1348–1351

Folder created at James's request, 2026-11-19. Subject: the accusation that Jews caused the Black Death by poisoning wells, and the massacres that followed. Everything below is from the sources listed at the bottom; exact words from the period are quoted where they survive.

The accusation

  • The Black Death (bubonic, septicemic, and pneumonic plague, caused by the bacillus Yersinia pestis / Pasteurella pestis) raged through Europe 1348–1350 (1346–1353 in the widest dating) and killed perhaps a third to a half of Europe's population.
  • Toward the end of 1348 and in early 1349, Jews across Europe were accused of causing the deaths by poisoning the wells and other water sources. Heinrich Graetz's summary of the charge: "the suspicion arose that the Jews had poisoned the brooks and wells, and even the air, in order to annihilate the Christians of every country at one blow."

Where the "proof" came from — the Chillon confessions

  • The first time Jews were tortured into confessing complicity in spreading the plague was September 1348, at the Castle of Chillon on Lake Geneva (Savoy).
  • Balavignus (Balavigny), a Jewish surgeon/physician, was racked first. Under torture he "confessed" that Rabbi Jacob of Toledo had sent him, by the hand of a Jewish boy, a leather pouch of poison — in one version "a preparation of poison or venom in a narrow, stitched leathern bag," hidden in an eggshell — with orders, on pain of excommunication, to throw it into the larger wells of Thonon, after warning his own friends and relations not to drink the water.
  • The Jewish Encyclopedia's version of the alleged plot: the poison was said to have been compounded by Jacob à Paskate of Toledo, Peyret of Chambéry, and one Aboget, from "Christians' hearts, spiders, frogs, lizards, human flesh, and sacred hosts," ground into a powder and distributed to be put in the wells Christians drew from.
  • Balavignus also declared under torture that no Jew could exculpate himself — that the plot had been communicated to all, and all were guilty. Ten similar confessions were racked out of others (including Agimet of Geneva, confessed at Châtel, October 20, 1348).
  • The resulting dossier was copied and sent from town to town — the confessions crossed Europe faster than the plague did. City mayors forwarded "official reports" of them to other cities.

The massacres

In total, more than 200 Jewish communities were annihilated between 1348 and 1351 (some counts say ~300 communities affected, across Spain, France, and Germany, spreading to Poland-Lithuania).

  • April 1348, Toulon — the first plague-related massacre: the Jewish quarter sacked, 40 Jews murdered in their homes. Massacres in Provence followed in May; Jews of a Provençal town were burned on the well-poisoning charge.
  • June–July 1348, Spain — first outbreaks in Barcelona, Cervera, and Tárrega.
  • September 21, 1348, Zurich — the new charge combined with the old blood accusation; several Jews burned, the rest expelled.
  • January 1349 (Jan 9 or Jan 16 in different accounts), Basel — the community locked into a wooden building on an island in the Rhine and burned (numbers given range from ~300 to 600). Basel's citizens had marched on the city hall and forced the council to swear it would burn the Jews and allow no Jew to enter the city for the next two hundred years (Königshofen's chronicle).
  • February 14, 1349, Strasbourg — the "Valentine's Day massacre." The plague had not yet reached Strasbourg (it arrived months later). About 2,000 Jews were burned on a wooden platform in their own cemetery; those who accepted baptism were spared, and Jewish children were baptized and distributed to Christian families. Days before, Strasbourg's old council — which had tried to protect the Jews — was overthrown in a coup led by the butchers' and tanners' guilds backed by nobles who owed the Jews money.
  • March 1, 1349, Worms — pogrom; the Judengasse and synagogue quarter set ablaze.
  • Spring 1349, Frankfurt am Main — the community annihilated; Jews set fire to their own homes and street rather than be taken.
  • August 24, 1349, Mainz — the largest community, ~3,000–6,000 people. Mainz's Jews fought back and at first held off the attackers, then were overwhelmed; according to some sources 6,000 perished, many by setting fire to their own homes and the Jewish street. The same day in Cologne, Jews set themselves on fire to avoid forced baptism.
  • Erfurt — about 3,000 killed (one account says in a single day).
  • Munich and Bavaria — the plague reached Bavaria by June 1348 (over the Tyrolean Alps). The Jewish Encyclopedia records that at the time of the Black Death the Jewish community of Munich "ceased to exist" — annihilated in the general slaughter; survivors who escaped later formed a new community, confirmed in its rights by Duke Stephen in 1363.

The defense — from the top of the Church, and from Bavaria

Pope Clement VI (at Avignon) defended the Jews in two bulls of 1348: - July 4/6, 1348 — renewed the old bull Sicut Judaeis, forbidding violence against Jews. - September 26, 1348 — Quamvis perfidiam — denounced the allegation directly, saying that "certain Christians, seduced by that liar, the devil, are imputing the pestilence to poisoning by Jews." His arguments: Jews were dying of the plague in the same proportions as Christians; the plague struck places with no Jews at all; therefore the well-poisoning charge was not credible. He forbade killing, robbing, or forcibly baptizing Jews, and threatened persecutors with excommunication. - The defense worked where the Pope's writ ran: at Avignon the Jews suffered no harm. Outside, it largely failed.

Konrad of Megenberg — a Bavarian cleric and natural philosopher, canon of Regensburg cathedral — wrote a Latin treatise during the 1349 plague (Concerning Mortality and Earthquake in the City of Vienna) arguing on medical and theological grounds that the well-poisoning accusations were factually false. It circulated in fewer than two dozen manuscript copies and had no documented effect on the massacres — but it is evidence the falsity of the charge was known and stated in writing at the time, in Bavaria itself.

Some city patricians also tried to protect their Jews: Strasbourg's original council defended them at the Benfeld meeting of Alsatian towns and was answered: "If you are not afraid of poisoning, why have you yourselves covered and guarded your wells?" Cologne's council wrote to Strasbourg on January 12, 1349 urging restraint. Basel's patricians likewise tried and failed.

The money side — in a contemporary's own words

The Strasbourg chronicler Jacob von Königshofen (1346–1420), writing about the February 14, 1349 burning, stated the motive plainly:

"And everything that was owed to the Jews was cancelled, and the Jews had to surrender all pledges and notes that they had taken for debts. The council, however, took the direct cash that the Jews possessed and divided it among the working men proportionately. The money was indeed the thing that killed the Jews. If they had been poor and if the feudal lords had not been in debt to them, they would not have been burnt."

Other money facts from the record: - At the Benfeld council (January/February 1349), the bishop of Strasbourg, the Alsatian lords, and city deputies withdrew protection from the Jews; historians note the lords realized that eliminating their creditors eliminated their debts to Jewish moneylenders. - Emperor Charles IV decreed that the property of Jews murdered for supposedly spreading the plague could be seized by their Christian neighbors with impunity — a financial incentive that intensified the attacks — and afterward officially pardoned Strasbourg's citizens for killing their Jews and taking their money. - The flagellants — wandering penitential processions — also took part in shedding Jewish blood; Clement VI's bull condemning them complained that most of them, "beneath an appearance of piety, set their hands to cruel and impious work, shedding the blood of Jews, whom Christian piety accepts and sustains."

Aftermath

  • European Jewry lost an estimated 10–30% of its pre-1349 population in the massacre regions.
  • The massacres permanently redrew the map: surviving communities migrated east into Poland and Lithuania, where King Casimir III offered protection and charters — the beginning of the great eastward shift of European Jewish life.
  • In May 1349 the city fathers of Brandenburg even passed a law condemning Jews of well-poisoning in advance (a priori).

Open threads for later searching

  • The well-poisoning charge did not start with the plague: it was documented as early as 1321 in France (then aimed at lepers, with Jews accused alongside) — worth its own file if James wants the run-up.
  • Bavaria-specific plague records: the plague was in Bavaria by June 1348; a Chronicling America / German-source pass on Bavarian towns (Regensburg, Nuremberg, Passau) in 1348–49 has not been done.
  • The Memorbuch (memorial book) tradition: Jewish communities recorded the names of the massacred — the Nuremberg Memorbuch already appears in the Munich 1285 story in the laws timeline.
