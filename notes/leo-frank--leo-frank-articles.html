Article 1 — Trial / Verdict, August 1913

The Washington Herald (Washington, D.C.), Tuesday, August 26, 1913, page 1. Headline: "FRANK GUILTY OF GIRL'S MURDER." Subheads: "Jury's Verdict Cheered by Big Crowd Around Atlanta Courthouse." / "LAWYERS CLAIM MISTRIAL" / "Will Be Given Death Penalty Today for Killing Fourteen-Year-Old Mary Phagan." URL: https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045433/1913-08-26/ed-1/?sp=1&q=Leo+Frank

Atlanta, Aug. 25.—Leo M. Frank this afternoon was found guilty of the murder of fourteen-year-old Mary Phagan on April 26. The case was given to the jury at 11:17 and the verdict was announced at 10:[?] o'clock. Two ballots were cast before an agreement was reached. The first ballot showed eleven for a verdict of guilty without the recommendation of mercy and one in doubt. After one more ballot, an hour later, the twelfth man came over to the majority and made the early verdict possible. Frank will be condemned to death when he is brought before Judge Roan Tuesday. Frank was not in court when the verdict was announced. After the jury retired he was taken to jail by request of his lawyers. The prisoner was accompanied to the jail by his wife and aged mother, Mrs. [first name unclear in OCR] Frank, of Brooklyn, N.Y., who came here to attend her son's trial. Solicitor Dorsey wept as he polled the jury. He asked each member solemnly, 'Is this your verdict' and one by one [they] replied, 'It is.' Five thousand people awaited the announcement of the verdict in the streets around the courthouse and the demonstration following the news of the verdict drew double that number to the scene.

— Crowd Cheers Verdict. — As the verdict was announced, a great cheer arose and it was heard by Frank, his wife and mother in the jail, two blocks away. When Solicitor Dorsey passed from the courthouse door he was picked up bodily by members of the waiting crowd and on their shoulders carried to his office. Frank's lawyers will move for a new trial. Just after Solicitor Dorsey concluded his impassioned address against Frank, Attorneys Arnold and Rosser, representing the defendant, asked Judge Roan to declare a mistrial on the ground that Frank had not been fairly tried. They alleged the behavior of the spectators and the crowds about the courthouse had been disgraceful and had tended to coerce and intimidate the court and jury. The repeated applause for Solicitor Dorsey was cited as an act of intimidation. 'In fact,' said Attorney Arnold, 'the mob has run this court.' Judge Roan declined to declare a mistrial and immediately charged the jury. The Judge's charge was brief and was declared to be impartial by both State and the defense. Frank was put on trial July 28, twenty-nine days ago. The evidence in the case aggregated nearly [200,000? — figure garbled in OCR] words, and the attorneys consumed nearly five days in argument, Solicitor Dorsey speaking more than ten hours. Leo Frank is twenty-eight years old and his parents reside in Brooklyn, N.Y. He was educated at Pratt Institute, Brooklyn, and at Cornell. He came to Atlanta six years ago and took charge of the National Pencil Factory. He married here and has been prominent in Hebrew circles, having been president of the local lodge B'nai B'rith.

[The verdict time reads "10:!" in the OCR and could not be resolved from the page image; headline and subheads confirmed against the page image.]

Article 2 — Governor Slaton's Commutation, June 1915

New-York Tribune (New York, N.Y.), Tuesday, June 22, 1915, pages 1–2. Front-page headline: "Frank Spared from Gallows; Mobs, in Rage at His Escape, Menace Governor for Mercy." Deck: "STAKED HIS CAREER TO SAVE FRANK. — 'I would be a murderer if I allowed this man to hang,' the Governor said. 'It means that I must live in obscurity for the rest of my days.' And mobs throughout the state last night clamored for his blood." Photo caption quote from Slaton: 'I WOULD RATHER BE PLOTTING IN OBSCURITY WITH AN EASY CONSCIENCE THAN TO BE THE GOVERNOR OF GEORGIA WITH BLOOD ON MY HANDS AND FAME IN THE EYES OF THE WORLD. IF THEY WANT TO KILL ME, LET THEM; IT IS BETTER FOR ME TO DIE THAN TO KILL A MAN WHO I BELIEVE IS INNOCENT.' URLs: page 1 https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1915-06-22/ed-1/?sp=1&st=text ; page 2 https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1915-06-22/ed-1/?sp=2&q=Leo+Frank

The page 1 main dispatch is partly garbled in OCR; the readable core:

Atlanta, June 21.—Governor John M. Slaton, to spare Leo M. Frank from the gallows, staked his career and his life today when, after the most dramatic trial of his administration, he commuted his death sentence to imprisonment for life. In the face of threats of the infuriated mobs who twice before yesterday besieged the capitol, the Governor, surrounded by state militia, read his decision in the Senate chamber. [...] Governor Slaton was guarded throughout the afternoon and night by state militiamen and policemen, and the authorities tonight are getting ready [to repel an attack on] the state capitol, the Governor's home and the state prison farm at Milledgeville, where Frank has been since his conviction. [...] Several squads of militia are serving as guard and have [orders to] shoot if an attack is made.

Slaton, per the Tribune's summary of his statement: "I have given this matter my earnest attention, and after a thorough investigation believe that the death penalty should not be inflicted on Leo Frank."

Adolph Lewisohn: "I am profoundly convinced that Governor Slaton acted justly and humanely in commuting the sentence of Leo M. Frank. The Governor is a brave man and I trust that history will vindicate him." Louis Marshall (Frank's counsel): "I congratulate the Governor on his courage. His action will be approved by fair minded men."

Marietta section: effigies of Slaton burned in the streets, labelled "John M. Slaton, the King of the Jews and Traitor to Georgia"; a crowd of about 300 paraded calling "Down with Slaton! Down with Slaton!"

Page 2 highlights: - Frank's sister, Miss Helen Frank: "I am delighted with the news. My brother will soon be free. We shall fight on until he is cleared." - Frank, at the Milledgeville prison farm: "I shall be vindicated, and I would rather be in prison a thousand [years?] than to die on account of a crime of which I am innocent. The Governor has done his duty and I am grateful to him." - Frank's wife: "I have so much faith in my husband's innocence that I feel absolutely confident he will ultimately be set free." - The Governor's 29-page review of the case disputed Jim Conley's testimony — Conley, the state's star witness, "lied often," per the Governor's review.

Article 3 — The Lynching, August 1915

The Hattiesburg News (Hattiesburg, Miss.), Thursday, August 19, 1915, pages 1 and 4. Headline: "HERE'S THE STORY OF LEO FRANK'S LYNCHING." URLs: page 1 https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn87065167/1915-08-19/ed-1/?sp=1&q=Leo+Frank ; page 4 https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn87065167/1915-08-19/ed-1/?sp=4&st=text

A first-person account from an unnamed "responsible Atlanta business man" describing how the lynching was organized. Key passages (full text was transcribed in the research report; this file keeps the essential account):

"The men who hanged Leo Frank, the murderer of Mary Phagan, did not go about it with a spirit of lawlessness nor vindictiveness. They felt it a duty—a duty to their state and commonwealth, a duty to the memory of Mary Phagan, whom all Cobb county loved..."

The account says: the lynching had been planned since Slaton's commutation; an earlier attempt leaked and Governor Harris put the militia on alert, buying Frank "at least one month of grace." Twenty-five men were chosen, led by "a man who bears as reputable a name as you would ever hear in a lawful community." Advance men mapped the roads to Milledgeville, studied the prison's barbed wire and its telegraph/telephone connections, and on the night cut the wires so no alarm could go out. The men were picked up quietly by automobile after midnight; none of their households knew. They drove at speed via Roswell to Marietta. The original plan was to hang Frank at Mary Phagan's cemetery, but daybreak overtook them, so it was done in the grove near her birthplace where she had played as a girl. Asked for Frank's last words, the second source answered: "Nobody will ever know that outside of the men who were actually present... Not even that."

Article 4 — Lynching Aftermath / Comment, August 1915

The Bolivar County Democrat (Rosedale, Miss.), Saturday, August 21, 1915, page 1. Headline: "LEO FRANK LYNCHED." URL: https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn87065645/1915-08-21/ed-1/?sp=1&q=Frank+lynched

LEO FRANK LYNCHED. The lynching of Leo Frank, convicted of the murder of Mary Phagan in Atlanta, sentenced to be hanged and finally sent to the penitentiary for life [was taken?] by a mob on Monday night, but emphasizes the intensity of the feeling in Georgia against Frank, and also furnishes evidence to show that the court and jury, in the trial and conviction of Frank may have been influenced by the force of public sentiment. A considerable element of Georgians evidently believed Frank guilty and they determined that he should die. When Governor Slaton commuted the death sentence to imprisonment for life, it was necessary to protect the governor from threatened mob violence with state militia. Governor Slaton, now in San Francisco, telegraphed his opinion of the lynching, expressing his condemnation of the murder of Frank by the mob in the strongest words. His expressions may make his return to Georgia unsafe. While public opinion is often erratic and unstable, when thoroughly aroused and in action is an avalanche that sweeps everything before it.

[One clause in the opening sentence is garbled in the OCR; the sense is that Frank, whose sentence had been commuted to life imprisonment, was taken by a mob on Monday night.]

Frank's lawyers (added 2026-11-06, from web sources incl. leofrank.com / Atlanta Constitution transcriptions)

  • Trial counsel (1913): Luther Z. Rosser and Reuben R. Arnold — two of Georgia's most respected trial lawyers. They cross-examined Jim Conley for three days without shaking him on anything substantive. It was Arnold who told Judge Roan, "the mob has run this court," when asking for a mistrial. Rosser, at the new-trial hearing: "I shall stand in his defense as long as I live and as long as I have a breath of life."
  • Rosser and Arnold let Frank be absent from the courtroom when the verdict was read (for his safety, with the crowds). The later defense team built a federal appeal on that waiver, arguing the trial lawyers had no right to waive his presence — the issue that reached the U.S. Supreme Court (Frank v. Mangum, 1915).
  • Appeals team: brothers Herbert and Leonard Haas, Harry A. Alexander, and the firm Tye, Peeples & Jordan (Henry Peeples argued the habeas corpus petition). Rosser and Arnold stayed on through the appeals.
  • Louis Marshall of New York — president of the American Jewish Committee, one of America's leading constitutional lawyers — assisted Frank's counsel when the case went to Washington. Chicago advertising tycoon Albert Lasker helped fund the defense and the publicity campaign.
  • Note: Governor Slaton had earlier been a law partner in Rosser's firm; critics attacked the commutation on that connection.
