Gessius Florus — the governor who robbed the Temple

Researched 2026-11-19 — search list item 1: "Who was the Roman governor that robbed the Temple?". Sources: Josephus (Jewish War 2, Antiquities 20.11), Tacitus (Histories 5.10), Smith's Dictionary of Greek and Roman Biography.

Who he was

  • Gessius Florus, from Clazomenae, a Greek city on the coast of Asia Minor (modern Turkey).
  • Procurator (governor) of Judea AD 64–66, succeeding Lucceius Albinus. He was the last of the line of governors whose misrule led to the war.
  • Josephus's verdict is total: he says Florus treated Judea as if he had been sent "to be the executioner of condemned persons," and that he deliberately fanned the revolt into flame — in Josephus's telling, to bury his own crimes under a war. Tacitus, a Roman with no reason to flatter Josephus, agrees in substance: the war broke out under Florus's administration.

How he got the job

  • Not on merit. Josephus says it plainly: Florus owed the post to his wife Cleopatra, who was a friend of Poppaea, Nero's wife. Josephus adds that Cleopatra was "in no way different from him in wickedness."
  • That connection at Nero's court also explains why complaints against him went nowhere while he was in office.

What he did

  • Open plunder. Josephus says whole cities were stripped, and that no one could get justice without paying for it — men were robbed "not in a private way" but wholesale, and Florus took a cut of banditry across the province rather than suppressing it.
  • The Caesarea crisis (66). When the Jews of Caesarea lost an imperial ruling on their civic rights and a local provocateur sacrificed a bird at a synagogue entrance, a Jewish delegation came to Florus with money (eight talents, per Josephus) begging help. He took the money, then had the delegates seized and imprisoned.
  • The robbery (66). Florus took seventeen talents out of the Temple treasury in Jerusalem, "on the pretense that Caesar needed it" — roughly 450 kg of silver by modern reckoning (the exact weight depends on which talent standard is used; flag it as an approximation). It was sacred money, held in trust, and everyone knew the emperor had not asked for it.
  • The mock collection. Jerusalem answered with bitter street theatre: people passed around a basket collecting coppers "for poor Florus," as if the governor were a beggar. Florus marched on the city with troops and demanded the mockers be handed over.
  • The massacres (May 66). When the city would not surrender them, his soldiers were loosed on the Upper Market area. Josephus gives 3,600 killed — including women, children, and even Jews who held Roman citizenship, some scourged and crucified before his tribunal. (The number is Josephus's; as with his other figures it may be rounded or inflated, but no source disputes that the killings were large and indiscriminate.)
  • Berenice's plea. Queen Berenice, in Jerusalem for a vow, sent her officers to beg him to stop, then came herself barefoot before his tribunal. He ignored her, and his soldiers nearly killed her too (see berenice-deep-dive.md). Florus then tried to seize the Temple itself; the crowd blocked the passages, and he withdrew to Caesarea — leaving a cohort behind whose later surrender and massacre sealed the rupture.
  • The sequence after he left — the stopped sacrifice for the emperor, the seizure of Masada, the defeat of Cestius Gallus — is told in the-war-66-70.md. Florus lit the fuse; others built the bomb.

What happened to him afterward?

  • He disappears from the record. Josephus never reports a trial, a punishment, or a death for him. Once Cestius Gallus, the governor of Syria, took over the crisis in late 66, Florus simply walks out of the history.
  • One older reference work (Smith's Dictionary) notes a death for Florus recorded in later writers (Orosius), but Josephus does not confirm it and modern references generally list his fate as unknown.
  • Honest bottom line: the man Josephus blames more than any other single person for starting the war was never, so far as any surviving source tells us, called to account for it.
