The "Khazar Theory" of Ashkenazi Origins — Koestler's Claim vs. the Mainstream Consensus

Research file, compiled 2026-11-19. The theory is considered fringe. This file gives the evidence on both sides with sources, fairly, in each side's strongest honest form — no mockery either way. Labels: [CLAIM] = the theory as its proponents state it; [CONSENSUS] = the mainstream scholarly/genetic position; [DEBATED] = live dispute.

1. What the theory claims

[CLAIM] In its classic form: the Jews of Eastern Europe — the Ashkenazim — are not primarily descended from the Jews of ancient Israel who migrated west to the Rhineland and then east. Instead, they descend in large part from the Khazars: the Turkic people whose ruling class converted to Judaism in the 8th–9th centuries, and who, after their empire was destroyed in 965–969, migrated westward into Poland, Lithuania, and Ukraine, becoming the great mass of East European Jewry.

The theory matters to people for reasons beyond demography: if true, it would mean the world's largest Jewish population descends mainly from converts rather than from the ancient Israelites — a point that has been picked up by anti-Zionist writers, by some antisemitic writers, and (ironically, given his intent) was advanced by its most famous proponent partly to defuse antisemitism.

2. Arthur Koestler, The Thirteenth Tribe (1976) — presented fairly

Who he was: Arthur Koestler (1905–1983) was a Hungarian-born Jewish novelist and journalist (Darkness at Noon), himself a former Zionist who had lived in Palestine. He was not an academic historian, and said so in effect by writing the book as a work of popular synthesis.

What the book argues [CLAIM]:

  • The Khazar conversion was real and, in Koestler's reading, broad — he accepted the strongest versions of the sources (e.g., Ibn Fadlan's "all the Khazars and their king are Jews") and the c. 740 date.
  • After Sviatoslav destroyed the khaganate, and again under Mongol pressure in the 13th century, Khazar Jews migrated west in large numbers into the rising Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, where Jewish numbers grew explosively.
  • The demographic math, he argued, could not be explained by the small Rhineland Jewish population alone: the Jews of Poland must have had a major Khazar source.
  • Therefore: "the Jews of our times" in Eastern Europe descend mainly from Khazars, not from the "twelve tribes" — hence the title, The Thirteenth Tribe.

His stated motive: Koestler wrote that he hoped the book would undermine racial antisemitism: if most Jews were not "Semites" by descent, the racial premise of Nazi-style hatred collapsed. Whatever one thinks of the argument, his intent was anti-antisemitic, and the book's later use by antisemitic and anti-Zionist writers was against his purpose.

What Koestler did and didn't do: He synthesized earlier scholars — above all Abraham Poliak (Tel Aviv University professor of Jewish history, whose 1951 Hebrew work Khazaria argued the Khazar origin of East European Jewry) and Hugo von Kutschera (1890s). He did no original archival or genetic research (genetics of this kind did not exist yet). Earlier anticipations of the idea exist too — Ernest Renan floated Khazar contributions to East European Jewry in the 19th century, and Karl Neumann (1847) had suggested Khazar emigrants influenced Eastern Jewry.

The book's modern afterlife: Shlomo Sand's bestseller The Invention of the Jewish People (2008) revived the Khazar thesis for a mass audience. Geneticist Eran Elhaik's 2013 paper ("The Missing Link of Jewish European Ancestry") claimed genetic support, modeling Khazars via Armenian and Georgian proxy populations — a method other geneticists heavily criticized (see §4).

3. The theory's strongest supporting points — stated honestly

A fair account has to concede what is genuinely true or arguable in the theory's foundation:

  1. The conversion is documented. A Turkic empire's ruling class really did adopt Judaism; that is attested by Muslim, Jewish, Latin, and Byzantine-era sources, not invented by Koestler. (See khazar-conversion-to-judaism.md.)
  2. The Khazars had to go somewhere. A state of that size, destroyed in the 960s, dispersed real people. Absorption into neighboring populations — including, plausibly, some Jewish communities — is not in doubt at the level of some individuals and groups.
  3. The Kabar precedent. Constantine Porphyrogenitus records three Khazar tribes joining the Magyars — proof that Khazar groups did migrate west and merge into European peoples.
  4. The demographic puzzle was real in Koestler's day. East European Jewry grew to millions; in 1976, before genetics, the size of the Rhineland founding population versus the later Polish population was a legitimate historical question.
  5. Direct ancient Khazar DNA long did not exist. For decades, geneticists tested the theory only indirectly, because there were no confirmed Khazar royal burials to sample. (A 2023 conference report on ancient Khazar genomes — see §4 — is a poster, not yet a full peer-reviewed paper, and its sample was small. [DEBATED])

What the theory needs, and has never produced, is positive evidence of a mass westward migration of Judaized Khazars into Poland-Lithuania in the 10th–13th centuries, or a genetic signature tying Ashkenazim to the Caucasus/steppe rather than to the Levant and Europe.

4. The mainstream consensus — and its evidence

[CONSENSUS] The mainstream position in Jewish history, linguistics, and population genetics is that Ashkenazi Jews descend mainly from Jews of the Mediterranean world — ultimately from the Land of Israel, through Italy and the Rhineland — with substantial local admixture in Europe, and that any Khazar contribution was at most small. The standard scholarly formulation, quoted verbatim by supporters of the consensus:

[CONSENSUS — Flegontov et al., 2016] "Virtually all contemporary historians and linguists reject any notion of Khazars directly contributing to the ancestry of Ashkenazi Jewry."

Historical and linguistic evidence

  • Yiddish. The mother tongue of Ashkenazi Jewry is a Germanic language (Middle High German base) with Hebrew-Aramaic and later Slavic components — the signature of a population that lived in German-speaking lands and moved east. A mass Khazar origin would predict a Turkic linguistic substrate in Yiddish; there is none. (Album item M275 claims Yiddish is "the original Khazar tongue" — this is the claim of the books clipped there, and it is contradicted by mainstream linguistics: Khazar itself was Turkic, Yiddish is Germanic.)
  • Documented migration. The paper trail runs the other way: Rhineland communities (Speyer, Worms, Mainz — the ShUM cities) are documented from the 10th century; Ashkenazi settlement in Poland grows with documented charters and migrations from German lands in the 13th–16th centuries, especially after persecutions in the west. No medieval source records a mass Khazar immigration into Poland.
  • The Khazar Jews' documented fate is absorption into the steppe and Crimean world (see khazar-empire-history.md, §7) — and the Crimean "Khazar descent" dossier is contaminated by the Firkovich forgeries.

Genetic evidence

  • Atzmon et al. 2010 (Nature, "Abraham's Children in the Genome Era"): genome-wide study finding the major Jewish groups (including Ashkenazim) share substantial Middle Eastern ancestry, with varying European admixture — a profile incompatible with a primarily Caucasus/Turkic origin.
  • Behar et al. 2013, "No Evidence from Genome-Wide Data of a Khazar Origin for the Ashkenazi Jews" (Human Biology): the direct test — 1,774 samples from 106 populations. Findings: Ashkenazim show no Caucasus/Khazar markers; their ancestry "reveals no significant contribution of Caucasus populations"; they cluster with other Jewish and Levantine populations.
  • Costa et al. 2013 (Nature Communications): ~80% of Ashkenazi maternal (mtDNA) lineages derive from prehistoric European sources — i.e., conversion/admixture in Europe long before, and unrelated to, the Khazar period. (This cuts against a purely Levantine picture too — the consensus is a mixed Levant + Europe origin, which is precisely what the historical record suggests.)
  • Waldman et al. 2022 (Cell): DNA from a medieval Ashkenazi cemetery in Erfurt, Germany (14th century) shows the medieval community already had the modern Ashkenazi genetic profile — continuity before the great Polish population growth, refuting a large later Khazar influx.
  • 2023 ASHG conference poster (Shaus, Shingiray, Lalueza-Fox et al.): ancient genomes from the Khazar Khaganate's core area show a typical Western Turkic profile, unlike Ashkenazi Jews — reported as putting the Khazar-origin claim "to rest." [Caveat: a conference poster, not yet a full peer-reviewed publication; treat as strong indication, not final word.]

The Elhaik counter-study, and why it didn't hold

Eran Elhaik's 2013 paper claimed Ashkenazi genomes matched "Khazaria" better than the Rhineland. The criticism was methodological and severe: he used Armenians and Georgians as stand-ins for Khazars (peoples who are not Turkic and never were the Khazars), and his "Khazarian" model was built partly from the conclusion it was meant to test. Behar et al.'s larger direct comparison found the Caucasus signal absent. Elhaik's work remains the theory's main genetic citation, and it has not been replicated into a consensus.

5. The fair bottom line

  • True and agreed: the Khazar elite converted to Judaism; Khazaria sheltered real Jewish communities; some Khazar individuals and groups were absorbed into surrounding peoples after 969, very plausibly including some Jewish communities of Eastern Europe. A minor Khazar contribution to Ashkenazi ancestry cannot be excluded and would surprise no historian.
  • Not supported: the theory's core claim — that Ashkenazi Jews descend mainly from Khazar converts. It is contradicted by the linguistics of Yiddish, by the documented Rhineland-to-Poland migration, and by two decades of genome-wide studies, most directly Behar et al. 2013.
  • Why it persists: the conversion itself is dramatic and real; the theory flatters several political narratives at once (anti-Zionist, antisemitic, and some nationalist ones); and Koestler wrote it brilliantly. Its persistence is a fact about the story's power, not about the evidence.
  • A note on stakes: nothing in the mainstream position requires Jewish identity to be "pure" descent — Judaism has accepted converts since antiquity, and the consensus itself describes Ashkenazim as a Levantine-European mixed population. Koestler's hope that ancestry arguments would settle hatred was, on the evidence of the last 50 years, misplaced either way.
