The menorah — a deep dive

Written 2026-11-20 , answering the questions from 2026-11-19 in order: what it was, what it was used for, what it resembled or signified, its meaning, its origins, and why it resembles the Jewish candle thing — the connections and ties. Where ancient writers disagree or a reading is only a theory, it is flagged as such.

1. What it was

The menorah — the Hebrew word just means "lamp" or "lampstand" — was the great golden lampstand of the Tabernacle, the portable sanctuary the Israelites carried through the desert, and then of the Temple in Jerusalem. Seven branches: a central stem with three curving arms on each side, every branch topped with a lamp.

The pattern is given in Exodus as shown to Moses on the mountain, in God's own words:

"And thou shalt make a candlestick of pure gold: of beaten work shall the candlestick be made" (Exodus 25:31).

It was hammered from a single piece of gold — a talent of it, per Exodus 25:39 (a talent is roughly 30 kg; the figure is the Bible's own). No soldering, no separate parts: one lump of gold, beaten out into a tree shape.

Every surface was worked with almond-blossom decoration — cups, buds and blossoms along the branches (Exodus 25:33–34). Josephus, who had seen the Second Temple's menorah with his own eyes, counts seventy ornaments in all.

Size: no ancient text gives its height directly. From the scale of the figure carrying it on the Arch of Titus relief, scholars usually estimate about five feet (roughly 1.5–1.6 m) — flag that as an estimate from the carving, not a measurement from a text.

One shape dispute, honestly flagged: the arms are almost always drawn curved, like the Arch of Titus carving. But Maimonides — and Rashi, on one reading — held the branches were straight, rising at an angle. His son Abraham left a sketch of straight branches. Philo and Josephus, who both describe round arms, and most other authorities, say curved. Nobody can settle it from the text alone; the seven lamps are the certain part, the curve is not.

2. What it was used for

It stood in the Holy Place — the outer room of the sanctuary, outside the veil of the Holy of Holies — alongside the table of showbread and the altar of incense. It was the room's light, and the only light in there: no windows.

The seven lamps burned pure olive oil, tended by the priests morning and evening. Exodus 27:20–21 commands it as a perpetual light:

"to cause the lamp to burn always" (Exodus 27:20).

The priests trimmed the wicks and refilled the oil every day. Josephus says three of the lamps were kept burning through the daylight hours, and all seven were lit at night. The Talmud (Menachot 86b) says the westernmost lamp — the one nearest the Holy of Holies — was the special one: it was given as much oil as the others but, by tradition, its flame lasted until the next evening, a small daily miracle. That lamp was called the ner tamid, the "perpetual light," and the other six were kindled from it.

So its daily job was simple and sacred at once: keep a light burning before God, without a break, in the holiest room in the world. Light was not decoration. In the Temple it meant God's presence was there.

It was also a Temple treasure in the plain sense: Solomon's Temple had the menorah — and more, 1 Kings 7:49 lists ten lampstands of gold, five on each side. The single seven-branched one belongs to the Tabernacle pattern and to the Second Temple. Babylon carried off the Temple vessels in 586 BC; Antiochus IV looted the menorah in 169 BC (1 Maccabees 1:21–23); the Maccabees, cleansing the Temple in 164 BC, "made… a new candlestick" among the restored vessels (1 Maccabees 4:49–50). Herod's rebuilt Temple housed a menorah of the same pattern — the one Rome took in AD 70. (The full trail after Rome — Arch of Titus, Carthage, Constantinople, then silence — is in menorah.md.)

3. What it resembled, and what it signified

This is where the ancient writers themselves start interpreting, and they do not all say the same thing — so here are their readings, each labelled with who said it.

A tree. The shape itself is a tree: a central trunk, branches spreading out, almond blossoms and buds all over it. The almond was the first tree to flower in spring in Israel, a sign of watchfulness and new life. Some modern scholars go further and suggest the menorah began as a tree of life image, and one theory links it to the goddess Asherah's tree symbol, purged of polytheism in the biblical version. That Asherah link is a scholarly theory, not a settled fact — flag it as such. What is certain is the tree shape and the almond decoration, straight from Exodus.

The seven days of creation. In his Jewish War (7.5.5), Josephus says the seven branches with their seven lamps were "an emblem of the seven days of creation and rest." Six branches for the six days of work, the central lamp for the Sabbath — the day of rest that gives light to all the rest. That reading is still the most quoted one in Jewish tradition.

The seven planets — the whole universe. In his Antiquities (3.7.7), Josephus reads it the other way, as a model of the heavens: the seventy ornaments "secretly intimated the Decani, or seventy divisions of the planets; and as to the seven lamps upon the candlesticks, they referred to the course of the planets, of which that is the number." The seven were the sun, the moon, and the five planets known then — Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn. Philo of Alexandria says the same: the central lamp is the sun, and the six around it are the planets. So for Josephus and Philo, the menorah was a little universe in gold: stand before it and you were standing before a model of creation itself.

Wisdom and the Torah. The Talmud links the menorah with wisdom: its light is the light of the Torah shining in the world. The prophet Zechariah saw it in a vision (Zechariah 4:1–6) — a golden lampstand with seven lamps, fed by two olive trees — and heard the words, "Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts." In that vision the menorah is not just a lamp; it is God's spirit keeping Israel alive.

Light and life. The scholar E. R. Goodenough, studying menorahs carved on Jewish tombs in the Greco-Roman world, read it as the mystic symbol of light and life — God made manifest in the world, through whom Jews hoped for immortality. That is why the menorah turns up everywhere in the ancient Jewish world — on coins, on synagogue walls, in catacombs across the Roman Empire. It became the sign by which a Jew marked a place as Jewish.

The short answer to "what did it mean": a tree of light, standing for creation, for the heavens, for wisdom, and for God's presence that never goes out. The ancient writers agreed on the light; they argued about which picture the light was painting.

4. Origins — where it came from

The Bible's own origin story is the mountain: God showed Moses the pattern on Mount Sinai, and Bezalel the craftsman made it in the desert, from one piece of gold (Exodus 25:40, 37:17–24). That is the origin as the tradition tells it.

What historians can add, honestly:

  • No menorah survives from the First Temple period. There is no picture or object of one from Solomon's time. The earliest pictures we have are Second Temple period and later — coins, synagogue carvings, and above all the Arch of Titus.
  • The seven-branched lampstand was unusual. Most ancient lampstands had one lamp, or a few. A seven-branched tree of lamps is distinctive, and that distinctiveness is part of why it became the symbol.
  • It was never copied for ordinary use. The Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 24a–b) records that a seven-branched menorah was reserved for the Temple alone — other menorahs in homes and synagogues could have five, six or eight branches, but not seven. That rule is why the seven-branch shape still means "the Temple" whenever you see it.

5. The Jewish candle thing — the connections and ties

The direct question: why does the Temple menorah look like the candles Jewish families light today? Because the candles today descend from it — three different descendants, and they are not the same thing. Keeping them apart is the whole trick:

(a) The Hanukkah menorah — the chanukiah, nine branches

This is the one most people picture. It has eight lights plus a ninth, the shamash (the "helper" or "servant" light), which is set apart — higher, lower, or off to the side — and is used to light the other eight.

Its story goes back to the menorah itself. When the Maccabees retook the Temple in 164 BC and relit the menorah, the Talmud (Shabbat 21b) says they found only one sealed jar of pure olive oil, enough for one day — and it burned for eight days, until new pure oil could be made. To remember that, Jewish families light one more light each night of Hanukkah for eight nights.

So the chanukiah is not the Temple menorah. It is a memorial of the Temple menorah's relighting: eight lights for the eight days of the miracle, plus the shamash to do the lighting. A seven-branched menorah cannot be used for Hanukkah — the lighting needs eight plus one. Same family, different object, different job.

(b) The Shabbat candles — usually two, on Friday night

Every Friday evening, before sunset, candles are lit in the home to welcome the Sabbath. The usual custom is two candles, and the oldest reason given for two is that they stand for the two words of the Sabbath commandment: "Remember" and "Observe" — the two versions of the commandment in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:8 and Deuteronomy 5:12). That explanation goes back to the early Ashkenazic rabbis, around the 1100s–1200s.

The tie to the Temple menorah is the light itself: the home takes over a piece of the Temple's job. The tradition even reads the command to keep the Temple lamp burning (Exodus 27:20, "you shall command… to raise up a candle to burn eternally") as pointing toward the women's lighting of Shabbat candles in the home — the Temple's eternal light, carried into the house once the Temple was gone. Shabbat candles are not a little menorah; they are the Temple's light, kept alive at home.

(c) The ner tamid — the eternal light in the synagogue

Every synagogue today has a lamp burning before the ark, the ner tamid — the "eternal light." It is the direct descendant of the menorah's western lamp, the one that burned constantly in the Temple. The synagogue is called in tradition a mikdash me'at, a "small sanctuary," and its ever-burning light is the Temple's ever-burning light, kept going in miniature.

So when "the Jewish candle thing" and it looks like the menorah — it is because it is the menorah's family. The Temple lamp was taken, the Temple was destroyed, and the light did not die with it: it split into the Hanukkah lights, the Friday-night candles, and the lamp that never goes out in the synagogue. That is the connection.

6. After the Temple — why it is still everywhere

  • The menorah became the emblem of the State of Israel — the seven-branched lampstand from the Arch of Titus, flanked by olive branches, is on Israel's coat of arms. The carving the Romans made to celebrate carrying it away became the symbol of the people they carried it away from.
  • The Temple Institute in Jerusalem has made a full-size replica of the Temple menorah in gold, ready for a rebuilt Temple — a modern craftsman's version of the Exodus pattern.
  • And in Jewish art and on gravestones, the menorah is still the mark of Jewish identity — the sign, as one scholar put it, of light and life, and of a light that was never meant to go out.

Telling order (for the walk-through)

  1. What it was — one piece of gold, beaten into a tree, seven lamps (Exodus's own words).
  2. What it did — burned olive oil morning and evening, the western lamp never out.
  3. What it meant — creation's seven days, the seven planets, the tree of life, wisdom, God's presence (Josephus and Philo named).
  4. Where it came from — the mountain pattern, and the honest limit: nothing survives from the First Temple.
  5. The candle thing — chanukiah (9, Hanukkah), Shabbat candles (2, Remember and Observe), ner tamid (1, always burning) — three descendants, not the same object.
  6. The end of the trail — Rome, Carthage, Constantinople, silence (in menorah.md), and the emblem of Israel today.
