3. The Spanish historian

James remembered "a Spanish historian that talked about him changing his name." The name that fits best is Salvador de Madariaga (1886–1978), the Spanish historian and diplomat. A second, earlier Spanish claimant is Celso García de la Riega — both are set out here so James can see which one he meant.

Salvador de Madariaga — Christopher Columbus (1940)

A period review states it plainly (Present Truth, April 4, 1940):

"IN his latest book, Christopher Columbus, Salvador de Madariaga raises again the disputed question of whether the great discoverer of America was a Jew."

Religion News Service's history of the claim (October 28, 2024) says the same:

"Nobel Prize nominee Salvador de Madariaga endorsed the theory of Columbus' Jewish origins in his 1940 book on Don Cristobal Colón."

Madariaga's argument, as summarised by historian Jonathan D. Sarna: Columbus's Castilian Spanish was polished while his Italian was poor — odd for a man supposedly born and raised in Genoa. Madariaga "anchored his thesis on this point, hypothesizing that Columbus' parents were Spanish Jews who had departed for Genoa in the face of persecution and then 'remained faithful to the language of their country of origin.'" On that reading, the Genoese "Cristoforo Colombo" was the cover identity of a Spanish Jew whose real name and tongue were Cristóbal Colón and Castilian.

Celso García de la Riega — the Pontevedra theory (1898–1914)

The Spanish writer Celso García de la Riega argued from 1898, and in print by World War I, that Columbus was a Spaniard from Pontevedra, Galicia — the same Pontevedra "Colon" family the 1912 Sentinel article discusses — and of hidden Jewish origin. The claim was "bluntly refuted" in 1913 by the American historian Henry Vignaud, who showed the Genoese documents and that La Riega's claims were false (per the historiography summary in §6). García de la Riega is the Spanish source behind the name side of the claim — a documented Jewish family actually surnamed Colón in Pontevedra.

Other named claimants (for the trail)

  • Cecil Roth, A History of the Marranos (1932) — promoted the idea Columbus was a marrano (a forced convert); Sarna notes Roth "even hinted that he could identify Columbus' real Jewish name," and that among Italian Jews the shift back and forth from "Colon" to "Colombo" was "not only possible but invariable."
  • Simon Wiesenthal, Sails of Hope (1973) — argued Columbus was a Spanish converso seeking, among other things, a safe haven for persecuted Jews.
  • Estelle Irizarry (Georgetown, 2009) — linguistic analysis concluding Columbus's writing shows features compatible with Ladino / Sephardic writing patterns.

4. Spanish & Italian news — the 2024 DNA study

What was announced

On October 12, 2024 — Spain's national day — the Spanish public broadcaster RTVE (TVE) aired the documentary "Colón ADN: su verdadero origen" ("Columbus DNA: His True Origin"), presenting a 22-year investigation led by José Antonio Lorente, professor of forensic medicine at the University of Granada, with historian Marcial Castro. Samples had been taken in 2003 from the fragmentary remains in Seville Cathedral attributed to Columbus, and from his son Hernando (Fernando) Colón and his brother Diego.

Lorente's own words in the documentary, as reported by Reuters and repeated across the coverage:

"We have DNA from Christopher Columbus, very partial, but sufficient. We have DNA from Hernando Colón, his son. And both in the Y chromosome (male) and in the mitochondrial DNA (transmitted by the mother) of Hernando there are traits compatible with Jewish origin."

The documentary's conclusion: Columbus was not Genoese but a Sephardic Jew from the Spanish Mediterranean coast — most likely Valencia — following the long-standing theory of the Catalan researcher Francesc Albardaner that Columbus came from a Valencian family of Jewish silk weavers. The team also said the Seville remains were "definitively confirmed" as Columbus's. Lorente told Reuters:

"Today it has been possible to verify it with new technologies, so that the previous partial theory that the remains of Seville belong to Christopher Columbus has been definitively confirmed."

Italian coverage — ANSA wire, October 13, 2024

Italy's ANSA news agency carried the story the next day under the headline:

"'Cristoforo Colombo non era genovese ma spagnolo e ebreo'" ("Christopher Columbus was not Genoese but Spanish and Jewish")

ANSA's report (as carried by the Italian paper Il Nord Est) gives the claim and the pushback in one piece: the researchers "guidati dal medico forense José Antonio Llorente, dell'università di Granada" concluded Columbus "sarebbe stato un ebreo sefardita originario di luoghi che potrebbero essere la costa di Valencia, della Catalogna o delle Baleari" — and then, in ANSA's words, "Una conclusione tuttavia contestata dagli specialisti che criticano la mancanza di prove scientifiche" (a conclusion contested by specialists who criticise the lack of scientific proof). ANSA quotes Antonio Alonso, former director of Spain's National Institute of Toxicology and Forensic Sciences, via El País:

"La mia conclusione è che il documentario Colon Adn non mostra in alcun momento il Dna di Colombo" ("My conclusion is that the Colón ADN documentary does not at any moment show Columbus's DNA")

The response, and the study's status now

  • The results were announced on television, not in a scientific journal. As reported at the time and since: Lorente's research "has not been published in any peer-reviewed scientific journal, and Lorente has not made the data available for review by other scientists." In response, "forensic geneticists and historians dismissed the claim," pointing to the documentary record for Genoa.
  • A detailed later examination (ExploreYourDNA, updated 2026) states the position plainly: "No sequence data has been published, no haplogroup has been named, and no peer reviewed paper accompanies the claim." It also notes a structural problem with the mitochondrial half of the claim: Hernando's mtDNA came from his mother, Beatriz Enríquez de Arana of Córdoba — not from Columbus — so it describes her line, not his.
  • A Jewish News Review summary (2026) agrees: "Two years later, the promised scientific publication still has not appeared."
  • Ohio State historian Matt Goldish's caution, widely quoted: "DNA does not actually tell you that someone is or is not Jewish — it says that most Jews from a certain region seem to have common ancestors, and a given person shares to whatever degree those ancestors."

Status, stated plainly: as of the latest coverage checked (late 2026), the 2024 claims remain unpublished in peer review, with the underlying data unreleased. "Compatible with Jewish origin" is the strongest wording the study's own leader used. The claim is not accepted as settled by historians or geneticists.

5. The name question — Colombo / Colón / Xpo Ferens

What the documents actually show about his names:

  • In Genoa: the notarial record names him Cristoforo Colombo, son of Domenico Colombo, a wool weaver, and Susanna Fontanarossa. "Colombo" is an ordinary Italian surname (it means "dove").
  • In Spain: he is Cristóbal Colón in Spanish service and signed himself so — the 1912 Sentinel quotes his tomb boast: "A Castilla y a Leon, Nuevo mondo dio Colon." "Colón" is also a genuine Spanish surname — including, claimants note, a documented Jewish family named Colón at Pontevedra.
  • His signature: Columbus signed with a cryptic pyramid of letters ending "Xpo FERENS" — Christoferens, Latin/Greek for "Christ-bearer," matching the meaning of "Christopher." Claimants have read the letter pyramid as a coded Hebrew prayer (one 1966 Cádiz lecture, by Brother Nectario María, called it an acrostic of a Hebrew prayer for forgiveness "for the sin of pretending to be a Christian"); mainstream historians treat it as a devotional Christogram, not a code.
  • His languages: his surviving writing is in Castilian Spanish (with Portuguese colouring) and Latin. He left essentially no Italian writing — the fact Madariaga built his thesis on. The mainstream answer is that a Genoese who left Italy young and lived in Portugal and Spain would write the languages he lived in.
  • His own statement of origin: in his 1498 deed of entail (mayorazgo), Columbus refers to Genoa as his homeland — the single strongest document on the other side, quoted in every mainstream biography.

So the "changed his name" claim, precisely: nobody disputes that Colombo became Colón when he moved from Italian to Spanish service — names were routinely translated between languages in that century. The disputed claim is the stronger one: that the change was a concealment — a Spanish Jew hiding behind a Genoese identity (Madariaga, García de la Riega) or a Jew hiding his religion behind a Christian name (Rabbi Simon, in the 1912 Kennebec item).

6. His parents — Domenico Colombo and Susanna Fontanarossa

James asked for a section of its own on the father and mother: who they were, where they were from, and whether they were Jewish — claims and record kept separate.

The documented record

Domenico Colombo (the father). Genoa's notarial archives hold the family in ordinary detail. Domenico was the son of Giovanni Colombo of Quinto, outside Genoa; in 1429, aged eleven, Giovanni signed him as an apprentice weaver. He became a master weaver in Genoa's wool guild, kept a weaving shop with a workman and an apprentice, dealt also in wool and wine, kept a tavern and sold cheese, and in 1447 the Doge of Genoa made him keeper of the Porta dell'Olivella, one of the city gates. The Genoese chronicler Antonio Gallo, who knew the family, wrote in 1506 that Christopher and Bartolomeo were brothers "of Ligurian birth and raised in Genoa among plebeian relatives… for their father was a weaver, and the sons were sometimes carders." An older biography sums up the paper trail:

"The fact of the father of Christopher Columbus being a Genoese, cannot be disputed. It is proved by his own declarations, in many authentic acts, whose originals exist at this day, preserved in the archives of the notary's offices of Savone and Genoa."

Christopher enters the same record working beside him: a notarial act of September 1470 shows Columbus, aged nineteen, in his father's wool business, and by 1472 he is styled lanaiolo — wool worker. The Genoa state archive holds some 188 official documents on Columbus and his family.

Susanna Fontanarossa (the mother). She married Domenico in the mid-1440s and bore five children — Cristoforo, Bartolomeo, Giovanni Pellegrino, Giacomo, and Bianchinetta. A notarised deed in the Genoa archive names her in Latin:

"Sozana, (quondam) de Jacobi de Fontana Rubea, uxor Dominici de Columbo de Ianua ac Christophorus et Pelegrinus filii eorum" — "Susanna, daughter of the late Giacomo of Fontanarossa of the Bisagno, wife of Domenico Colombo of Genoa, and Cristoforo and Pellegrino, their sons."

Her family was from Fontanarossa in the Bisagno valley, the inland district behind Genoa. (A different village also named Fontanarossa, in the Trebbia valley, claims her too — it has a marble plaque reading "In questo borgo nacque Susanna Fontanarossa, madre di Cristoforo Colombo" — "In this village was born Susanna Fontanarossa, mother of Christopher Columbus." The archive deed ties her to the Bisagno one.)

Honest gap: beyond that deed and the marriage, almost nothing about Susanna survives — no record of her own words, her family's religion, or even firm dates for her birth and death. Everything said about her beyond the deed is inference, which is exactly the space the claims below move into.

The claims made about them

The Pontevedra parents (García de la Riega, answered by Vignaud, 1913). The Spanish claimant Celso García de la Riega found, in Pontevedra's archives, a Colón family whose names mirror the Genoa family — Domingo Colón (1434, 1437), Bartolomeo (1438), Cristobo (1496), Blanca (1434) — and, in Henry Vignaud's account of the theory (American Historical Review, April 1913):

"…during the same period there was at Pontevedra a Fonterossa family who had relations with the Colons, and who were Jews, if we are to judge by their Biblical forenames."

Vignaud lays out the leap the theory then makes — that Domingo Colón of Pontevedra was Columbus's father, and that the mother,

"…whose name of Susanna is Jewish and whose family name of Fontenarossa closely resembles that of the Jewish family of Fonterossa of Pontevedra, was herself of that same family"

— and gives his verdict in the same breath:

"Clearly the documents which have been brought to light establish nothing of the kind."

The 1912 Sentinel article in §2 prints the Pontevedra paper the theory leans on: a document of July 29, 1427, recording a debt "due jointly to Domingo de Colon and Benjamin Fonterossa." A Colon and a Fonterossa doing business together in Galicia in 1427 is a real document; that they were Columbus's parents is the unproven step.

The "Ponti Rossi" newspaper claim (rebutted 1924). American newspaper editors of the period went further. Walter F. McEntire's Was Christopher Columbus a Jew (1924) quotes them claiming "the blood that flowed in Columbus' veins was three-quarters Jewish" and that "investigation shows that his mother came of a well known Jewish family, the Ponti* Rossi." McEntire, who was otherwise sympathetic to parts of the theory, corrects the name flatly:

"The name of Columbus' mother was not Ponti Rossi, as stated in the newspaper articles referred to in our introduction: …her name was Susanna of Fontanarossa. The name Susanna is Hebrew, signifying lily."

("Ponti Rossi" looks like a garbled echo of "Fontanarossa" passed from paper to paper.)

Madariaga's version (1940). As set out in §3: Columbus's parents were Spanish Jews who "departed for Genoa in the face of persecution" and "remained faithful to the language of their country of origin" — which would make Domenico and Susanna Spanish conversos living under Italian names. No Genoese or Spanish document naming such a couple has ever been produced.

The name itself as "evidence." Later writers keep returning to the mother's first name. A modern survey of the origins debate notes: "Among those who argue Columbus was a converted Jew, his mother's name is held up as evidence in favour of that theory." A popular 2014 claimant book, Columbus, Secret Jew, puts it as a question — whether "his mother, Susanna (Shoshana in Hebrew) Fontanarossa, is thought to have been the offspring of a Jewish family, some of the descendants of whom were burned at the stake during the Spanish Inquisition." Susanna is a Hebrew name (Shoshana, "lily") — but it was also a normal Christian name in Liguria, borne by Domenico's wife in a deed that calls her father Giacomo. The name alone proves nothing either way, and no document ties her family to any burning.

What the 2024 DNA study said about the parents — stated precisely. The Granada documentary did not name, document, or test Domenico or Susanna. Two careful points: - The Y chromosome passes father to son, so the "traits compatible with Jewish origin" reported in Hernando's Y would, if the Seville remains and the family chain are what the team says, bear on Columbus's paternal line — the line of Domenico. That is an inference about ancestry, not a document about Domenico, and it rests on data that has not been published (§4). - The mitochondrial DNA the team discussed was Hernando's — and a son's mtDNA comes from his own mother, Beatriz Enríquez de Arana of Córdoba. It says nothing at all about Susanna. Popular write-ups that said the DNA showed Jewish heritage "through both his mother's and his father's families" were describing Hernando's parents (Columbus and Beatriz), not Columbus's. - The documentary's preferred origin theory (Francesc Albardaner's) replaces the Genoa parents wholesale: in ANSA's report, Albardaner holds that Columbus "nasce da una famiglia di tessitori di seta di Valencia" — "is born into a family of silk weavers of Valencia, a great tradition in the Jewish community." Those Valencian parents are unnamed and undocumented; the theory does not reinterpret Domenico and Susanna, it deletes them.

7. The Spanish Inquisition — the world that made men hide their names

James asked for this as its own section: the backdrop that explains why, in Columbus's Spain, a changed name and a concealed grandfather could be a matter of life and death. It may later be split out into a standalone topic.

What led up to it

1391 — the massacres and the mass conversions. A century before the Inquisition, Spain held the largest Jewish community in Europe. In June 1391, after years of preaching against the Jews by the Seville archdeacon Ferrán Martínez, riots broke out in Seville and spread. A modern account (London Review of Books):

"In June 1391, an anti-Jewish riot broke out in Seville, prompted by the incendiary preaching of a local priest. Four thousand Jews were murdered, and the violence soon spread to more than ninety Iberian cities."

Under threat of death, Jews converted in enormous numbers — "A third to a half of the Spanish Jews — the largest community in Europe at the time — were converted to Christianity, the greatest mass conversion in modern Jewish history" (Atlas Obscura). Preaching campaigns by Vincent Ferrer and the forced Disputation of Tortosa (1413–14) brought tens of thousands more. The converts and their descendants were the conversos, or New Christians.

The converso rise — and the resentment. Baptism opened doors that had been shut to Jews: conversos became royal advisers, tax farmers, physicians, bishops, and city councillors. "Conversion also opened access to positions New Christians could not have attained as Jews… Predictably, there was soon a backlash" (LRB). Old Christians increasingly suspected that many conversos were Christians only on the surface — judaizantes, secretly keeping Jewish practice — and resented their success whether the suspicion was true or not.

1449 — Toledo and "purity of blood." The backlash turned legal at Toledo. A tax revolt in January 1449 against the royal favourite Álvaro de Luna's demand for a loan of a million maravedís was redirected against the city's conversos by the rebel Pero Sarmiento. On June 5, 1449, his regime issued the Sentencia-Estatuto, barring all conversos — sincere Christians included — from public and private office in Toledo. It declared them, in the statute's own framing, "descendants of the lineage and breed of the Jews," and ordered their places filled by "fine old Christians [christianos viejos lindos]." The rebels justified it by blood: conversos came from "the perverse lineage of the Jews." This was the birth of limpieza de sangre — "purity of blood" — the idea that Jewish ancestry itself, not belief, disqualified a person. The king and the pope both condemned the Toledo statute, yet blood-purity rules spread anyway, into cathedrals, colleges, guilds, and military orders, and lasted for centuries.

1477–78 — the monarchs act. Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, newly joined and fighting to unify their kingdoms, heard reports — pressed by the Dominican Alonso de Ojeda during the queen's stay in Seville in 1477–78 — that Andalusian conversos were practising Judaism in secret. For the monarchs the motives were mixed, and historians list them together: genuine religious zeal for one faith in one realm; state power — a tribunal answering to the crown, not to local bishops or Rome; and money — the property of the convicted flowed to the royal treasury. On November 1, 1478, Pope Sixtus IV issued the bull Exigit sinceras devotionis affectus, authorising the monarchs to appoint inquisitors of their own choosing. That royal control is what made the Spanish Inquisition different from the medieval inquisitions before it: as the New World Encyclopedia puts it, it was founded "under the direct control of the Spanish monarchy."

How it worked

  • First inquisitors: the Dominicans Miguel de Morillo and Juan de San Martín, appointed September 27, 1480, at Medina del Campo; the first tribunal sat at Seville.
  • First auto-da-fé: Seville, February 6, 1481. "On February 6, 1481, six people were burned at the stake in Seville. They were conversos, Spaniards of Jewish descent whose families had converted to Christianity, and they had been convicted of secretly continuing to practice Judaism."
  • Torquemada: in 1483 the Dominican Tomás de Torquemada — the monarchs' confessor, himself of converso descent on the usual accounts — became Inquisitor General and built the machine: a central council (the Suprema), regional tribunals, and a uniform rulebook.
  • Who it could touch: in law, only the baptised. The Inquisition "had jurisdiction only over baptized Christians" — an unbaptised Jew committed no heresy against a faith he had never joined. Its prey was the converso suspected of judaizing. (That legal fact is the direct reason for the Alhambra Decree below: the unconverted Jews were expelled by royal order, not tried by the Holy Office.)
  • Method: an Edict of Grace opened each tribunal's work — a period, usually 30–40 days, to confess and to denounce others. After that came secret denunciations (the accused was not told who accused him), arrest with seizure of all property, months or years in the Inquisition's prisons, interrogation, and torture in a minority of cases under written rules. Most cases ended in penance, not death: fines, flogging, exile, the galleys — and the sanbenito, the penitential robe hung afterwards in the parish church with the family name on it, a public stain on descendants for generations. Those "relaxed" to the secular arm were burned at the auto-da-fé, the great public ceremony of sentences.
  • The numbers — estimates, labelled as estimates. Modern archival estimates: around 150,000 people prosecuted over the Inquisition's 356 years (1478–1834), of whom between 3,000 and 5,000 were executed — "Between 3000 to 5000 people died during the Inquisition's 350 years" (New World Encyclopedia) — with most executions in the first fifty years, the anti-converso decades of Columbus's lifetime. Nineteenth-century writers, including the Inquisition's own former secretary Juan Antonio Llorente (a different Lorente from the DNA researcher of §4), gave far higher figures — tens of thousands burned — which modern historians working from the surviving case files do not accept.

How it affected Columbus

He sailed for the Inquisition's monarchs, on the Inquisition's money trails. Columbus's patrons and paymasters were the very class the Holy Office hunted: - Luis de Santángel, the royal finance official who talked Ferdinand and Isabella into the voyage and advanced much of its cost, was a third-generation converso. The Jewish Virtual Library's sober summary: "It is certain only that the expedition's interpreter, Luis de Torres, was born a Jew but had converted shortly before the expedition set sail; that two 'New Christians,' Luis de Santangel and Gabriel Sanchez, had a hand in the financing." - Gabriel Sánchez, treasurer of Aragon, came of a distinguished converso family of Saragossa (Encyclopaedia Judaica). After the inquisitor Pedro de Arbués was murdered in 1485, Sánchez's own brothers were accused of complicity and fled Aragon; "grave charges were brought against Gabriel as well. The Inquisition, however, disregarded these accusations and his position was not affected." Columbus's famous first report of the discovery was addressed, in its two versions, to Santángel and to Sánchez. - Claimed, beyond the documents: popular retellings add Don Isaac Abrabanel, the Jewish statesman, as a third lender of the same "interest free loan of 17,000 ducats" — a figure that comes from claimant literature (and from Henry Ford's The International Jew, which has Santángel advance "17,000 ducats in all"). Santángel's role and money are documented; the tidy three-lender loan, and Abrabanel's part in it, are not in the mainstream record. One further sourced claim, from a Jewish Museum exhibit text: the second, largest voyage (17 ships) "was financed through the sale of confiscated Jewish property" — confiscation being the Inquisition's engine.

The timing — 1492. On March 31, 1492, in the Alhambra palace at Granada, Ferdinand and Isabella signed the decree expelling every Jew who would not accept baptism; it was proclaimed on April 30, giving four months — to the end of July, extended to August 2, 1492. Modern estimates of the expelled run from about 40,000 to 100,000 people (period writers gave far larger numbers). Columbus sailed from Palos on August 3, 1492 — the morning after the last exiles embarked.

Columbus put the expulsion in the very prologue of his journal — the document he wrote for the monarchs themselves. In the standard translation (from Las Casas's surviving abstract of the lost original):

"So after having expelled the Jews from your dominions, Your Highnesses, in the same month of January, ordered me to proceed with a sufficient armament to the said regions of India."

Two honest notes on that sentence. First, Columbus misdates the expulsion: he says January (when his commission was agreed); the decree was March 31 — Meyer Kayserling flagged it in 1894: "Not in January, as Columbus asserts in his journal, but on March 31, 1492, the Catholic monarchs sent forth from the palace of the Alhambra the edict." Second, the prologue remarks on the expulsion but does not describe the departing crowds; the picture of a harbour full of exiles comes from the dates themselves — deadline August 2, sailing August 3 — which Kayserling already juxtaposed: "On August 2d the Spanish Jews began their wanderings, and the next day, Friday, August 3d, Columbus with his fleet of three ships, the Santa Maria, Pinta, and Nina, sailed…"

Luis de Torres — the converso in the landing boat. Columbus's interpreter was a Jew who had accepted baptism in the shadow of the expulsion — the ANU Museum's account: "Right after the publication of expulsion decree, Luis de Torres converted to Christianity in order to save himself and became a 'Converso'." Las Casas's abstract of the diary describes him in Columbus's own circle's words: "un Luys de Torres que avía bivido con el Adelantado de Murçia y avía sido judío y sabía dizque ebrayco y caldeo y aun algo arávigo" — "a Luis de Torres who had lived with the Governor of Murcia and had been a Jew, and knew, it is said, Hebrew and Chaldean and some Arabic." He was chosen because Columbus, expecting Asia, wanted a man who could address the Great Khan's court or Jewish merchants of the East in Hebrew. On November 2, 1492, Columbus sent Torres and the sailor Rodrigo de Jerez inland in Cuba — the expedition's first embassy into the new land — where they were received in a large village and brought back the first European report of tobacco smoking. Later sources give Torres's birth name as Yosef ben HaLevi HaIvri; he stayed behind at the La Navidad settlement on Hispaniola with 39 men when Columbus sailed for Spain, and all of them were dead by the time Columbus returned in 1493.

The theorists' argument — labelled as argument. The Jewish-descent writers (Wiesenthal, Madariaga's readers, the modern claimants) make one climate argument, and it should be stated as they make it, not as fact: in a kingdom where a grandmother's name, a neighbour's denunciation, or a Fonterossa in the family tree could bring the Holy Office to the door — and where the Admiral's own backers were conversos living under that threat — a man of Jewish descent who wanted royal favour had every reason to say as little as possible about where he came from. The silence they point to is real: Columbus never once, in any surviving letter, states where he was born. What the silence means is the argument: the mainstream reads it as a self-made man's embarrassment at a weaver's cottage; the theorists read it as concealment. The Inquisition is why the second reading exists at all.

8. What the record shows vs. what is claimed

Claimed (by the sources above, in their own words): - Columbus's true name was Christoval Colón, "a Jewish name"; he "changed his name and concealed his true religion" because his race was persecuted (Rabbi Simon, via the Daily Kennebec Journal, 1912). - A Jewish family named Colón lived at Pontevedra, Galicia, and Columbus descended from it (Del Olmet / García de la Riega line, discussed in The Sentinel, 1912). - Columbus's parents were Spanish Jews who moved to Genoa and kept their Spanish tongue (Madariaga, 1940). - Columbus was a marrano (Cecil Roth, 1932); a converso seeking a refuge for Jews (Wiesenthal, 1973). - DNA from the Seville remains shows traits "compatible with Jewish origin," and Columbus was a Sephardic Jew from the Valencia coast (Lorente / RTVE, 2024 — unpublished, data unreleased).

The record shows: - Genoese notarial documents name Cristoforo Colombo, son of Domenico Colombo of Genoa; Columbus himself named Genoa as his homeland in 1498; his son Ferdinand's biography and contemporaries place the family in Liguria. - The mainstream verdict was put by Samuel Eliot Morison (1942), whose biography's index famously reads "Jews, C.C. not one": there is "no more reason to doubt that Christopher Columbus was a Genoese-born Catholic Christian, steadfast in his faith and proud of his native city, than to doubt that George Washington was a Virginian-born Anglican of English race." - Even the sympathetic 1912 Sentinel article concluded there was "not a scintilla of evidence" that Columbus's faith was Jewish. - The 2024 DNA claim has not passed peer review and its data has not been released; specialists quoted in Spanish and Italian coverage dispute it.

Bottom line: the Jewish-Columbus theory is a real, documented, century-old claim with named claimants — Spanish, Jewish, and academic — and James's newspapers were printing it by 1912. It remains a minority claim, not the historians' consensus, and the 2024 DNA announcement has not changed that status as of the latest coverage.

9. The Diary (Diario) — original Spanish and English, side by side

James asked for the diary itself, "original and translated." Here is the honesty point first, because it decides what "original" can mean:

Columbus's original diary manuscript is lost. He presented it to Ferdinand and Isabella; both it and a complete early copy have been lost for centuries. What survives is the version made by Bartolomé de las Casas in the 1530s — part word-for-word quotation of Columbus, part Las Casas's own summary of him. The standard scholarly edition (Oliver Dunn & James E. Kelley Jr., The Diario of Christopher Columbus's First Voyage to America, 1492–1493, University of Oklahoma Press, 1989) describes the situation exactly:

"Unfortunately both Columbus's original manuscript, presented to Ferdinand and Isabella along with other evidence of his discoveries, and a single complete copy have been lost for centuries. The primary surviving record of the voyage — part quotation, part summary of the complete copy — is a transcription made by Bartolomé de las Casas in the 1530s."

So the Spanish printed below is the surviving Spanish text of the Las Casas abstract, labelled as such — it is the closest thing to Columbus's original words that exists. One part Las Casas presents as verbatim: the prologue. The old Navarrete edition heads it: "sin el prólogo que hizo a los Reyes, que va a la letra y comienza de esta manera" — "without the prologue he made for the Sovereigns, which goes word for word, and begins in this manner."

Passage 1 — The Prologue: the expulsion of the Jews

Spanish (Las Casas text, as transcribed by the Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes, Los cuatro viajes del almirante y su testamento):

"Así que, después de haber echado fuera todos los judíos de todos vuestros reinos y señoríos, en el mismo mes de enero mandaron Vuestras Altezas a mí que con armada suficiente me fuese a las dichas partidas de India; y para ello me hicieron grandes mercedes y me anoblecieron que dende en adelante yo me llamase Don y fuese Almirante Mayor de la mar océana e Visorrey y Gobernador perpetuo de todas las islas y tierra firme que yo descubriese y ganase y de aquí adelante se descubriesen y ganasen en la mar océana, y así sucediese mi hijo mayor y así de grado en grado para siempre jamás."

English (standard published translation, as reproduced by the University of Miami):

"So after having expelled all the Jews from the entirety of your dominions, Your Highnesses, in the same month of January, ordered me to proceed with a sufficient armament to the said regions of India."

(The translation excerpt ends there; the Spanish continues with the titles granted — Don, Admiral Major of the Ocean Sea, Viceroy and perpetual Governor — and the hereditary succession, as printed above. The date problem stands as flagged in §7: Columbus says "the same month of January"; the expulsion decree is dated March 31, 1492.)

Passage 2 — The sailing: Friday, August 3, 1492

Spanish (Las Casas text):

"Viernes 3 de agosto. — Partimos viernes 3 días de agosto de 1492 años de la barra de Saltes a las ocho horas."

English (literal rendering of the line above):

"Friday, 3 August. — We departed on Friday, the third day of August of the year 1492, from the bar of Saltes at eight o'clock."

This is the entry for the morning after the expulsion deadline expired (August 2) — the timing the whole debate turns on (§7).

Passage 3 — The Exodus comparison (September 1492)

Caught in a heavy swell that frightened the crew, Columbus — in Las Casas's abstract — compared his situation to the Exodus. Identity writers cite this passage: the Admiral reaching, in a moment of danger, for the Jews' escape from Egypt as his comparison.

Spanish (Las Casas text):

"Así que muy necesario me fue la mar alta, que no pareció salvo el tiempo de los judíos cuando salieron de Egipto contra Moisén, que los sacaba de cautiverio."

English (literal rendering):

"Thus the high sea was very necessary to me, the like of which had not appeared except in the time of the Jews when they came out of Egypt against Moses, who was leading them out of captivity."

What it proves is debated the same way as everything else in this file: a biblical comparison any Christian of 1492 might make — or, as the claimants read it, a telling choice of "we" moment. The words themselves are above; the reading is the reader's.

Passage 4 — Luis de Torres, the interpreter (November 2, 1492)

Spanish (Las Casas's abstract of the diary, describing the embassy sent inland in Cuba):

"…el otro era un Luys de Torres que avía bivido con el Adelantado de Murçia y avía sido judío y sabía dizque ebrayco y caldeo y aun algo arávigo."

English:

"…the other was a Luis de Torres who had lived with the Governor of Murcia and had been a Jew, and knew, it is said, Hebrew and Chaldean and some Arabic."

This is the diary's own testimony for §7's Torres account: the interpreter "had been a Jew" — avía sido judío — in the Admiral's own journal as Las Casas preserved it.
