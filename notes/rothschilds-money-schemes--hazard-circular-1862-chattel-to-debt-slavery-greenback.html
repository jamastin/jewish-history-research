The Hazard Circular (July 1862) — chattel slavery to debt slavery, and the greenback

Added to the Rothschilds Money Schemes search at James's request (2026-11-18): anything the bankers said about chattel slavery moving to debt slavery. This is the central document on that theme — and it also contains a banker's statement about the greenback, which is on James's list too.

What it is

A circular reportedly sent in July 1862 by British banking interests to their American banking counterparts, setting out the "European plan" for controlling labor through money once chattel slavery was abolished. It was quoted in Charles A. Lindbergh Sr., Banking and Currency and the Money Trust (Washington, D.C.: National Capital Press, 1913), page 102, and reprinted widely in money-reform literature ever since (e.g., Ellen Hodgson Brown, Web of Debt).

The circular's own words

"Slavery is likely to be abolished by the war power and chattel slavery destroyed. This, I and my European friends are glad of, for slavery is but the owning of labor and carries with it the care of the laborers, while the European plan, led by England, is that capital shall control labor by controlling wages. This can be done by controlling the money. The great debt that capitalists will see to it is made out of the war, must be used as a means to control the volume of money. To accomplish this, the bonds must be used as a banking basis. … It will not do to allow the greenback, as it is called, to circulate as money any length of time, as we cannot control that."

Why it fits this folder

  • Chattel → debt: the writer welcomes the end of chattel slavery because owning labor "carries with it the care of the laborers," while controlling wages through money controls labor without that cost.
  • The greenback: the greenback had to be stopped precisely because the bankers "cannot control that" — government-issued money outside their hands.
  • The war debt: the Civil War debt was to be made the basis of the banking system.

Honest note

The circular's text survives through Lindbergh's 1913 book and later reprints; no original 1862 copy is in an archive, and some historians question its authenticity. It is saved here as the document was quoted in print, which is what James asked to collect.

Sources: https://www.webofdebt.com/articles/debt-serfdom.php ; Lindbergh, Banking and Currency and the Money Trust (1913), p. 102.
