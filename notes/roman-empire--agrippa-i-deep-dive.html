Agrippa I — the father: last king of a united Judea

Researched 2026-11-19. Sources: Josephus (Antiquities 18–19), Acts 12, Britannica and standard summaries.

Who he was

  • Marcus Julius Agrippa (Agrippa I), c. 11/10 BC – AD 44. Grandson of Herod the Great through Aristobulus IV (whom Herod executed in 7 BC) and Berenice; brother of Herodias.
  • Raised in Rome as a companion of the imperial household — friends with Drusus, then with the future emperors Caligula and Claudius. That Roman upbringing, and those friendships, were his whole career.
  • A wild youth: spent fortunes, ran deep into debt, fled his creditors, at one low point nearly destitute in Idumea; later imprisoned by Tiberius for a careless remark wishing Caligula emperor — freed the day Caligula took the throne.

How he got the kingdom, piece by piece

  • AD 37: Caligula made him king over the dead Philip's northern tetrarchy (Batanea, Trachonitis, Gaulanitis/the Golan, Auranitis, Abila).
  • AD 39/40: Caligula exiled Herod Antipas and gave Agrippa his Galilee and Perea too.
  • The statue crisis (40): when Caligula ordered his statue set up in the Jerusalem Temple, Agrippa — at a banquet, at real personal risk — interceded and helped get the order stalled and dropped. It likely prevented a war a generation early.
  • AD 41: Caligula assassinated. Agrippa, in Rome, helped broker Claudius's acceptance as emperor — a kingmaker's moment. Claudius rewarded him with Judea and Samaria, the lands under Roman prefects since AD 6, plus consular honours; and gave Chalcis to Agrippa's brother Herod.
  • Result: for AD 41–44, Agrippa I ruled a kingdom nearly as large as Herod the Great's — Judea, Samaria, Idumea, Galilee, Perea, and the northern territories. The last time Judea had a Jewish king over the whole country.

His three years (41–44)

  • In Judea he played the observant Jewish king: lived in Jerusalem, kept the Law publicly, gave generously to the Temple, lowered taxes, and began strengthening Jerusalem's northern wall (Rome's governor of Syria made him stop).
  • Abroad he played the Roman grandee: lavish building and games in Beirut, Caesarea, Sebaste and elsewhere.
  • Acts 12 knows him simply as "King Herod": he executed the apostle, son of Zebedee, with the sword and imprisoned Peter (who escaped) — a persecution Josephus doesn't record but doesn't contradict.
  • He was also quietly building a regional bloc — he hosted five neighbouring client kings at Tiberias until the Roman governor of Syria pointedly broke the conference up. Rome watched him.

Death at Caesarea (AD 44)

  • At games in Caesarea honouring Claudius, wearing a robe of solid silver that flashed in the sun, the crowd shouted that his voice was "the voice of a god, and not of a man."
  • Acts 12:23: he did not give God the glory; he was struck down, "eaten by worms," and died.
  • Josephus (Antiquities 19.343–350) tells the same event independently: an owl (an omen he'd been warned about), violent abdominal pain beginning at that moment, carried to the palace, dead in five days, aged 54, in the seventh year of his reign.
  • The two accounts converging is one of the most-cited meetings of Acts and Josephus.

Why his kingdom died with him

  • His heir, Agrippa II, was 17 and in Rome; Claudius's freedmen and advisors argued a teenager couldn't hold a restless country, so Judea reverted to Roman procurators — the string of bad governors (Felix, Festus, Florus…) whose misrule led straight to 66. The three years of Agrippa I were the last glimpse of a united Jewish kingdom under Rome's umbrella.
