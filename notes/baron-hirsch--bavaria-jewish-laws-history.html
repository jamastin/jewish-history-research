When the Laws Against Jews Began in Bavaria — and What Led Up to Them

Timeline prepared for James, 2026-11-19. The laws Maurice de Hirsch lived under in the 1800s were the tail end of roughly 600 years of restrictions.

The two forces that built the system

The Church (the medieval Catholic Church — the papacy, its councils, bishops, and preachers): - 1215 — the Fourth Lateran Council (called by Pope Innocent III, held in Rome at the Lateran Palace) set the template for all of Europe: Canon 68 required Jews to be distinguished by their clothing; Jews could not hold public office or any position of power over Christians. - Church law also banned Christians from lending money at interest (usury bans, 1179 onward). Combined effect: Jews were pushed out of most work and pushed into moneylending, the one job Christians were forbidden to do. - Church teaching held Jews collectively responsible for the crucifixion; Pope Innocent III's doctrine of "perpetual servitude" framed Jewish subordination as divinely ordained.

The rulers (emperors, kings, dukes): - 1236 — Kammerknechtschaft ("chamber serfdom"). Emperor Frederick II declared the Jews servi camerae nostri — "serfs of our chamber." Jews legally belonged to the ruler's treasury: he protected them because they were profitable (taxed at will), but he could also pawn them, fine them, or seize their property. Bavarian dukes held the same power locally. - Jews could live somewhere only by buying a letter of protection (Schutzbrief) from the local ruler, paid for year by year — the origin of the later "protected Jew" (Schutzjude) system and of the special Jewish taxes (protection money, Leibzoll body toll, marriage and residence fees).

The practical bans that followed from this system: - Guilds required a Christian oath → Jews shut out of crafts and the trades. - Land holding required Christian oaths and feudal duties → land ownership and farming closed (the Hirsch family breaking this in 1818 was a first for Bavaria). - No weapons, no public office, no military service, no public baths (Munich), residence confined to a Jewish quarter/street in the early centuries.

Bavaria, step by step

  • 1229 — first authentic reference to a Jew of Munich (Abraham de Munichen, witness in Regensburg). Munich's Jews lived in their own quarter with a synagogue, ritual bath, and hospital.
  • 1276 — Duke Louis the Strict banished the Jews from Upper Bavaria (Jewish Encyclopedia, "Bavaria").
  • Oct 12, 1285 — the Munich blood libel. Jews accused of purchasing and murdering a Christian child; a mob attacked, the Jews fled into the synagogue, which was set on fire — about 180 perished in the flames (68 names recorded in the Nuremberg Memorbuch, 1296). King Rudolph's investigation found the accused innocent; the accusers were punished; the synagogue was rebuilt in 1287.
  • 1348–49 — the Black Death. Jews accused of poisoning wells; Munich's community was annihilated ("ceased to exist as such" — Jewish Encyclopedia).
  • Late 1300s — Emperor Wenceslaus's remission of debts owed to Jews wiped out Munich Jews' assets.
  • 1413 — host-desecration accusation against Munich Jews.
  • 1442 — expulsion from Upper Bavaria. The clergy stirred the populace; Duke Albrecht III ("the Pious") expelled the Jews from Munich and forty other places in Upper Bavaria. Their property was seized; the Munich synagogue was given to the duke's physician Johann Hartlieb and converted into a church (Marienkirche).
  • Oct 5, 1450 — expulsion from Lower Bavaria. The refugees from 1442 had been received by Duke Henry of Landshut, who hoped for revenue from them; his successor Ludwig IX first imprisoned them for four weeks and fined them 32,000 florins, then banished them outright. Preaching by Capistrano and the baptized Jew Peter Schwartz inflamed the Franconian bishoprics and free cities at the same time.
  • Dec 23, 1551 — the lasting expulsion. The estates demanded it; Albrecht V issued the edict. Josel of Rosheim, representing the German congregations, had to guarantee Jews would never again set foot in Upper or Lower Bavaria. The Upper Palatinate followed in 1555.
  • 1553 — Bavarian police law classes Jews among "dangerous, frivolous, and suspicious persons." Jews passing through had to buy a passport and pay toll, convoy money, and the humiliating Leibzoll (body toll).
  • 1566 — an imperial privilege declared contracts between Jews and Bavarian subjects void, ending trade relations.
  • March 12, 1715 — Elector Max Emanuel ordered the deportation of the few Jews still in the country; passports were recalled in 1733.
  • For roughly 300 years Jews were effectively banned from Munich and Old Bavaria — excluded from all trades, military service, and merchant guilds, denied the public baths, forbidden to settle. The exceptions, from the 1720s onward, were a handful of court Jews (e.g., Wolf Wertheimer, resident in Munich from 1729/1734); by 1750 court Jews and pass-holders were formally excepted from the ban. That exception is the direct line to the Hirsch family.
  • Meanwhile Jews survived under other rulers — Fürth, Sulzbach (whose duke invited Jews expelled from Vienna), Franconia — which is why Bavarian Jewry in Hirsch's day was mostly Franconian and rural.
  • 1785–1791 — Enlightenment-era drafts in Munich produced the first real improvement in Jewish status.
  • 1805 — the Munich Regulation for Jewry (basis of the 1813 Judenmatrikel): Jews could inherit domicile rights, hold services, live in all parts of the city.
  • 1813 — the Jewish Edict codified the old patchwork into one modern law, restrictions included (see bavaria-in-hirschs-time.md).
  • 1861 / 1868 / 1871 — Matrikel clause abolished 1861; remaining restrictions lifted 1868; full legal equality with the German Empire in 1871.

What led up to the laws — the four drivers

  1. Religious accusation — deicide teaching, then blood libel (from 1285 in Munich) and host-desecration charges (1413), spread by preachers.
  2. Debt resentment — expulsion cancelled what Christians owed Jewish lenders and wiped out claims (Wenceslaus's debt remission did the same by decree).
  3. The ruler's money interest — Jews were a taxable asset while tolerated, and a confiscation windfall when expelled (1442 property seizures; the 32,000-florin fine of 1450 before banishment).
  4. Guild protectionism — Christian craftsmen and merchants used the bans to keep out competition.
