Pope Clement VI and the Jews

Saved, 2026-11-19. Clement VI is the pope who, during the Black Death, publicly declared the well-poisoning accusation against the Jews to be false and tried to protect them. This file covers who he was, how he came to power, what his connection to the Jews actually was, and the exact words of his 1348 bulls.

Who he was

  • Born Pierre Roger in 1291 at the château of Maumont, in Limousin, south-central France — minor nobility.
  • Entered the Benedictine monastery of La Chaise-Dieu at age ten; studied theology in Paris; became a famous preacher and scholar.
  • Rise, helped by his patron Cardinal Pierre de Mortemart and closeness to the French crown: Abbot of Fécamp (1326); Bishop of Arras and Chancellor of France (1328); Archbishop of Sens (1329); Archbishop of Rouen (1330); created cardinal by Benedict XII on 18 December 1338.
  • Elected pope 7 May 1342: Benedict XII died 25 April 1342; the conclave at Avignon (18 of the 19 cardinals present) opened 5 May and reached agreement on the morning of 7 May — a two-day conclave. Crowned 19 May 1342 (Pentecost), taking the name Clement VI. He was the fourth Avignon pope (the papacy then resided at Avignon, France, not Rome).
  • Known for a lavish court, for appointing many cardinals from his native Limousin including relatives (his nephew Pierre Roger de Beaufort later became Pope Gregory XI), and for the bull Unigenitus (1343) on indulgences.
  • 9 June 1348: he bought the city of Avignon from Queen Joanna I of Naples for 80,000 gold florins, making it papal territory outright.
  • Survived the plague in Avignon; his physician Guy de Chauliac famously had him sit between two great fires to purify the air. Died 6 December 1352.

His connection to the Jews

Clement had no Jewish family or personal connection — he was a French Catholic churchman of noble birth. His connection was jurisdictional and fiscal:

  • The Jewish communities of Avignon and the papal territory in southern France (the Comtat Venaissin) lived under the pope's direct rule and were known as "the Pope's Jews" — protected by him, restricted by him, and paying their taxes to the papal treasury.
  • He stood in a long papal line: the bull Sicut Judaeis, first issued by Calixtus II in 1120 (after the Rhineland massacres of 1096) and rooted in Gregory the Great's letters, was reissued by pope after pope. It forbade Christians, on pain of excommunication, from killing or harming Jews, robbing them, or forcing them to convert. This was the Church's traditional doctrine — Jews were to be preserved (in Augustine's teaching, as witnesses), but kept subordinate. Protection, not equality.

The two bulls of 1348

  1. Early July 1348 (6 July in most accounts) — Clement reissued Sicut Judaeis, renewing the standing protection: no violence, no forced baptism.
  2. 26 September 1348 — Quamvis perfidiam — aimed directly at the well-poisoning accusation. Key words, as translated from the bull:

"…certain Christians, seduced by that liar, the devil, are imputing the pestilence to poisoning by Jews."

"It cannot be true that the Jews, by such a heinous crime, are the cause or occasion of the plague, because through many parts of the world the same plague, by the hidden judgment of God, has afflicted and afflicts the Jews themselves and many other races who have never lived alongside them."

  • The bull forbade killing, wounding, robbing, or forcibly baptizing Jews, threatened persecutors with excommunication, and urged clergy everywhere to protect Jews. It was distributed across Europe through the papal nuncios.
  • Clement's personal physician Guy de Chauliac supported him with evidence from his own treatment of the infected that the Jews were not to blame.
  • Clement also offered Avignon itself as a sanctuary: several thousand Jews fleeing the German pogroms are believed to have taken refuge in the papal city during 1348–1349, appearing in the Avignon registers as taxpayers and residents. In Avignon, under his direct power, the Jews were not harmed.
  • In October 1349 he also condemned the flagellants (bull Inter sollicitudines, 20 October 1349), whose movement was entangled with the violence of that year.

The limits — where his protection failed

  • The bulls were widely read and copied, but in the Holy Roman Empire papal authority was weak against autonomous cities, prince-bishops, and the Emperor. The Basel (January 1349), Strasbourg (February 1349), and Cologne (August 1349) massacres all happened despite the bulls.
  • Emperor Charles IV actively undercut him, decreeing that the property of Jews killed in riots was forfeit and effectively giving local authorities a financial incentive to turn a blind eye (in Nuremberg he had granted impunity in advance — see bavarian-state-library-finds.md in this folder).

Summary

No blood tie, no secret tie: Clement VI was the Jews' ruler and tax-lord in his own lands, heir to a centuries-old papal protection policy, and — when the plague accusation came — nearly the only major ruler in Europe who said in writing that the charge was a devil's lie, and backed it with sanctuary where his power ran.
