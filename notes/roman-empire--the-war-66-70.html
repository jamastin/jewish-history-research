The War, AD 66–70 — how it started and how the Temple fell

Written 2026-11-19, continuing the Rome folder walk at James's request. Main source: Josephus, The Jewish War (he lived it — first as a rebel commander in Galilee, then as a prisoner, then on the Roman side). Modern historians treat his numbers with caution; flagged where relevant.

The spark (AD 66)

  • The procurator Gessius Florus took 17 talents of gold from the Temple treasury "for Caesar." Jerusalem mocked him with a street collection "for poor Florus"; he answered by loosing his soldiers on the city — thousands killed (Berenice's barefoot plea happened in this, see berenice-deep-dive.md).
  • The daily sacrifice for the emperor in the Temple was stopped — a formal act of rebellion, pushed through by Eleazar, captain of the Temple, against the chief priests' protests.
  • Rebel forces seized Masada and massacred its Roman garrison; the Roman garrison in Jerusalem surrendered on terms and was massacred anyway.
  • Cestius Gallus, governor of Syria, marched on Jerusalem with a legion — then, bafflingly, withdrew, and was ambushed and mauled in the pass of Beth-Horon (Nov. 66), losing ~5,300 infantry and 480 cavalry (Josephus's figures). After Beth-Horon there was no going back: Jerusalem formed a revolutionary government and began minting its own silver coins — "Shekel of Israel, Year One."

Vespasian in Galilee (67–68)

  • Nero sent Vespasian, a blunt, capable soldier, with three legions (~60,000 men with auxiliaries and allied kings' troops — Agrippa II among them).
  • Galilee fell piece by piece: Jotapata after a 47-day siege — its commander, Josephus, survived a suicide pact and surrendered, then saved his life by "prophesying" Vespasian would become emperor. Gamala fell after a bloody fight (Agrippa II was wounded there by a sling stone). By the end of 68 the north was Roman; the rebels' best fighters fled into Jerusalem — which made the city stronger and far more divided.

Rome falls apart; Titus takes over (68–70)

  • Nero died in 68; Rome tore through four emperors in a year. In July 69 the eastern legions proclaimed Vespasian emperor — the bid Berenice had bankrolled. He left for Rome, and his son Titus took command of the Jewish war with fresh reinforcements.
  • Inside Jerusalem, three factions fought a civil war of their own — John of Gischala, Simon bar Giora, and Eleazar's party — and in the fighting they burned the city's stored grain, the food that could have fed a long siege. Josephus says this, more than Rome, doomed the city.

The siege and the burning (70)

  • Titus arrived before Jerusalem at Passover, with the city swollen with pilgrims, and built siege works and a wall of circumvallation around it. Famine inside became horrific — Josephus's accounts of it are the most harrowing pages in his history.
  • The Romans took the outer walls one by one through the summer. On the 9th of Av (late July/early August 70) — the same date in Jewish tradition as the Babylonian destruction of the First Temple — the Temple burned. Josephus insists Titus ordered it spared and a soldier threw the torch; most modern readers doubt Titus's innocence, but the disagreement is ancient and unresolved.
  • The Upper City fell a month later. Simon bar Giora was captured alive, kept for Titus's triumph in Rome (71), and executed there at the end of the procession. The Temple's treasures — the great menorah, the showbread table, silver trumpets — were paraded through Rome; the carving of that procession still stands on the Arch of Titus in the Roman Forum.
  • Josephus claims 1.1 million died in the siege and 97,000 were captured. Modern historians consider those numbers far too high for the city's real population — but even the sober estimates describe a demographic catastrophe, mass enslavement, and captives sent to mines and arenas.

What remained

  • A few fortress holdouts survived the fall — Herodium, Machaerus, and Masada, which held out until 73/74.
  • Judea was left with a permanent legion camped in Jerusalem's ruins (Legio X Fretensis), the Temple tax redirected to Rome as the Fiscus Judaicus, and no Temple — Judaism had to rebuild itself around synagogue, prayer and study instead of sacrifice. That rebuilding is the seed of everything in this project that comes after.
