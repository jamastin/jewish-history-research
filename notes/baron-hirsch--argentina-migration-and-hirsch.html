Argentina — the migration, and Baron Hirsch's money behind it

Written 2026-11-22 at James's request: "for the Argentina section make sure you include the migrating there — they started migrating there quite a while before [1919]. Check into Hirsch — I believe he was funding some migration over to Argentina."

James is right on both points. Jews were arriving in Argentina decades before the January 1919 riots in his album item M098, and from 1891 the biggest single funder of the migration was Baron Maurice de Hirsch, through the Jewish Colonization Association (JCA). But the organized migration did not start with Hirsch — it started two years before his association existed, with the immigrants themselves. This file builds on the other files in this folder (the four colonies, the colonies' daily life, the Chronicling America finds) and does not repeat their full text.

A note on the numbers: the sources do not all agree, and where they disagree the disagreement is shown. Figures labelled "estimate" are estimates in the sources themselves.

1. Before Hirsch — the migration was already under way

The first trickle (1840s–1880s). The foundations of Jewish life in Argentina were laid by immigrants from Western Europe — German and French families arriving from the 1840s and 1860s onward. The markers the record gives:

  • 1860 — the first Jewish wedding recorded in Buenos Aires.
  • 1862 — a minyan meeting for the High Holidays in Buenos Aires developed into the Congregación Israelita (formally the Congregación Israelita de la República Argentina, CIRA, by 1868) — the first organized Jewish congregation in the country.
  • Argentina's 1853 constitution guaranteed religious freedom, and the country actively recruited immigrants to populate its empty land.
  • August 6, 1881 — after the pogroms in Russia, President Julio Argentino Roca signed a decree to promote Russian Jewish immigration to Argentina, and the government sent an emissary to invite Jews from the Tsar's empire.
  • The first Sephardim arrived in the early 1880s, from Tetuán and Tangier in Morocco.
  • The 1887 census of Buenos Aires counted only 366 Jews; the Encyclopaedia Judaica estimates that by 1889 there were between 1,500 and 2,000 Jews in the whole Argentine Republic (estimate, not a count).
  • 1888 — a first small organized group, eight Russian Jewish families, reached Argentina with the help of the Alliance Israélite Universelle in Paris.

(Sources: Encyclopaedia Judaica, "Argentina — Early Jewish Life: 1840–1890," via encyclopedia.com; Jewish Virtual Library, "Argentina Virtual Jewish History Tour"; Avotaynu, Paul Armony, "Immigrants to Argentina Listed as Baron de Hirsch Colonists.")

The organized start — the Weser, August 1889. The event Argentine Jewish history treats as its founding moment came before any Hirsch money:

  • On August 14, 1889, the steamship Weser (also spelled Wesser in some sources) docked in Buenos Aires carrying a group of Russian Jews from Kamenetz-Podolsk in Podolia, fleeing the pogroms. The sources give the group's size slightly differently: 824 (Wikipedia / Jewish Virtual Library), 820 (Encyclopaedia Judaica; the Tandfonline scholarly article by Raanan Rein and others), 813 individuals in 120 families (Avotaynu's account of the passenger lists), and 136 families (Encyclopaedia Judaica's agricultural-settlement section). All agree on the ship, the date, and the rough size: about 800 people.
  • They had organized themselves — they had sent a delegation to Paris, travelled via Berlin and Hamburg to Bremen, and come out to buy land and farm. Nobody's charity sent them.
  • The welcome was bad: the land agent Pedro Palacios failed them, families were stranded, children died of typhus in the first weeks, and the dead were buried in makeshift coffins (moved in September 1891 to the new Moisés Ville cemetery, the first Jewish cemetery in Argentina). A German Jewish scientist in government service, Dr. Wilhelm Löwenthal, intervened for them.
  • About 40 of the families acquired land from Pedro Palacios near the railway station of the same name in Santa Fe province and founded Moïseville (Moisés Ville) in 1889 — named, by the settlers' own account, for Moses and the Exodus. It was a self-founded colony: the settlers bought the land themselves, on credit, before the JCA existed.

That is the sequence James asked to have straight: the migration and the first colony came first (1889); Hirsch's organization arrived two years later (1891) and took the struggling colonies under its wing — Moïseville was "reorganized by the JCA in 1891" (see the-four-colonies-identified.md).

2. Hirsch's role — the Jewish Colonization Association, verified

James's belief checks out, and the paper trail is in his own files.

The organization. In 1891 Hirsch founded the Jewish Colonization Association (JCA) in London — at the time, by one historian's account, the largest charitable organization in the world. From hirsch-beginning-to-end.md in this folder:

  • Founded with £2,000,000 capital; Hirsch added £7,000,000 more in 1892.
  • Of the JCA's 20,000 shares, Hirsch personally held 19,991 — it was his money and his instrument.
  • His widow Clara's will later added 10,000,000 francs to the JCA, and on her death in 1899 the association's capital rose to £11,000,000.
  • Separately, also in 1891, he set up the Baron de Hirsch Fund in the United States with $2,500,000 for Jewish immigrants from Russia and Romania — the American papers in James's finds print the U.S. fund as $2,500,000 (New-York Tribune, Feb. 8, 1891; The Journal, Apr. 22, 1896) and one paper as $2,400,000 (Foster's Daily Democrat, Apr. 22, 1896). Each is quoted as printed in chronicling-america-finds.md; the papers do not agree, and this file does not pretend they do.

What he intended. Hirsch's own statement of purpose, in the North American Review, July 1891: to give his "companions in faith the possibility of finding a new existence, primarily as farmers and also as handicraftsmen." The plan was to move Russian Jews out of the Pale of Settlement — where they were largely forbidden to own land — and resettle them as farmers on land of their own overseas, above all in Argentina. The scale he talked about was enormous: the Portland Daily Press (July 30, 1892, reprinted from the New York Sun — in James's finds) described a proposed $100,000,000 scheme for the organized emigration of 3,500,000 Jews. That was a reported proposal, not what was achieved — the achieved figures are in section 3 below. The Journal (Apr. 22, 1896) reported that "The Russian Government in 1892 agreed to the scheme of emigration of the Jews to the Argentine and Canada and other countries, the Baron guaranteeing to expend $10,000,000 on the work."

How the funding worked. The JCA was not a ticket charity; it was a land-and-loan machine:

  • It bought land in bulk in Argentina. In August 1891, by Hirsch's direction, a vast tract was bought for $1,300,000 (£260,000) (Jewish Encyclopedia, via colonies-daily-life-1891-1900.md). By the Baron's death in 1896 the JCA owned 302,736 hectares in Buenos Aires, Entre Ríos, and Santa Fe provinces; by the eve of the First World War, 586,473 hectares (Encyclopaedia Judaica).
  • Committees in Russia selected the colonist families; the JCA organized and subsidized their transport.
  • Each settler family received a farm — the American papers in James's finds put it at about 185 acres per family (Bill Barlow's Budget, May 15, 1895; Daily Morning Journal and Courier, Oct. 1, 1895, which adds "on payment of $165") — bought from the association on long-term credit, repayable over years, with the JCA supplying tools, seed, livestock, schools, synagogues, mills, and a medical staff, and running the colonies through its own administrators.
  • Hirsch described the operation himself in an 1892 interview (People's Voice, Wellington, Kan., Sept. 23, 1892 — James's extended finds): "he had sent 6,000 Jews there and had negotiated for the purchase of 7,000,000 acres of land, but the government would grant only 5,000,000."
  • The honest failure side is also in James's files: the New-York Tribune's front page of August 7, 1892 — "BARON HIRSCH'S COLONY A FAILURE" — reporting Moïseville's first collapse, families "huddled together in tents and sheds," and 800 colonists sailing back to Europe (see chronicling-america-finds-colonies-extended.md). Theodore Norman's history of the JCA (An Outstretched Arm, 1983) records the attrition plainly: of every two families brought to Argentina, roughly one stayed on the farm; of 1,550 families settled in Clara and San Antonio by 1901, 841 had left and 709 stayed.

3. The colonies and the numbers

The colonies. The four in place by the JCA's 1894 report — identified in the-four-colonies-identified.md — are:

  1. Moïseville (Moisés Ville), Santa Fe — founded 1889 by the Weser immigrants themselves; reorganized by the JCA 1891; the oldest.
  2. Mauricio, Buenos Aires province — set apart by the JCA in 1891, named for the Baron; about 62,000 acres (25,000 hectares).
  3. Clara, Entre Ríos — established 1894, named for Baroness Clara; its main body was organized in Russia in 1894 in ten groups of about forty families each — about 400 families in one year. The steamship Pampa had already brought Jewish families out in December 1891 who were settled in the Villaguay–Basavilbaso country that became Clara's villages (see colonies-daily-life-1891-1900.md).
  4. Lucienville, Entre Ríos, at Basavilbaso — founded October 1893, named for the Baron's dead son Lucien.

The record supports more colonies after the first four, among them San Antonio (Entre Ríos — named in Norman's attrition figures above), Rivera and Lapin (Buenos Aires province), Villa Domínguez, San Gregorio, Carmel, Ingeniero Sajaroff, and Villaguay (Entre Ríos), and Montefiore (Santa Fe, founded 1912). One scholarly summary credits the JCA with founding 26 agricultural colonies in Argentina in all (Rein et al., Jewish Social Studies/Tandfonline, 2023).

How many people. Keep the two different counts separate — colony settlers, and all Jewish immigration:

  • On the colonies: at Hirsch's death in 1896, 910 families — 6,757 persons — were settled on JCA land (Encyclopaedia Judaica). The number of persons settled on the land reached 18,900 by the First World War era, peaking at 20,382 persons in 1925.
  • Moved under JCA auspices: historian Carl Solberg's figure, quoted in Jewish Social Studies (2020): before 1914, about 25,000 Jews moved to Argentina under the auspices of the JCA and its colonies, and another 25,000 on their own, mainly settling in Buenos Aires.
  • All Jewish immigration: the JCA's own statistics (printed in the NBER volume International Migrations, 1931) count 118,213 Jewish immigrants entering Argentina in 1904–1924 alone; another scholarly count puts total Jewish emigration to Argentina from 1881 to 1914 at 113,000, mainly Russian Jews. In the peak years 1906–1912, about 13,000 Jews a year were arriving.
  • Argentina's Jewish population: 6,085 counted in the census of 1895 (often rounded to 6,000); an estimated 100,000–117,000 in 1914 (Encyclopaedia Judaica table); 125,000 in 1919, of whom 80% lived in cities by then (Encyclopaedia Judaica, "Argentina"); the Jewish Virtual Library rounds it as "by 1920, more than 150,000 Jews lived in Argentina" (estimate). The American Jewish Year Book figure for 1917 was 110,000 for the republic.

So by January 1919 Argentina held one of the largest Jewish populations in the world outside Europe and the United States — roughly 125,000 people, most of them immigrants of the previous thirty years or their children, and most of them by then in the cities, above all Buenos Aires.

4. The Buenos Aires end — January 1919 and album item M098

James's album item M098 (filed under russia-pogroms-revolution) is a cropped American newspaper block: "BOLSHEVIK RIOTS IN ARGENTINA" — "BATTLES BETWEEN STRIKERS AND POLICE RAGING IN BUENOS AIRES" — "10,000 MARINES LANDED." The event behind those headlines is the Semana Trágica — the Tragic Week, January 7–14, 1919. (M098's exact source paper is still being hunted; nothing here guesses it.)

What happened. A strike at the Vasena metal works in Buenos Aires turned violent on January 7, 1919 — police killed five workers and wounded about twenty. The funeral on January 9 became a general strike and street fighting. The army under General Luis Dellepiane, with naval infantry (marines), restored order by force; martial law followed. The total death toll is genuinely disputed: published estimates run from 77 to about 700 (some accounts say 700–800, with 2,000–4,000 injured). One caution on James's clipping itself: the record shows about 500 Argentine marines landed to reinforce Dellepiane — the "10,000 MARINES" of the headline is American-paper exaggeration, roughly twenty times the documented figure.

The pogrom inside the strike. The repression did not stop at strikers. Right-wing civilian vigilantes — the movement that organized itself that week as the Argentine Patriotic League (Liga Patriótica Argentina), working alongside police — turned on the city's Russian Jews, who were collectively accused of plotting a Bolshevik revolution in the wake of the Russian Revolution:

  • The hunt was called "the Chase of the Russians" (la caza de rusos — in Buenos Aires slang, ruso meant a Jew). Mobs used the slogan "Death to the Jews."
  • The Jewish neighborhoods of Once and Villa Crespo were invaded: Jews were dragged from their homes, beaten, shot, and killed; homes, synagogues, libraries, and cooperatives were wrecked and burned, books and furniture burned in the street. Victor Mirelman's scholarly study ("The Semana Trágica of 1919 and the Jews in Argentina," Jewish Social Studies, 1975) — the source the modern accounts rest on — records that some Jews escaped the mobs by pleading they were Italians.
  • Police announced they had raided an apartment where 40 Russian Jews were supposedly sitting as a revolutionary "soviet." Named Jewish and Russian men — among them Germán Boris Wladimirovich, Gregorio Petroff, and Moisés Scutz — were accused of organizing a Communist revolution. The Jewish immigrant Pinie Wald was arrested, falsely accused of leading the supposed Judeo-communist plot, and tortured; he later told it in Yiddish in his book Koschmar ("Nightmare," published 1929; Spanish translation 1987).
  • Wikipedia's summary of the toll, citing Mirelman, lists 100+ Jewish dead among the week's victims; other totals vary as wildly as the overall count, and this file does not pick one.

The "only pogrom in the Americas" line — stated carefully. The Tragic Week is very often described as the only pogrom ever to take place in the Americas (or "the first and only pogrom in the Americas" / "the only pogrom in the Western Hemisphere"). That exact characterization appears in popular and some scholarly writing — e.g., Marcelo Brodsky's essay in LitHub ("it is the only pogrom ever to take place in the Americas") and historian Jonathan Ablard in A Contracorriente (2025: "the only pogrom in the Western Hemisphere"). What can be said on the record: it is the one event in the Americas that historians and reference works routinely single out and name as a pogrom — an organized anti-Jewish riot with killings, carried out with police complicity. The phrase is a characterization repeated by those sources, not a statistic, and it is recorded here as their claim.

Why Buenos Aires had Jews to attack. This is where the two halves of this file join. The crowd the vigilantes hunted in Once and Villa Crespo existed because of the migration above: three decades of arrivals — the Weser group of 1889, the JCA's organized transports, and tens of thousands who came on their own — had built a Buenos Aires Jewish community of peddlers, artisans, factory workers, shopkeepers, Yiddish newspapers (about 40 Jewish periodicals existed in Argentina by 1914), theatres, and unions. The colonists' children were also drifting from the farms to the capital. By 1919 four out of five Argentine Jews were city people. The "Bolshevik" of the American headlines and the Jewish farmer of the Baron's colonies were, in many families, the same people's two generations.
