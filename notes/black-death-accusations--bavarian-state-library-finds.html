From the Bavarian State Library (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Munich)

Search done at James's request, 2026-11-19. The Bavarian State Library (BSB) is Bavaria's state library in Munich. It publishes the Historical Lexicon of Bavaria (Historisches Lexikon Bayerns) and runs the digital collections at digitale-sammlungen.de, where many of the items below can be read page by page. The key article used here is Josef Kirmeier, "Judenverfolgungen (Spätmittelalter)" [Persecutions of the Jews (Late Middle Ages)], Historical Lexicon of Bavaria, published 24 Sept 2014: https://www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de/Lexikon/Judenverfolgungen_(Spätmittelalter)

German passages are quoted exactly, each followed by my English translation.

1. How the accusation worked — the wave ran ahead of the disease

"Die Pest wütete 1348 erstmals in Europa und brachte für mindestens ein Drittel der Bevölkerung den Tod. Die bakteriellen Erreger der Krankheit wie auch deren Verbreitung über die Flöhe der Ratten waren unbekannt. Schnell entstand der Vorwurf, Juden hätten, als Feinde der Christen, Wasser und Brunnen vergiftet. So eilte bald der Krankheit eine fast alle jüdischen Gemeinden betreffende Verfolgungswelle voraus."

Translation: "The plague raged in Europe for the first time in 1348 and brought death to at least a third of the population. The bacterial agents of the disease, and its spread through the fleas of rats, were unknown. The accusation quickly arose that the Jews, as enemies of the Christians, had poisoned water and wells. And so a wave of persecution, affecting almost all Jewish communities, soon ran ahead of the disease itself."

2. Bavarian towns in 1348–1349

"In allen schwäbischen und fränkischen Städten, in denen nach den Verfolgungen von 1336 und 1338 wieder Juden lebten, kam es zu Verfolgungen, so auch in Augsburg, wo bereits am 22. November 1348 über 100 Juden ermordet worden waren, und am 21. April 1349 in Würzburg."

Translation: "In all the Swabian and Franconian towns where Jews were living again after the persecutions of 1336 and 1338, there were persecutions — including Augsburg, where more than 100 Jews had already been murdered on 22 November 1348, and on 21 April 1349 in Würzburg."

On Würzburg, the Lexicon's picture caption (from the Bishop's Chronicle of Lorenz Fries, Würzburg University Library) adds that on 21 April 1349 the Würzburg Jews, in despair, are said to have burned themselves in their own houses to forestall a pogrom. (The House of Bavarian History calls the Würzburg event the "Weinrebenpogrom" — the vine pogrom — after crop failures from a frost, and a flagellant procession passing through.)

3. Nuremberg, 6 December 1349 — arranged in advance

"Die Morde an der Nürnberger Gemeinde, bei der vermutlich 562 Menschen starben, erfolgten am 6. Dezember 1349. Der Nürnberger Rat unter Führung des Patriziers Ulrich Stromer (gest. 1385) hatte bereits zwei Monate vor dem Pogrom bei Kaiser Karl IV. (reg. 1346-1378) dessen Erlaubnis als Schutzherr der jüdischen Gemeinde eingeholt. In der berühmten Markturkunde wurde bestimmt, die unmittelbar am Rathaus gelegene jüdische Siedlung abzureißen, um hier den Hauptmarkt zu errichten und an Stelle der Synagoge die heutige katholische Pfarrkirche 'Unserer lieben Frau' zu erbauen."

Translation: "The murders of the Nuremberg community, in which probably 562 people died, took place on 6 December 1349. The Nuremberg council, led by the patrician Ulrich Stromer (died 1385), had obtained the permission of Emperor Charles IV, as the Jewish community's lord protector, two months before the pogrom. In the famous Market Charter it was decreed that the Jewish settlement, directly beside the town hall, be demolished, to build the main market square (Hauptmarkt) there, and to build on the site of the synagogue the Catholic parish church of 'Our Dear Lady' that stands there today" — the Frauenkirche.

"Auch die Verteilung des jüdischen Besitzes und Außenstände wurden in Nürnberg bereits im Vorfeld geregelt. Ulrich Stromer wurde 'um seiner Treue und Dienste willen' als Anführer des Rates ein jüdisches Haus geschenkt. Der jüdische Besitz und auch die Forderungen der Juden fielen an die Stadt. Nürnberger Bürgern wurden die Schulden bei den jüdischen Gläubigern erlassen und der Bamberger Bischof und der Burggraf mit Hausschenkungen wurden mit höheren Geldsummen beteiligt."

Translation: "The distribution of the Jewish property and outstanding debts was likewise arranged in Nuremberg in advance. Ulrich Stromer, as leader of the council, was given a Jewish house 'for the sake of his loyalty and services.' The Jewish property, and also the Jews' outstanding claims, fell to the city. Nuremberg citizens had their debts to Jewish creditors cancelled, and the Bishop of Bamberg and the Burgrave were given shares in the form of houses and larger sums of money."

The Lexicon notes the same pattern elsewhere: as in Würzburg, and later at the expulsions from Munich, Landshut, Bamberg, and Passau, a church was immediately set on the site of the destroyed synagogue — reclaiming the ground for the Church and permanently preventing the Jews' return to that place.

4. The Bavarian duchies, 1349 — the Landshut murder charter

"Auch die Verfolgungen in den Herzogtümern Bayerns liefen 1349 organisiert und mit ausdrücklicher Genehmigung der bayerischen Herzöge ab. Die gerade wieder mit großzügigen Privilegien in die Städte gelockten Juden wurden in der Zeit der Pestepidemie wiederum Opfer der Gewalt. Bereits bevor die Pest in Bayern ankam, wurden die Landshuter Juden am 17. Februar 1349 ermordet. An diesem Tag stellte Herzog Stephan II. (reg. 1347-1375) wiederum für Albrecht von Staudach eine Urkunde aus, in der er die jüdische Gemeinde von Landshut für 600 Pfund Regensburger Pfennige und die Unkosten, die er mit seinem Gefolge zur Fastnacht haben würde, an diesen verpfändete. Aus dem Text der Urkunde geht dabei eindeutig hervor, dass es sich nicht um eine übliche Verpfändung sondern um einen klaren Auftrag zum Mord handelte. Insbesondere sollten auch alle Pfand- und Schuldbriefe eingezogen und so wie der gesamte weitere Besitz der Juden mit dem Herzog abgerechnet werden. Wenig später wurde Albrecht von Staudach auch mit der Eintreibung der gesamten jüdischen Habe in München und auch in allen anderen bayerischen Städten beauftragt. Verschont blieb die Regensburger Gemeinde. Teile der Regensburger Bürgerschaft, denen die Einnahmen aus der Judensteuer verpfändet waren, stellten sich ausdrücklich vor die jüdische Gemeinde und verteidigten diese auch gegen Übergriffe der bayerischen Herzöge."

Translation: "The persecutions in the duchies of Bavaria in 1349 likewise proceeded in an organized fashion and with the express permission of the Bavarian dukes. The Jews, who had just been lured back into the towns with generous privileges, again became victims of violence in the time of the plague epidemic. Even before the plague arrived in Bavaria, the Jews of Landshut were murdered, on 17 February 1349. On that day Duke Stephan II (reigned 1347–1375) issued a charter to Albrecht von Staudach, in which he pledged the Jewish community of Landshut to him for 600 pounds of Regensburg pennies, plus the expenses the duke would have with his retinue at Carnival. From the text of the charter it emerges unambiguously that this was not an ordinary pledging, but a clear commission to murder. In particular, all pledge-letters and debt instruments were to be confiscated, and, like all the rest of the Jews' property, settled with the duke. A little later Albrecht von Staudach was also commissioned with collecting all Jewish property in Munich and in all other Bavarian towns. The Regensburg community was spared. Parts of the Regensburg citizenry, to whom the revenues from the Jewish tax had been pledged, expressly placed themselves in front of the Jewish community and defended it even against attacks by the Bavarian dukes."

5. The same official did the same job in 1338 — Deggendorf

The same Lexicon article shows this machinery already existed eleven years before the plague, in the host-desecration pogrom that began at Deggendorf on 30 September 1338 and hit more than 20 Lower Bavarian towns (Braunau, Cham, Dingolfing, Dorfen, Eggenfelden, Erding, Kelheim, Kraiburg, Landau, Landshut, Massing, Moosburg, Neumarkt-St. Veit, Pfarrkirchen, Pfeffenhausen, Straubing, Velden, Vilsbiburg, Vilshofen, and more — for many of these towns the Nuremberg Memorbuch's list is the only evidence a Jewish community ever existed there):

"Kurz darauf griff der niederbayerische Herzog Heinrich XIV. (reg. 1310-1339) ein, aber nicht als Schutzherr seiner Juden, sondern an der Seite der Mörder. Er sprach die Mörder frei, schenkte ihnen das gesamte Raubgut und deklarierte alle jüdischen Pfandbriefe als ungültig. … In Landshut rechnete der Herzog dann die Einnahmen aus dem Mord an den dortigen Juden direkt ab: Der herzogliche Beamte und Geldgeber des Herzogs, Albrecht von Staudach, wurde hier von Heinrich beauftragt, den Besitz der ermordeten Juden zu sammeln und der herzoglichen Kasse zuzuführen."

Translation: "Shortly afterwards the Lower Bavarian Duke Heinrich XIV (reigned 1310–1339) intervened — not as protector of his Jews, but on the side of the murderers. He acquitted the murderers, gave them all the loot as a gift, and declared all Jewish promissory notes invalid. … In Landshut the duke then settled up the revenues from the murder of the Jews there directly: the ducal official and the duke's own financier, Albrecht von Staudach, was commissioned by Heinrich to gather the property of the murdered Jews and deliver it to the ducal treasury."

The Lexicon calls the 1338/39 Bavarian pogroms a novelty in one respect: the persecutions had the express support of the lord protector, who was himself responsible for their extent. A few years later, Jews were taken back into some of the same towns under new ducal privileges — the same Jews who were then murdered in 1349. Note that the collector in both 1338 and 1349 is the same man, Albrecht von Staudach.

6. The historians' verdict (the Lexicon's research summary)

"Es zeichnet sich klar ab, dass die Ansiedlung, Duldung und Ermordung bzw. Vertreibung der Juden vor allem wirtschaftlich motiviert war."

Translation: "It is becoming clear that the settlement, toleration, and murder or expulsion of the Jews were motivated above all economically."

The House of Bavarian History (Haus der Bayerischen Geschichte, hdbg.eu) says the same of 1348/49 specifically: many Bavarian towns were not affected by the plague at all at the time of their pogroms; alongside the old prejudices, indebtedness to Jewish moneylenders was a weighty motive for the persecutions and murders.

7. What the library itself holds (BSB treasures named in the article)

  • The Schedel World Chronicle (Nuremberg Chronicle), Nuremberg 1493 — BSB shelfmark Rar 287, fol. 230v — contains a famous woodcut image of a Jewish pogrom; its text alludes to the Deggendorf pogrom of 1338. Digitized in the BSB's digital collections.
  • The Deggendorf host-desecration cycle — a 1716 Regensburg print, Warhaffte History, Was sich mit dem Hochwürdigisten Sacrament des Altars zu Deggendorff, durch unmenschliche geübte Boßheit der Juden, verloffen ["True History of what transpired with the Most Reverend Sacrament of the Altar at Deggendorf, through the inhumanly practised wickedness of the Jews"], p. 15, held by the BSB and digitized (www.digitale-sammlungen.de). A matching 14-panel picture cycle explaining the "Deggendorfer Gnad" pilgrimage hung in Deggendorf's Church of the Holy Sepulchre from 1710 to 1969. (The pilgrimage itself continued in weakened form until 1992.)
  • Alois Schmid, "Die Judenpolitik der Reichsstadt Regensburg im Jahre 1349" [The Jewish policy of the imperial city of Regensburg in the year 1349], Zeitschrift für bayerische Landesgeschichte 43 (1980), 589–612 — the scholarly study of the one Bavarian community that was spared; digitized by the BSB: https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00009447?page=598,599
  • Hebrew manuscripts from a synagogue destroyed in 1349 — the BSB's Hebrew collection includes 8 Hebrew liturgical manuscripts (Pentateuchs and prayer books) collected by Hartmann Schedel, which he acquired in 1504; they had been found in the Bamberg synagogue that was destroyed in 1349. (Aliza Cohen-Mushlin, "Hebrew Manuscripts in the Bavarian State Library Munich," lecture, Hamburg 2014.)

8. The flagellants in Bavaria (companion Lexicon article)

The Lexicon's article "Geißlerzüge" (Flagellant processions), also BSB-published, records flagellant groups in Bavaria attested at the monasteries of Ensdorf, Heilsbronn, and Engelthal, and in (or probably in) Nuremberg, Augsburg, Regensburg, Eichstätt, and Würzburg, with the peak in spring and summer 1349. It cautions that modern scholarship doubts the flagellants directly carried out the pogroms: in some towns the events coincided, and some sources accuse the flagellants of calling for the murder of Jews, but on closer inspection those accusations look like city chroniclers shifting blame away from their own citizens — as in a 16th-century Frankfurt chronicle fragment.
