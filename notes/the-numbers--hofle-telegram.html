The Höfle Telegram (January 11, 1943)

What it is: a secret SS radio message sent on January 11, 1943 by SS-Sturmbannführer Hermann Höfle, chief of staff of Operation Reinhard (the program that ran the Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka killing centers), reporting "recorded arrivals" at the Reinhard camps through December 31, 1942. The SS sent it by Enigma radio. British codebreakers intercepted and decoded it; the SS never knew. The intercept sat in British archives, was declassified in the 1990s, and was recognized by researchers Peter Witte and Stephen Tyas in 2000–2001. It is held by the UK National Archives under reference HW 16/23.

Image file — the decoded telegram as filed in the British National Archives (via Wikimedia Commons).

What it records

Arrivals in the last two weeks of 1942, and cumulative arrivals through December 31, 1942. The camps are identified by single letters:

Letter Camp Last 2 weeks of 1942 Total through Dec 31, 1942
L Lublin (Majdanek) 12,761 24,733
B Bełżec 0 434,508
S Sobibór 515 101,370
T Treblinka 10,335 713,555
Total 23,611 1,274,166

One honest wrinkle, stated plainly: in the surviving British decode, the Treblinka cumulative figure is typed as "71,355" — an obvious dropped digit in transmission or transcription. Only 713,555 makes the four camp figures add up to the telegram's own stated total of 1,274,166 — and that total matches the Korherr Report's figure for the same camps exactly (see korherr-report.md). That is why every serious source reads it as 713,555.

Why this document is so hard to argue with

  • It was never meant for any audience outside the SS — it is a routine progress report from the man running the operation to his superiors (one copy addressed to Eichmann's office in Berlin, one to the Security Police commander in Kraków).
  • It survived only because the British intercepted it. The SS's own copies of the Operation Reinhard records were destroyed (see the-full-picture.md, "What was destroyed").
  • "Arrivals" at Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka meant death: these were not labor camps with large registered populations. Bełżec, for example, is known to have had only a handful of survivors — against 434,508 recorded arrivals in 1942 alone.
  • It covers only 1942. Treblinka and Sobibór kept operating into 1943, which is why final camp totals are higher than the telegram's figures (Treblinka's total is estimated at roughly 870,000–925,000).
