Books Hitler read / that influenced him — evidence-graded

Researched 2026-11-19. Rule used throughout: only items with real evidence are listed as influences. Every entry carries an evidence grade:

  • STRONG — his own surviving copy with his markings (marginalia), or a borrowing record, or he names/praises it in Mein Kampf or speeches.
  • MEDIUM — documented ownership, documented personal contact, or repeated contemporary reports — but no surviving marked-up copy or direct statement.
  • WEAK / MYTH — claimed by later writers; little or no documentary evidence. Listed only to be marked as such.

The evidence base (read this first)

  • Hitler owned an estimated 16,000 volumes by 1945 (estimate by US journalist Frederick Oechsner, 1942; Baldur von Schirach claimed ~6,000 — no exact count exists).
  • About 1,200 volumes survive at the Library of Congress (Rare Book and Special Collections Division), as part of the Third Reich Collection: books from the Reich Chancellery library and the private collections of Hitler and other Nazi leaders, found in a salt mine near Berchtesgaden, screened by the US Army Document Center in Munich, shipped to the Library of Congress in 1946. LOC's own description: https://www.loc.gov/research-centers/rare-book-and-special-collections/collections/selected-special-collections/ (Third Reich Collection: 1,019 titles; "a large number of the 1,200 volumes transferred… are official presentation copies which bear dedications… or in some cases Hitler's eagle bookplate. Several books were acquired by Hitler before 1930.")
  • Another ~80 volumes are at Brown University (John Hay Library).
  • Timothy W. Ryback, Hitler's Private Library: The Books That Shaped His Life (Knopf, 2008, ISBN 978-1-4000-4204-3) is the standard study — it works from the actual surviving books and their marginalia. Full bibliography of the LOC holdings: Philipp Gassert & Daniel S. Mattern, The Hitler Library: A Bibliography (Greenwood Press, 2001).
  • Standing caution (Ryback's own): roughly two-thirds of the surviving collection is presentation copies Hitler probably never opened. Ownership alone ≠ reading. The grades below separate the two.

1. Henry Ford, The International Jew (1920–22; German: Der internationale Jude, 1921) — STRONG

  • Ford's Dearborn Independent articles, collected in book form; German translation published by Theodor Fritsch's Hammer-Verlag from summer 1921.
  • Evidence: a German translation was in Hitler's own library (Ryback); Friedrich Krohn's inventory of books Hitler borrowed 1919–1921 also lists it. The New York Times (20 Dec 1922) reported Ford's portrait on Hitler's Munich office wall and a table of copies of the book for visitors. Hitler praised Ford by name in Mein Kampf (Vol. 2) as the one great man standing up to Jewish influence in America — Ford is the only American named approvingly in the book. Hitler reportedly called Ford "my inspiration" (widely quoted; treat the exact quote as reported, not stenographed).
  • Note: claims that passages of Mein Kampf were copied "verbatim" from Ford circulate in popular books; the documented facts above are the solid ground.

2. Houston Stewart Chamberlain, Foundations of the Nineteenth Century (Die Grundlagen des neunzehnten Jahrhunderts, 1899) — STRONG

  • The English-born Wagner son-in-law's racial history: Teutons/Aryans as creators of civilisation, Jews as their opposite.
  • Evidence: listed in Krohn's record of Hitler's 1919–1921 borrowings; Ryback describes Hitler's pencilled underlinings in Chamberlain. Personal contact is documented: Hitler visited the ailing Chamberlain at Bayreuth in October 1923; Chamberlain's letter of 7 October 1923 blessing him ("That Germany, in the hour of her greatest need, brings forth a Hitler — that is proof of her vitality… May God protect you!") survives. Hitler attended Chamberlain's funeral in January 1927.

3. The Protocols of the Elders of Zion (first published Russia 1903; German edition 1920) — STRONG that he knew and used it; it is a FORGERY

  • What it is: a fabricated "record" of a secret Jewish plan for world domination, plagiarised largely from Maurice Joly's 1864 satire Dialogue in Hell and Hermann Goedsche's 1868 novel Biarritz. Exposed as a forgery by Philip Graves in The Times (London), 1921; declared a forgery by the Berne court, 1935.
  • Evidence for Hitler: he discusses the Protocols by name in Mein Kampf (Vol. 1, Ch. 11), arguing that the Frankfurter Zeitung's cries of "forgery" were themselves the best proof of authenticity — i.e. he knew the forgery charge and waved it away. His mentors Eckart and Alfred Rosenberg promoted the German edition (Rosenberg published a commentary edition in 1923); Stefan Link's scholarship notes a copy's presence in Hitler's library is unsurprising but the documented trail is the Mein Kampf passage plus his circle's promotion — there is no surviving Protocols copy with Hitler's marginalia identified by Ryback. Grade the "he read it directly" claim as strong for knowledge/use, unproven for a marked personal copy.

4. Karl May's adventure novels (Winnetou / Old Shatterhand series) — STRONG

  • Evidence: Hitler's own recollection, quoted by Ryback — "The first Karl May that I read was The Ride Across the Desert. I was overwhelmed! I threw myself into him immediately, which resulted in a noticeable decline in my grades." A leather-bound set of Karl May stood in his Berghof study; as Chancellor he still re-read May, and Albert Speer recalled him recommending May's heroes' stealth tactics to his generals. Lifelong, documented — but note this is boyhood adventure reading, an emotional favourite rather than an ideological source.

5. Dietrich Eckart — mentor, and Der Bolschewismus von Moses bis Lenin (1924/25) — STRONG (as influence); the book itself is Eckart's work

  • Eckart (1868–1923): poet, founder-member of the DAP, editor of Auf gut Deutsch, first editor of the Völkischer Beobachter. He lent Hitler books, taught him social polish, introduced him to wealthy patrons and to Alfred Rosenberg. Hitler dedicated Volume 2 of Mein Kampf to Eckart's memory and later called him a spiritual co-founder of the movement.
  • The posthumous dialogue Bolshevism from Moses to Lenin: A Dialogue Between Adolf Hitler and Me (Munich, 1924; some catalogues 1925) was completed and published by Rosenberg from Eckart's notes. Scholars are split: Margarete Plewnia argued the dialogue is Eckart's invention; Nolte, Heer and Scholder held it reflects Hitler's real words. Either way it is evidence of Eckart's ideas reaching Hitler, not a book Hitler sat down and read in the usual sense.

6. Theodor Fritsch, Handbuch der Judenfrage ("Handbook of the Jewish Question," from 1887 as Antisemiten-Katechismus; 49 editions by 1944) — MEDIUM-STRONG

  • Fritsch (1852–1933), the "old master" (Altmeister) of German antisemitism, ran the Hammer-Verlag in Leipzig, published the journal Hammer, issued the German Protocols and Ford's International Jew.
  • Evidence: Hitler himself credited the Handbuch as an important contribution that "paved the way for the National Socialist antisemitic movement" (as recorded in the Jewish Encyclopedia entry on Fritsch, encyclopedia.com). That is Hitler's own public acknowledgement — but no surviving copy with his marginalia has been identified, so it sits one grade below Ford/Chamberlain.

7. Richard Wagner — music and ideas — MEDIUM (admiration documented; direct reading thin)

  • Hitler's devotion to Wagner's operas from his Vienna youth onward is documented by contemporaries (Kubizek) and by his own statements; he was a welcome guest at Haus Wahnfried from 1923, close to Winifred Wagner, who supported him (including supplies during the Landsberg imprisonment) and ran Bayreuth under his patronage.
  • But as reading: Ryback found only a few Wagner-related volumes in the surviving library (including Chamberlain's Wagner biography), none by Wagner himself with Hitler's markings; historians such as Richard Evans caution that Wagner's direct intellectual influence "has often been exaggerated," and that Hitler's antisemitism is not shown to come from Wagner's writings. The main documented channel is indirect — through Chamberlain (item 2). Wagner's own antisemitic essay Das Judentum in der Musik (1850) appears in Hitler's world via a condensation ("Wagner and the Jews") in Krohn's borrowing list — exposure, yes; deep study, not proven.

8. Arthur Schopenhauer — WEAK for direct reading

  • Ryback's finding: Schopenhauer is absent from the surviving library, supporting the view Hitler knew him second-hand. A popular claim that Hitler carried The World as Will and Representation in the trenches and underlined its passages on will and compassion circulates in secondary summaries, but it is not backed by a surviving marked copy in the LOC/Brown collections — Ryback's "lost philosopher" material concerns other figures. Treat "Schopenhauer shaped Hitler" as anecdote, not documentation.

9. Friedrich Nietzsche — WEAK / MYTH as a personal influence

  • Same finding: no Nietzsche in the surviving library (Miskolczy: "no sign of Goethe, Schiller, Dante, Schopenhauer, or Nietzsche in his library" — with the honest caveat that some books were destroyed). Contemporaries claimed Hitler admired Nietzsche, and the regime exploited Nietzsche's name (and his sister Elisabeth's archive), but there is no credible evidence of Hitler personally studying Nietzsche's books. Ryback's verdict, in review summary: "little credible evidence of Hitler's personal engagement with serious philosophy… most of what we know is tenuous and at best anecdotal."

Also documented in the surviving library (for completeness — STRONG as ownership/markings)

  • Paul de Lagarde, Deutsche Schriften ("German Essays") — two copies at the LOC; Hitler's marginalia run over ~100 pages (Ryback). One of the most heavily annotated books he left.
  • Hans F. K. Günther, Rassenkunde des deutschen Volkes ("Racial Typology of the German People") — Ryback: "almost falling apart from frequent use."
  • Shakespeare (Georg Müller German edition, 1925), Don Quixote, Robinson Crusoe, Gulliver's Travels, Uncle Tom's Cabin — owned; Hitler ranked the last four among the great works of world literature (Ryback).
  • Clausewitz and a large military-history collection; a 1931 handbook on poison gas (with the Zyklon B chapter) sat in his library — ownership documented by Ryback; no evidence he annotated it.

Honest limits

  • The surviving ~1,280 volumes (LOC + Brown) are a fraction of the whole library, and an unrepresentative one — military and health collections largely did not survive. Absence from the survivors is evidence, not proof, for items 8–9.
  • Krohn's 1919–1921 borrowing list is a single document; grades leaning on it are marked as such.
  • Where a claim rests on later writers rather than paper evidence, it is graded WEAK and labelled — that includes most "Nietzsche/Schopenhauer formed Hitler" claims and the verbatim-copying claim about Ford.
