Khazar Primary Sources — The Documents in Their Own Words

Map of the Khazar khaganate, about 820. Source: Wikimedia Commons

Research file, compiled 2026-11-19. The project rule — exact words, not summaries — is followed as far as the sources allow: every block quote below is a published English translation of a period document, labeled with who wrote it, when, and how it survives. Where a document is fragmentary, legendary, or disputed, that is stated next to the quote, not buried.

1. The Khazar Correspondence (c. 954–961)

What it is: Two Hebrew letters. Hasdai ibn Shaprut (c. 915–975), Jewish courtier and foreign minister to the Caliph of Córdoba, heard reports of a Jewish kingdom in the east and wrote to its king. The reply came from King Joseph, khagan of the Khazars. The exchange survives in three slightly variant versions (a "short" and a "long" recension of Joseph's Reply). It is one of the very few documents authored by a Khazar ruler.

Why it matters: It is the Khazars' own account of who they were — and, notably, of where they came from.

Hasdai's opening

[PRIMARY — Hasdai ibn Shaprut to King Joseph] "I, Hasdai, son of Isaac, son of Ezra, belonging to the exiled Jews of Jerusalem in Sepharad, a servant of my lord the King, bow to the earth before him and prostrate myself towards the abode of your Majesty from a distant land. I rejoice in your tranquillity and magnificence and stretch forth my hands to God in heaven that He may prolong your reign in Israel…. I pray for the health of my lord the King, of his family, and of his house, and that his throne may be established for ever. Let his days and his sons' days be prolonged in the midst of Israel!"

Hasdai's first messenger was turned back at Constantinople; the letter that finally got through was carried by Isaac son of Eliezer, a Jew of Germany, via Germany, Hungary, and Rus'.

Joseph on his ancestry — the genealogy claim

Hasdai had asked the question every medieval Jew wanted answered: which of the twelve tribes are you from? Joseph's answer is the single most-quoted passage in Khazar studies — because he does not claim Israelite descent at all:

[PRIMARY — King Joseph's Reply] "We have found in the genealogical books of our fathers that Togarmah had ten sons, and these are their names: the firstborn [was] Uigur, the second Dursu, the third Avar, the fourth Oguz, the fifth Barzilia [Basilii], the sixth Tarniakh, the seventh Khazar, the eighth Zagora, the ninth Bulgar, the tenth Sabir. We are of the sons of Khazar, who is the seventh."

In the framing of the letter: "I am of the sons of Japheth, from the seed of Togarmah." Togarmah (Genesis 10:3) is a grandson of Japheth, son of Noah — in medieval Jewish geography, the ancestor of the Turkic peoples. Joseph, in other words, presents the Khazars exactly as the historians do: a Turkic people who adopted the religion of Israel, not a lost tribe of Israel itself. (Later writers who claim Joseph asserted Israelite blood descent are contradicted by his own letter.)

Joseph on the conversion

[PRIMARY — King Joseph's Reply] "After this, several generations passed until a certain King arose whose name was Bulan. He was a wise and God-fearing man, trusting in his Creator with all his heart. He expelled the wizards and idolaters from the land and took refuge in the shadow of his wings… After this his fame was spread broadcast. The king of the Byzantines and the Arabs who had heard of him sent their envoys and ambassadors with great riches and many great presents to the King as well as some of their wise men with the object of converting him to their own religion."

The disputation and Bulan's decision:

[PRIMARY — King Joseph's Reply] On the third day he called all the sages together and said to them, "Speak and argue with one another and clarify which is the best religion." They began to dispute with one another without arriving at any results, until the king said to the Christian priest, "What do you think? Of the religion of the Jews and the Muslims, which is to be preferred?" The priest answered: "The religion of the Israelites is better than that of the Muslims." The king then asked the [Muslim judge]: "What do you say? Is the religion of the Israelites or that of the Christians preferable?" He answered: "The religion of the Israelites is preferable." Upon this, the king said: "If this is so, you both have admitted with your own mouths that the religion of the Israelites is better. Therefore trusting in the mercies of God and the power of the Almighty, I choose the religion of Israel, that is, the religion of Abraham…"

And on Obadiah's reform (see the conversion file for the full passage):

[PRIMARY — King Joseph's Reply] "[Obadiah] reorganized the kingdom and established the Jewish religion properly and correctly. He built synagogues and yeshivot, brought in Jewish scholars, and rewarded them with gold and silver. They explained to him the Bible, Mishnah, Talmud and the order of divine services…"

Status of the document: The Correspondence is accepted as a genuine 10th-century exchange by most specialists, though the long recension of Joseph's Reply contains legendary expansions (including the implied c. 620 date, which cannot be right). Joseph's Reply also gives Khazaria's c. 960 status — still, on his telling, receiving tribute and militarily formidable — within a generation of the state's destruction.

2. The Schechter Letter / Cambridge Document (mid-10th century)

What it is: An anonymous Hebrew letter by a Khazar Jew, found in the Cairo Geniza and published by Solomon Schechter in 1912 (hence the names). Most of the folio is unreadable; two substantial blocks survive. It may have been written at Constantinople, possibly for Hasdai ibn Shaprut; its author calls Khazaria "our land" and Joseph "my lord."

Its conversion account contradicts Joseph's: Jews from Persia and Armenia migrated in and intermarried; the conversion is credited to a commander named Sabriel, converted under the influence of his wife Serakh, a descendant of the tribe of Simeon. No Bulan, no debate.

Its war account is historically precious — and chronologically explosive. It says the Byzantine emperor Romanus I Lecapenus (r. 920–944) bribed HLGW, king of Rus', to attack Khazaria; the Khazar general Pesakh defeated him in the Taman region, took three Byzantine-held cities, marched on Cherson, and forced HLGW to attack Constantinople instead (a Rus' attack on Constantinople did occur in 941); HLGW then fled to "FRS" (Persia?) and died there.

Status: [DEBATED] If HLGW is Oleg of Novgorod (Helgi), the Primary Chronicle's chronology (Oleg dead by 912) collapses — which is why Constantin Zuckerman used this letter to re-date the Rus' kings and the conversion. Other scholars identify HLGW differently or discount parts of the letter; a 2013 study judged its conversion section unreliable, noting Sabriel is an angel's name and Serah a biblical figure — the fingerprints of "lost tribes" pious literature. Honest label: a genuine Geniza document whose historical reliability is contested section by section.

Sources: https://wiki.poast.org/wiki/Schechter_Letter ; https://www.turkicatlas.org/sources/schechter-letter/

3. The Kievan Letter (c. 930)

What it is: A Hebrew letter of recommendation from the Jewish community of Kiev, found in the Cairo Geniza (Cambridge, Taylor-Schechter 12.122), rediscovered by Norman Golb in 1962 and published by Golb and Omeljan Pritsak in Khazarian Hebrew Documents of the Tenth Century (1982).

What it says, in its own words:

[PRIMARY — Kievan Letter] "…we, the community of Kiev, [hereby] inform you of the troublesome affair of this Mar Jacob bar Hanukkah, who is of the sons of [good folk]. He was of the givers, and not of the takers, until a cruel decree was decreed against him, in that his brother went and took money from gentiles; this Jacob stood surety. His brother went on the road, and there came [robbers and killed his brother and took the money]… [they] took captive this Jacob, they put chains of iron on his neck…"

The community ransomed Jacob for 60 coins and sent him traveling among Jewish communities to raise the remaining 40. The signatories mix Hebrew, Slavic, and Turkic names — among them "GWSTT (Gostata) bar KYBR (Kiabar) Kohen," "Judah, who is called SWRTH (Surta)," and two community leaders (parnasim), Abraham and Isaac. After the signatures stands a single word in Turkic runiform script, read by Pritsak as hoqurūm — "I have read [this]" — possibly the only surviving written word of the Khazar language.

Status: [DEBATED in its framing, genuine as a document] Nobody doubts the letter is a real 10th-century Geniza document. What is fought over: whether it was written from Kiev or sent to Kiev (Marcel Erdal argues the latter), whether its Turkic names and runic word prove a Khazar (as opposed to generally Jewish, or Danube-Bulgar) provenance, and whether the "KYBR" name connects to the Khazar Kabar tribe or should be read differently ("son of Kiy," per Torpusman). A 2026 reassessment in the Journal of Jewish Studies (Torres Prieto & Outhwaite) contests the Khazarian provenance again. Use it as firm evidence of a Hebrew-literate Jewish community in 10th-century Kiev with Turkic-named members — and as contested evidence for anything beyond that.

Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kievan_Letter ; Zuckerman's study at http://history.org.ua/JournALL/ruthenica/ruthenica_2011_10/2.pdf

4. The Arab and Persian geographers (9th–10th centuries)

These are outsiders looking in — Muslim officials, travelers, and geographers with no stake in Jewish tradition. Their testimony is why most historians accept the conversion happened at all.

Al-Masudi (writing 940s), Meadows of Gold:

[PRIMARY — al-Masudi] "The Jews are the king, his surrounding [court] and the Khazars of his clan."

[PRIMARY — al-Masudi] "The custom in the Khazar capital is to have seven judges. Of these two are for the Muslims, two are for the Khazars, judging according to the Torah, two for the Christians, judging according to the Gospel and one for the Saqualibah, Rus and other pagans, judging according to pagan law…. In his city are many Muslims, merchants and craftsmen, who have come to his country because of his justice and the security which he offers."

Al-Masudi also dates the king's conversion to the caliphate of Harun al-Rashid (786–809) and describes the khagan's standing army of armored horsemen.

Ibn Fadlan (envoy of the Caliph to the Volga Bulgars, 921–922; his Khazar material is reportage, preserved in Yaqut's dictionary):

[PRIMARY — Ibn Fadlan] "All Khazars and their king are the Jews."

And the synagogue incident of 922/923: the Khazar king, hearing a synagogue had been destroyed in Dar al-Babunj, "ordered the minaret torn down and the muezzins killed," saying he would have destroyed the mosque as well "if I did not fear that no synagogue would be left standing in the lands of Islam."

Ibn Fadlan also preserves court ritual material — the khagan's great brick palace, his 25 wives and 60 concubines, and the reported forty-year limit on a khagan's reign, after which "his subjects and attendants kill him, saying, 'His reasoning is already dimmed, and his insight confused.'" (Reportage of custom, to be handled as such.)

Ibn Khordadbeh and al-Fakih (9th–10th c.): the Khazars "are all Jews who accepted Judaism recently." Ibn Khordadbeh is also our source for the Radhanite Jewish merchants whose routes ran from Western Europe and Spain through Khazaria to China.

Ibn Hawqal (writing c. 977) is the key source for the fall: the Rus' in AH 358 (968/969) sacked Bulgar, Khazaran, Samandar, and Itil — the source of the famous line, "The Rus attacked, and no grape or raisin remained, not a leaf on a branch."

Sources: https://www.turkicatlas.org/places/itil/ ; https://oriental-world.org.ua/index.php/journal/article/download/670/638

5. Byzantine sources

Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus, De Administrando Imperio (c. 950) — the emperor's handbook for his son. It treats the Khazars as a great power to be managed: it records how the Khazars asked Constantinople for the engineers who built Sarkel (the mission of the spatharokandidatos Petronas under Theophilus), discusses Khazar relations with the Pechenegs, Magyars, and Rus', and preserves the detail that the Kabars — three Khazar tribes that rebelled against the khagan — joined the Magyars and became part of the Hungarian founding people. It is also a source for the imperial chancery's protocol for letters to the Khazar khagan (a gold seal of specified weight — a measure of the khagan's rank in Byzantine eyes).

Theophanes the Confessor's Chronicle records the 732 marriage: Emperor Leo III betrothed his son Constantine to the khagan's daughter; she "was made a Christian and named Irene" (Tzitzak/Çiçek, "flower"), and in 750 bore Leo — the future Leo IV "the Khazar."

The Life of Constantine (St. Cyril) describes Constantine's mission to the Khazar court c. 860/861 and debates with Jewish and Muslim scholars there — valuable evidence that the Khazar court of the 860s was a place where Judaism was argued from strength, though the Life is hagiography, not minutes.

Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzitzak ; https://www.turkicatlas.org/persons/tzitzak/

6. The Russian Primary Chronicle (compiled c. 1113)

The Slavic side of the record. Under 965:

[PRIMARY — Primary Chronicle] "Svyatoslav sallied forth against the Khazars. When they heard of his approach, they went out to meet him with their Prince, the Kagan, and the armies came to blows. When battle thus took place, Svyatoslav defeated the Khazars and took their city of Bela Vezha [Sarkel]. He also conquered the Yasians and the Kasogians."

The Chronicle also preserves the earlier tribute relationship (the Polianians' swords) and mentions Khazars as actors in Rus' affairs into the 11th–12th centuries — one reason historians say Khazaria was broken in the 960s but did not vanish overnight. Its 10th-century chronology is, however, notoriously unreliable — the very point at issue in the Schechter Letter controversy.

7. Yehuda Halevi's Kuzari (written c. 1140) — a literary source, labeled as such

What it is: Kitab al-Khazari ("The Book of the Khazars"), a philosophical dialogue in Arabic by the Spanish Jewish poet-philosopher Yehuda Halevi. A Khazar king, having dreamed that his intentions please God but his actions do not, interrogates a philosopher, a Christian, a Muslim, and finally a Jewish rabbi — whose arguments carry the book.

Status: [LITERARY, not documentary] Halevi was writing philosophy two centuries after the conversion, using the Khazar king as his framing device. The Kuzari is priceless evidence of how the conversion story lived in Jewish memory — and it is the source of the conventional date, since Halevi places the king "four hundred years before" his own time, i.e., c. 740. But it is not a 8th-century record, and the c. 740 date that descends from it should always carry that asterisk. (The disputation motif it shares with Joseph's Reply and the Vladimir legend is a medieval storytelling pattern.)

8. What we do not have

For honesty's sake, the gaps:

  • No Khazar chronicle, law code, or inscription book survives. Everything internal is two letters (Joseph's Reply, the Schechter Letter) plus one community letter (the Kievan Letter).
  • Itil has never been excavated — no confirmed site, so no material check on the geographers' descriptions.
  • The conversion's key narrative (Bulan) exists in two contradictory Khazar versions and one literary retelling 200 years later.
  • The 19th-century "Crimean Khazar" documents of Abraham Firkovich are forgeries and must never be quoted as evidence.
