Oświęcim Town — History from the Early 1800s to the End of WWII

Researched 2026-11-22 at James's request: the town of Oświęcim (German: Auschwitz) and the neighbouring village of Brzezinka (German: Birkenau) — how big the town was, its Jewish community, its banks and money, who really held economic weight there (including an honest check on the Rothschild name), the "pornography" angle as period sources actually present it, and what the Library of Congress newspapers show. The camp is covered only where it touches the town; this piece is about the town itself.

Names: Polish Oświęcim = German Auschwitz. Polish Brzezinka = German Birkenau ("birch grove" — Brzezinka comes from brzoza, birch). The town's Jews called it Oshpitzin in Yiddish.

1. The town and its size over time

Oświęcim is an old town — first mentioned in writing in 1178, once the capital of its own small duchy, sitting where the Soła river meets the Vistula, on trade routes between Kraków, Silesia and Moravia. In the First Partition of Poland (1772) it fell to the Habsburgs and became part of Austrian Galicia — so much a part of the province's formal name that the province was styled the Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria with the Duchies of Auschwitz and Zator. It stayed Austrian until 1918.

The 1800s — a small town the railway made important. At the start of the century it was a modest Galician market town. Two things changed it:

  • Industry and drink: in 1804 Jakub Haberfeld founded the vodka and liqueur factory that became the town's best-known business (see §3).
  • The railway: the Emperor Ferdinand Northern Railway reached Oświęcim from Bohumín in 1855, and in 1856 the line from Oświęcim via Trzebinia to Kraków opened, giving an all-Austrian Vienna–Kraków route. Historian Sybille Steinbacher's summary: the town got its station in 1856, and "thanks to its location between the coal-mining area around Katowice (Kattowitz)-Dombrowa and the industrial area of Bielsko (Bielitz), Oświęcim became a railway junction in 1900: three lines of the Emperor Ferdinand Northern Railway led directly to Cracow, Katowice and Vienna." While most of Galicia stayed agricultural and poor, Oświęcim grew into a prosperous industrial town on the edge of the Upper Silesian coal belt.

Emigration town. From the 1880s to 1914, Galicia poured out emigrants — Poles, Ukrainians and Jews heading for America, and seasonal labourers (Sachsengänger) heading for Prussian farms. Oświęcim, the last Austrian town before the Prussian border, became a collection and transit point for that stream. In 1916–1917 the Austrian state built a large emigration and seasonal-workers' camp in the town's Zasole district — 22 brick buildings and around 90 wooden ones, with a national employment exchange. After 1918 the Polish Army took the complex over as artillery barracks. Hold that fact: those barracks are the buildings that became Auschwitz I in 1940.

The Auschwitz-Birkenau Museum's own summary matches the picture: at the end of 1939 Oświęcim had "over 12,500 people, about half of whom were Jews." The 1921 dip in the Jewish share and the 1940 spike both have causes: after WWI some Jews left for Vienna and elsewhere, and in late 1940 the Germans forced Jews from surrounding villages into Oświęcim before expelling the town's Jews altogether in 1941 (§2).

Brzezinka (Birkenau), the village: a farming village about 3 km from the town, documented since the 14th century. In the late 19th century it had 1,025 inhabitants. Before WWII it had grown to roughly 3,000 people in 536 houses, with an elementary school, two nursery schools, twelve shops and seven workshops. It was a Polish Catholic farming village — the Jewish story belongs to the town; Brzezinka's part in this history is what was done to it in 1941 (§2, end).

WWI: Russia's 1914 offensive overran most of Galicia as far as the outskirts of Kraków "where they were halted, without advancing towards Oshpitzin" (the town's Yizkor book) — the front came close but the town was not taken. It lived the war as a garrison and transit town behind the front, full of soldiers and refugees; refugee Hasidic courts (the Sassow rebbe among them) sheltered there for a time. In 1918 it became part of reborn Poland.

Interwar: a Polish provincial town of about twelve thousand, half Jewish, with its factories, its railway junction, and a Jewish deputy mayor in 1933 (see §2). That is the town that entered September 1939.

2. The Jewish community

Four hundred years old. Jewish settlement is documented from the first half of the 16th century (a 1549 inventory already lists the town's Jews); the first wooden synagogue went up in 1588 near what became Żydowska ("Jewish") Street — today Berka Joselewicza Street — by the castle, and that southern quarter stayed the centre of Jewish life. Through the 1600s–1700s the community traded, distilled, and handled the wholesale trade in Bochnia salt; it survived a 1563 royal numerus clausus limiting new Jewish settlement, the Swedish wars that destroyed the town in 1656, and was re-confirmed in its privileges in 1766.

The 1800s — emancipation and growth. Under Joseph II's reforms after 1780 the town's Jews were gradually brought into the Monarchy's civic life. After 1867's emancipation the integration is visible in the council chamber — Virtual Shtetl, quoting the record: "in the very first council of the town of Oświęcim, elected in 1867, nine of twenty-four councillors were Jewish, while in the subsequent years the proportion was as follows: in 1870, eleven out of twenty-four councillors were Jewish, then, in the next elections, in 1873 and 1876, the number of the Jews performing the role of councillors increased to thirteen." Jewish deputy mayors served in 1873 (Juliusz Haberfeld) and 1876 (Dr Maurycy Schlank). By 1904, 13 of the 24 town councillors were Jewish; in 1932, 18 of 32; and in 1933 the town elected a Jewish deputy mayor, Dr Emil Samuel Reich.

Institutions. The community's crown was the Great Synagogue: built 1873 on the old synagogue site, rebuilt 1899–1900 to a design by Carl Korn, with 2,000 seats — and, a point of local pride, the first building in the town to get electric lighting, switched on in 1925. It was one of around 20 synagogues and prayer houses in the town, alongside Hasidic shtiblekh (the town had ties to the Bobov Hasidic world — Rabbi Szlomo Halberstam served there in the later 1800s), the small Chevra Lomdei Mishnayot prayer house (built c. 1913 — the only one whose building survives today), a Jewish cemetery, Talmud Torah and Jewish schools, Zionist, Agudath Israel and Bund organisations, sports clubs, a theatre and choir. The kehilla's jurisdiction ran over the Jewish villages of the whole old duchy — Babice, Brzeszcze, Brzezinka and dozens more were listed in the Oświęcim kehilla in 1900.

Work. Jews distilled (Haberfeld), tanned, made carbonated water, cement, chemicals and roofing paper, traded wholesale (grain and eggs — R' Shaul Dovid Englard became the region's principal egg supplier), and filled the professions — the Great Synagogue's congregation was notably the doctors, lawyers, officials and entrepreneurs. The best-known Jewish families were the Enochs, Schönkers, Haberfelds and Henerbergs.

1939–1941 — the end. German bombs fell on the town on 1 September 1939 (a school was hit in the first hour, per resident testimony collected by the Museum); the town was occupied in the first days of the war and in October 1939 annexed outright to the German Reich and renamed Auschwitz. On the night of 29–30 November 1939 German soldiers burned the Great Synagogue; its remains were demolished. In late 1940 the Germans first concentrated more Jews into the town from the villages, then reversed: at the beginning of 1941 they decided to remove Oświęcim's Jews entirely. In early April 1941 the whole Jewish population — about 7,000 people — was expelled to the ghettos of Sosnowiec, Będzin and Chrzanów; their homes were confiscated (many sold to IG Farben as housing for German staff). Most of Oświęcim's Jews were later deported from those ghettos to the camp built beside their own town, and murdered there.

Brzezinka's expulsion. The same April: between 7 and 12 April 1941 the Germans expelled Brzezinka's roughly 3,000 Polish villagers and demolished more than 500 of their houses — only a handful of buildings survived. In all, 8,000–9,000 Poles were displaced from the town and the eight villages of the camp's 40 km² "interest zone" (Babice, Broszkowice, Budy, Harmęże, Pławy, Rajsko and others). Birkenau camp was then built on Brzezinka's fields starting in October 1941; two of its first gas chambers were converted farmhouses of expelled Poles.

Why the camp was put here (the town-history part of the question): the proposal came from SS offices in Breslau in late 1939, driven by overcrowded Silesian prisons. The site offered (a) the ready-made Zasole barracks — the 1916–17 emigration camp turned Polish artillery barracks — in the fork of the Vistula and Soła, easy to isolate and expand; (b) the railway junction, with lines to Silesia, the General Government, Czechoslovakia and Austria; (c) annexed-Reich territory. Himmler ordered the camp on 27 April 1940; the first transport, 728 Polish political prisoners from Tarnów, arrived 14 June 1940. Separately, in early 1941, IG Farben chose the land east of the town (Dwory–Monowice) for its Buna synthetic-rubber plant — coal, water, rail, and, in the Museum's words, the prospect of camp labour "may in fact have been decisive." The town itself was progressively Germanised; Red Army troops liberated town and camps together on 27 January 1945 — over 230 Soviet soldiers who died in that fighting are buried in Oświęcim's municipal cemetery.

3. Money, banks and influence — what the record actually shows

Local money first. The documented money-power in Oświęcim was local and mostly Jewish-owned industry, not outside banks:

  • Jakob Haberfeld's distillery (founded 1804) — the town's oldest Jewish business; Steinbacher: it "made the town famous for many miles around with its trademark 'Schnapps from Oświęcim'." The Haberfelds were civic as well as economic leaders (a Haberfeld was deputy mayor in 1873).
  • Józef Schönker's "Union" chemical factory, and his Agrochemia fertiliser plant (1906); Schönker also took over the Frenkel Brothers factory.
  • Nathanson & Malcer's building-paper and asphalt factory; Landau & Wolf's roofing-paper factory; tanneries, cement and building firms.
  • Steinbacher's general sentence on the town's Jews: "Many became big businessmen and opened banks and factories in Oświęcim and the surrounding area." (The banks are not named in the sources pulled for this pass — an honest gap, flagged for a deeper Polish-language search.)

Credit for ordinary people is documented better than banks:

  • James's own album item M285 (Polish newspaper page, this folder) records that in 1904 a Józef Karol Biale became the Oświęcim-district representative of the municipal loan office (Kasa pożyczkowa) of Biała, that around half a million crowns had been lent, and mentions a Towarzystwo zaliczkowe (credit/advance association). Small-town credit ran through municipal loan offices and credit associations like this, Polish and Jewish alike.
  • Inside the Jewish community, the G'milus Chasodim (Free Loan Society) was one of the kehilla's standing charities, alongside Tomchei Aniyim (support of the poor), Bikur Cholim (medical aid) and Hachnosas Kalla (wedding assistance). The Yizkor book credits R' Miche'le Blumenfrucht — later head of the kehilla council — with founding "a free loan society and hachnosas kalla" among his communal institutions. Interest-free communal lending, not a bank, was the Jewish credit system of the town.

The Rothschild check — honest answer: no Rothschild bank, estate or office in Oświęcim appears in any record found. Searches pairing the Rothschild name with the town return conspiracy literature about other subjects (Thyssen, IG Farben, American banks), not records about this town. What the record does show is one genuine, indirect Rothschild thread, and it is about the railway, not the town's money:

  • The Emperor Ferdinand Northern Railway — the company whose lines made Oświęcim a junction (Bohumín–Oświęcim 1855; the Oświęcim–Kraków line followed in 1856, built by the Eastern State Railway and later taken over by the Northern Railway) — was financed by Salomon Mayer von Rothschild of Vienna; it was Austria's first steam railway company. So Rothschild money stands behind the railway age that transformed the town — the same kind of connection the family had to railways all over the Austrian Empire — but that is financing a Vienna railway company in the 1830s–50s, not control of Oświęcim, its banks, or its people.
  • The family's heavy-industry base in the wider region was in Moravia (the Vítkovice/Witkowitz ironworks near Ostrava) — a different town, some distance away. (Regional context; not re-verified line-by-line in this pass.)
  • The outside corporation that did take economic possession of the town's edge in the end was IG Farben in 1941 — a German chemical cartel, no Rothschild connection — which bought the Monowice land from the Reich treasury after it was seized from Polish owners, took over confiscated Jewish houses for its German employees, and hired camp prisoners at 3–4 marks a day. IG Farben director Otto Ambros wrote to his colleagues about the negotiations, in the Museum's quotation: "our new friendship with the SS is very fruitful."

The "pornography" angle. No period source found in this pass ties Oświęcim by name to pornography. What period sources do show — for Galicia as a whole — is the era's great vice panic, the "white slave trade" (German: Mädchenhandel, traffic in girls): the fear that emigration agents were luring Galician girls to brothels in Buenos Aires and Constantinople. Historian Edward Bristow's research (reviewed in the London Review of Books) records that between 1904 and 1908 police in Galicia identified over a hundred Jewish sex-traders, and the Austrian Interior Ministry's own police-science journal (Jürgen Nautz, 2012) describes Galicia as the main source region of the traffic and the emigration routes as its channel. Oświęcim sat directly in that stream as one of Galicia's great emigration transit stations, which is why the town's name belongs in the same paragraph as the panic — but a newspaper item naming Oświęcim itself in a trafficking or pornography case has not been found yet. James's search words for it, when LOC is reachable again: "Auschwitz" + "white slave", "Oswiecim" + "girls", "Mädchenhandel" + "Oswiecim" in the German-language American press.

Note the pattern in his own finds: before 1942 the town appears in the Polish-American press as a place (1913, 1914, 1919 items); from 1944 on, American papers are printing "Oswiecim" as the name of the camp — and two of his finds were made with the pair "Oswiecim population," i.e. the papers were already arguing about how many had died there while the war was still on. James's Polish-press album items M283–M287 (this folder) belong to the same picture: the railway (M283, M284), the 1904 loan office (M285), a Soła bridge accident report (M286), and a German report of fighting near Oswiecim in Galicia (M287).

One period line found tonight — not from LOC (from GenealogyBank's blog transcription of the Milwaukee Journal, labeled as such; do not file as a Chronicling America find): the Milwaukee Journal, February 2, 1945, p. 39, printing Pravda correspondent Boris Polevoy's report: the Red Army "had saved several thousand tortured, emaciated inmates of the Germans' greatest 'murder factory' at Oswiecim (Auschwitz) in southwest Poland," and Polevoy: "They can tell the whole story of this gigantic death plant, equipped with the most modern Nazi technology of torture and extermination." And from the Jewish press's own archive (Intermountain Jewish News, July 7, 1944 — a year before liberation): "1,750,000 Jews were slain in two extermination camps in Auschwitz and Birkenau, Upper Silesia, between April 15, 1942 and April 15, 1944." American papers knew the name, and a number, while it was still happening.
