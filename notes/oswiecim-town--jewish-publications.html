Jewish Publications About Oświęcim (Oshpitzin / Auschwitz) and Birkenau (Brzezinka)

Research compiled 2026-11-22 for James's Jewish history project. Town names: Polish Oświęcim · Yiddish Oshpitzin (אושפיצין) · German Auschwitz. Village: Polish Brzezinka · German Birkenau.

Every link below was copied from a page actually opened during this research. Anything that could not be verified is labelled UNVERIFIED. No link, headline, or date has been invented.

1. The Yizkor Book — Sefer Oshpitzin

Exact title: Sefer Oshpitsin (ספר אושפיצין) — catalogued in English as "Oswiecim; Auschwitz memorial book"; the St. Louis County Library catalogue gives the fuller form: Sefer Oshpitsin: Oshviyents'im-Oshvits [Oswiecim–Auschwitz memorial book].

  • Editors: Ch. Wolnerman, A. Burstin, M. S. Geshuri
  • Published: Jerusalem, Oshpitsin Society, 1977
  • Size / languages: 622 pages, in Hebrew and Yiddish (marked "H, Y" in the catalogues)
  • Catalogue record (Hitachdut Yotzei Polin yizkor book catalogue, compiled by Ada Holtzman): https://www.zchor.org/hityiz.htm
  • Library record (St. Louis County Library, "Yizkor books by locality"): https://www.slcl.org/yizkor-books-locality

3. Jewish Newspapers — Yiddish

Same JPress block applies (see §2). Yiddish titles held by the Historical Jewish Press that cover the town's period:

  • Haynt (הײַנט, Warsaw) and Der Moment (דער מאָמענט, Warsaw) — the big pre-war Warsaw Yiddish dailies; Oświęcim was in their Galician coverage area before September 1939
  • Forverts (פֿאָרווערטס, New York, on JPress 1897–2015) and Der Tog (דער טאָג, New York) — for 1939 synagogue-burning reports, 1945 liberation coverage, and post-war Auschwitz trial / museum coverage

4. Local Jewish Publications From the Town Itself

Honest result: no Jewish newspaper or periodical published in Oświęcim itself was found or verified. The town's Jews (over half the population before 1939) read the Kraków and Warsaw Jewish press; the community's own print legacy is the yizkor book (§1) and the kehilla records mined by the sources below.

What does exist for the town itself:

  • Virtual Shtetl (POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews) — full Oświęcim community entry: history, demography (Jews were 51.8% of the town in 1861; 53.6% in 1910; over 58% in 1939), synagogues (the Great Synagogue, built 1873, rebuilt 1899–1900, destroyed 29–30 November 1939; the surviving Chevra Lomdei Mishnayot synagogue, from 1918). History: https://sztetl.org.pl/en/node/137813 · Chevra Lomdei Mishnayot synagogue: https://sztetl.org.pl/en/node/87705
  • JewishGen — no separate KehilaLinks page for Oświęcim was located in this search (UNVERIFIED — worth a direct check at jewishgen.org/kehilaLinks); JewishGen's Oświęcim resources are the yizkor book translation and the Oshpitzin Yizkor Database (§1).
  • Pinkas HaKehillot (פנקס הקהילות, Yad Vashem's Hebrew Encyclopaedia of Jewish Communities), Vol. III: Western Galicia & Silesia, Jerusalem 1984 — the Oświęcim entry is at pp. 48–54. Volume table of contents: https://www.zchor.org/hitachdut/pinkas3.htm

5. Books About Jewish Oświęcim

  1. Lucyna Filip — Żydzi w Oświęcimiu 1918–1941 ("Jews in Oświęcim 1918–1941"), Oświęcim, 2003 (Polish). The first study in Polish of the town's Jewish community: religious, social-political, cultural and economic life, and its fate under the occupation — anti-Jewish legislation, resettlement to ghettos, deportation. Annexes include lists of synagogues, rabbis, cemetery burials, and names of deportees to the Sosnowiec ghetto. Expanded German edition: Juden in Oświęcim 1918–1941, Wydawnictwo Scientia, 2005, 348 pp. (Some English-language bibliographies cite an English title "Jews in Oswiecim 1918–1941, 2005" — a separate English edition was not verified; the publisher-verified 2005 edition is the German one.) Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum announcement: https://www.auschwitz.org/en/museum/news/jews-in-oswiecim-1918-1941,428.html

  2. Rabbi Moshe Weiss — From Oswiecim to Auschwitz: Poland Revisited, Mosaic Press, 1994 (English; ISBN 9780889625587 hardcover / 9780889625570 paperback). Weiss grew up in Oświęcim before the war; the book is his return journey to the town and the camps. A Hebrew edition appeared in 1998 (ISBN 9780967928623). Weiss also wrote the yizkor book chapter "My Town Oswiecim – A City of Blood and Tears" (p. 381). Library record: https://openlibrary.org/books/OL9331390M/From_Oswiecim_to_Auschwitz

  3. Auschwitz Jewish Center — Oshpitzin: A Guide to Jewish History of Oświęcim (English and Polish), Auschwitz Jewish Center, 2011 — the first guidebook to the town's Jewish sites, with a map; prepared by the Jewish Center after ten years of collecting materials. Catalogue entry (Jewish Genealogical Society of the Conejo Valley permanent library): https://www.jgscv.org/library-permanent.html · Presentation of the guide in Oś — Oświęcim, People, History, Culture magazine no. 30, June 2011 (Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum): http://auschwitz.org/gfx/auschwitz/userfiles/auschwitz/os_pdf/os_30_eng.pdf The Center's permanent museum exhibition is likewise titled "Oshpitzin. The History of Jewish Oświęcim": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auschwitz_Jewish_Center_in_O%C5%9Bwi%C4%99cim

  4. Artur Szyndler — "Oszpicin. Oświęcimscy Żydzi na przestrzeni wieków" ("Oshpitzin: Oświęcim Jews over the centuries"), a chapter devoted to the town's Jewish history in Volume II of the Oświęcim town monograph, Wydawnictwa Uniwersytetu Warszawskiego. Chapter record: https://www.ceeol.com/search/chapter-detail?id=875200

  5. Pinkas HaKehillot, Vol. III (Yad Vashem, 1984, Hebrew) — Oświęcim entry, pp. 48–54 (see §4).

6. Leaving / Migrating Out of the Area — What the Jewish Publications Say

James's angle: "If they're talking about moving people out of there or sending them away. Sending them on ship or bus or whatever. To leave that area, migrating out of there."

(a) Pre-war emigration — people leaving on their own

From the town itself. The Auschwitz Jewish Center's own educational publication (Jewish Life in Oświęcim and Sosnowiec Before 1939, Eternal Echoes project; text by Tomasz Cebulski, Ph.D.) says it in one sentence:

"Despite growing emigration to bigger Polish cities, the British mandate of Palestine or America, the Jews of Oświęcim remained economically and culturally strong."

— and, on the pull of the bigger economies:

"Looking for larger markets and bigger economies, many locals started to move to cities in Zagłębie/Silesia in the northwest, or to Krakow in the east."

https://www.eternalechoes.org/application/files/9916/0978/8245/lea_gleitman_context-1_en_eternal_echoes.pdf

The yizkor book's own chapters on leaving for Palestine (table of contents, Sefer Oshpitzin): "The Zionist Movement" (p. 269), "Hechalutz" (p. 271), "Hashomer Hatza'ir" (p. 273), "En Route to the Homeland" / Yitzchak Tzivoni (Farber) (p. 399), and "A City and its Chalutzim" / Prof. Dov Sadan (p. 411). http://www.jewishgen.org/yizkor/oswiecim1/oswiecim.html

The town as an emigration transit point — the station in Brzezinka. Oświęcim station stood about two kilometres outside town, in the parish of Brzezinka — the same ground that later became Birkenau. Martin Pollack's The Emperor of America (Der Kaiser von Amerika), in the Asymptote translation, describes the 1881 Russian-Jewish refugee stream:

"They are sent first to Oświęcim, the last Austrian stop before the Prussian border, and from there onto Hamburg to board ships for North America and Argentina."

"The emigrants congregate at Oświęcim station, situated two kilometres outside the town, in the parish of Brzezinka, Birkenau in German: Ukrainian and Polish peasants and cottagers, farmhands from large estates and day labourers, Slovak villagers and shepherds, Galician and Russian Jews, small craftsmen and merchants…"

https://www.asymptotejournal.com/nonfiction/martin-pollack-the-emperor-of-america/

The emigration business around the station was big enough to produce the famous 1889 Wadowice trial of Oświęcim's (largely Jewish) emigration agents. Historian Tara Zahra's paper "Travel Agents on Trial: Policing Mobility in Late Imperial Austria":

"Oświecim, located at the juncture of Prussian, Russian, and Austrian railway lines, had recently developed a booming emigration business. Since 1880, hundreds of thousands of East Europeans trekked toward the German ports of Hamburg and Bremen en route to America."

https://history.osu.edu/sites/history.osu.edu/files/zahra-paper.pdf

(b) Sent away — the German expulsion of the town's Jews, April 1941

Jewish reference works describe the emptying of the town directly. The Encyclopaedia Judaica entry on Będzin (via Encyclopedia.com):

"Several thousand Jews from the district were expelled and forced to reside in Bedzin, among them all the Jews from Oswiecim (German name – Auschwitz), who arrived in April–May 1941, prior to the construction of the Auschwitz camp."

https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bedzin

JewishGen's Będzin Census of 1939 page gives the number:

"Several thousand Jews from the district were evacuated in April-May 1941 and forced to live in Będzin, among who were all the Jews, approximately 3,000, from Oswiecim (that is, Auschwitz), before the Auschwitz camp was built."

https://jewishgen.org/databases/Holocaust/0128_Bedzin_census.html#Search

How they were moved — by train, with their belongings. Witness testimony collected by the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum ("Expulsions of the local population of Oświęcim"):

"In 1941, at Easter, at Passover, the complete expulsion of the entire Jewish population of Oświęcim took place. This action lasted more than a week. The community sent out notices. You were allowed to take everything, but as a rule people left the furniture to its fate. The Jews of Oświęcim were resettled to Sosnowiec and Będzin. The wealthy and those who had relatives in Chrzanów tried to get there. The transports went on passenger trains. I was resettled to Sosnowiec."

"With heavy hearts, people loaded their belongings onto freight wagons that took the people and their possessions to Sosnowiec."

https://www.auschwitz.org/en/education/e-learning/podcast/expulsions-of-the-local-population-of-oswiecim/

The order and dates, from a scholarly study of the camp staff and the town (University of Łódź repository): on 26 February 1941 Himmler ordered the deportation of all Jews from Oświęcim and the surrounding area; the action began 9 March 1941, and "Between the 2nd and 7th April, around five thousand Jewish residents of Oświęcim were displaced. They were sent to the ghettos in Sosnowiec, Będzin, and Chrzanów." https://repozytorium.uni.lodz.pl/xmlui/bitstream/handle/11089/44626/243-261_Kita.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y

Lucyna Filip's book (§5, item 1) prints the names of the deportees to the Sosnowiec ghetto in its annexes. The Będzin and Sosnowiec ghettos themselves were liquidated in 1942–43, and their Jews — including the Oświęcim deportees — were sent back to Auschwitz-Birkenau to be murdered.

(c) After the war — survivors come back, then leave

A Jewish Committee was established in Oświęcim in April 1945 and kept a register of survivors who returned:

"The Register of Jewish residents of Oświęcim was run by the local Jewish Committee, which was established in April 1945. The notebook contains the names of several hundred Jews who survived the Holocaust and returned to Oświęcim… Most of the people whose names were included in the register remained in Oświęcim for only a brief period."

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auschwitz_Jewish_Center_in_O%C5%9Bwi%C4%99cim

By September 1945 the count was already down to a remnant:

"All Jews were expelled from Oświęcim in 1941, with most of them being sent to the ghettos in Chrzanów, Sosnowiec, and Będzin… In September 1945, the Jewish community in Oświęcim numbered 186 souls."

https://www.auschwitz.org/en/museum/news/an-invitation-from-the-jewish-center,320.html

The leaving, in a Jewish publication — Ari L. Goldman's "The Last Jew of Auschwitz," CONTACT magazine (Steinhardt Foundation), Summer 2016:

"After the Second World War, only a handful of Jews returned to Oswiecim… By the 1960s, nearly all of the Jews left Oswiecim to settle as new immigrants in Israel."

https://steinhardtfoundation.org/contact/summer_2016/summer_2016-goldman.html

The POLIN Museum's collection page on Szymon Kluger says the same of the 1960s departures:

"…the last remaining Jews were on the point of leaving the town in the face of mounting antisemitism and a growing sense of isolation. They decided to leave Poland for good, joining their families, primarily in the United States and in Israel, and less commonly in select Western European cities."

https://kolekcje.polin.pl/en/auschwitz-cross

The one man who came back against the current was Shimson (Szymon) Kleuger (1925–2000): liberated, taken to Sweden, his brother and sister emigrated to the United States — and in 1961/62 he returned to Oświęcim and found, in the Wikipedia summary of his story, that "all the other surviving Jews had left for America or Israel." He lived in the family house beside the Chevra Lomdei Mishnayot synagogue until his death on 26 May 2000 — the town's last Jewish resident. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szymon_Kluger

The Bricha by name: the organised post-war "Bricha" (flight) movement of survivors toward Palestine ran through Poland, but no source found in this research ties the Bricha by name to Oświęcim specifically — the town's own record is the one above: a brief 1945 return, then a steady leaving for Israel and America until no one was left. Stated plainly, as found.

Method note

Sources were read directly (JewishGen translation pages and table of contents, Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum pages, Virtual Shtetl, POLIN Museum, library catalogues). The one archive that could not be read is the National Library of Israel's Historical Jewish Press (JPress), which refused the connection (HTTP 403) on 2026-11-22; §§2–3 therefore give the exact titles, spellings, and date windows to search there rather than results. James's Chronicling America keyword method (short pairs, word-order swaps, "Jew"/"Jews" forms, long-S variants) applies to JPress the same way when it can be reached.
