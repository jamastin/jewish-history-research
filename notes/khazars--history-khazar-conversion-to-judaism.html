The Khazar Conversion to Judaism — What Happened, When, and How Far It Went

Research file, compiled 2026-11-19. Labels: [PRIMARY] = a period source's own words; [CONSENSUS] = mainstream scholarly position; [DEBATED] = genuinely contested. This is the most argued-over part of Khazar history, so the labels matter more here than anywhere else in this folder.

1. The short version

[CONSENSUS] At some point in the 8th or 9th century, the Khazar khagan, his court, and a portion of the Khazar ruling stratum adopted Judaism — in the end, apparently rabbinic Judaism. It happened in (at least) two stages remembered by the sources: a first royal conversion associated with a king called Bulan, and a later religious reorganization under a king called Obadiah, who is credited with building synagogues and importing Jewish scholars.

[CONSENSUS] There is no sign that Judaism was imposed on the population, and no evidence of religious persecution in Khazaria. Muslims were probably the largest religious group in the cities, Christians second; Jews and Judaized Khazars were a minority — though the Crimean Jewish communities were substantial and influential.

[DEBATED] Almost everything else: the date (proposals run from c. 620 to c. 861), whether Bulan's conversion was private or official, and above all how far down the social scale Judaism reached.

2. The story the Khazars themselves told

The fullest account is King Joseph's Reply to Hasdai ibn Shaprut (c. 954–961). Quoted at length in khazar-primary-sources.md; the bones of it:

  1. Generations after the Khazars' ancestors, King Bulan — "a wise and God-fearing man" — expelled the wizards and idolaters and turned to the one God (in the long version of the Reply, after a dream in which God promised him might and glory, and after victories over the Arabs on the road to Ardabil).
  2. The Byzantine emperor and the Arab caliph, hearing of this, sent envoys with riches and sages to convert him to Christianity and Islam respectively. Bulan also summoned a learned Israelite.
  3. After the sages' disputation deadlocked, Bulan questioned them separately — asking the Christian priest which he preferred, Judaism or Islam, and the Muslim qadi which he preferred, Judaism or Christianity. Each ranked Judaism second only to his own faith — and first above the other's. Bulan declared:

[PRIMARY — King Joseph's Reply] "If this is so, you both have admitted with your own mouths that the religion of the Israelites is better. Therefore trusting in the mercies of God and the power of the Almighty, I choose the religion of Israel, that is, the religion of Abraham…"

  1. Bulan was circumcised, with his court and people (the Reply's phrasing covers the royal circle).
  2. A descendant, King Obadiah, completed the work:

[PRIMARY — King Joseph's Reply] "[Obadiah] reorganized the kingdom and established the Jewish religion properly and correctly. He built synagogues and yeshivot [houses of study], brought in Jewish scholars, and rewarded them with gold and silver. They explained to him the Bible, Mishnah, Talmud and the order of divine services…"

[DEBATED — the literary problem] The disputation scene is a story-pattern, not a transcript. Versions of a ruler choosing among three faiths after a debate appear in many medieval conversion tales (the Rus' Primary Chronicle tells a very similar story about Vladimir of Kiev choosing Christianity in 988). Scholars treat the Bulan narrative as a foundation legend wrapped around a historical core — the debate as staged probably did not happen as described; that a royal conversion happened is supported by independent Muslim and Christian sources (below).

3. The second Khazar account — and it disagrees

The Schechter Letter (anonymous Khazar Jew, mid-10th century) tells a different founding story: Jews from Persia and Armenia migrated into Khazaria and intermarried with the Khazars, and the conversion is credited to a war-leader named Sabriel, who took Judaism under the influence of his wife Serakh, said to descend from the tribe of Simeon. It has no Bulan and no disputation.

[DEBATED] The two Khazar accounts cannot both be literally true. The Schechter version's names (Sabriel is an angel's name in Jewish mystical tradition; Serah is a biblical figure) lead some scholars to class it with pious "lost tribes" literature. Others treat the two texts as rival traditions of different Khazar Jewish circles. Either way: the Khazars' own surviving traditions disagree with each other, which is a fact any honest account has to state.

4. Outside witnesses

The conversion is not known only from Jewish sources — this is the strongest argument that something real happened:

  • Al-Masudi (writing 940s, Meadows of Gold) dates the king's conversion to the caliphate of Harun al-Rashid (786–809) and says Jews then flocked to Khazaria from Muslim lands and Byzantium. His summary of who was Jewish is precise and limited:

[PRIMARY — al-Masudi, as translated in Minorsky] "The Jews are the king (malik), his surrounding (his court) and the Khazars of his clan (gins)."

  • Ibn Fadlan's report (preserved via Yaqut) is broader:

[PRIMARY — Ibn Fadlan] "All Khazars and their king are the Jews."

(He also reports that when the Khazar king heard Muslims had destroyed a synagogue in Dar al-Babunj in 922/923, he ordered the minaret of the mosque in Itil torn down and the muezzins killed, saying he would have destroyed the mosque too "if I did not fear that no synagogue would be left standing in the lands of Islam" — evidence of a king who treated Judaism as his own faith and retaliated in kind.)

  • Al-Fakih and Ibn Khordadbeh likewise say the Khazars "are all Jews who accepted Judaism recently" — "all" here is the loose usage of geographers writing about a state's official identity, and it sits in tension with al-Masudi's careful limitation and with the seven-judges arrangement (two judges judged "the Khazars" by the Torah, while Muslims, Christians, and pagans had their own). Read together, the Muslim sources say: the state's ruling people is officially Jewish; the population is mixed.

  • Christian sources: the earliest Western mention is the Frankish monk Druthmar of Aquitaine (c. 864). The Life of Constantine (St. Cyril) describes Constantine's mission to the Khazar court c. 860/861 and religious disputations there — but, curiously, Byzantine sources as a whole say almost nothing direct about Khazar Judaism, a silence scholars have often remarked on.

  • The coins [DEBATED]: a small issue of Khazar imitation dirhams dated 837/838 bears the legend "Moses is the messenger of God" (adapting the Muslim coin formula). Some scholars take this as hard, dated evidence of an official Judaizing step in the 830s; others caution about reading a whole conversion off one coin type.

5. The date — six positions, honestly laid out

Proposed date Basis
c. 620 The long version of Joseph's Reply implies ~340 years before c. 960. [Rejected by most] — the Reply's own story includes a Muslim qadi, impossible before Islam reached the Caucasus.
c. 740 Yehuda Halevi's Kuzari (written 1140) sets the conversion 400 years before his time; Joseph's Reply links Bulan's turn to victories over the Arabs (cf. Ardabil, 730). The traditional date, repeated in the Jewish Encyclopedia and in Koestler.
786–809 Al-Masudi's dating to Harun al-Rashid's caliphate.
799–809 (as the official step) Omeljan Pritsak's two-stage model: Bulan adopts Judaism privately c. 730–740; the beg Obadiah makes it official 799–809.
830s The "Moses is the messenger of God" coins of 837/838.
c. 861 Constantin Zuckerman's redating, tying the (official) conversion to the period of Constantine/Cyril's Khazar mission and re-dating the Rus' kings' chronology in the Schechter Letter.

[CONSENSUS, such as it is] Most current specialists work with a process rather than an event: first royal steps in the later 8th century, institutional/rabbinic consolidation in the 9th. Dan Shapira's summary (Encyclopaedia Iranica): the c. 740 date "rests mainly on a twelfth-century literary work and is weaker; a ninth-century date (c. 800–861) fits the evidence best."

[The skeptical position] A minority of scholars — most prominently Moshe Gil, and Shaul Stampfer (2014, "Did the Khazars Convert to Judaism?") — argue the conversion of even the elite is a legend that grew in the telling: no contemporary Khazar source, contradictory second-hand accounts, no clear archaeological signature (e.g., no identified ritual baths at Sarkel). This is a real scholarly position, not a crank one — but it remains a minority against the combined weight of the Muslim, Jewish, and Latin witnesses listed above. It should be reported, not hidden.

6. The extent — the heart of the argument

For a limited, elite conversion (the majority view): - Al-Masudi's own wording limits Judaism to "the king, his court and the Khazars of his clan." - The seven-judges system implies large non-Jewish populations under their own laws. - No sign Judaism was ever a missionary state religion; the khaganate's tax base and army depended on Muslim, Christian, and pagan subjects left unmolested. - Kevin Alan Brook's often-cited estimate puts the Judaized Khazar elite/core at perhaps 30,000–40,000 people — a ruling stratum, not a nation. (An estimate, not a measurement.) - Archaeology has produced Khazar-period burials and artifacts with occasional Jewish symbols, but nothing like mass Judaization of the steppe population.

For a broader conversion: - Ibn Fadlan, al-Fakih, and Ibn Khordadbeh say flatly "all the Khazars" are Jews. - Joseph's Reply describes Obadiah's program — synagogues, schools, imported scholars, instruction in Bible, Mishnah, and Talmud — which reads like more than a palace cult. - The Kievan Letter shows an organized, Hebrew-literate Jewish community in Kiev with Turkic-named members (whether ethnic Khazars or Jews under Khazar rule is itself debated). - The Crimean Jewish communities were old, large, and closely tied to Khazaria.

[CONSENSUS verdict, fairly stated] Judaism in Khazaria was real, official at the top, rabbinic in form by the 10th century, and centered on the king, court, and the Khazar tribal core around the capital — with genuine Jewish communities (of mixed origin: immigrants from Byzantium, Persia, and the Caliphate, plus converts) in the towns. Whether ordinary Khazar herdsmen across the steppe became Jews in any numbers is not supported by the evidence we have.

7. Why Judaism? The usual explanations — all inferences

No Khazar source explains the motive. Historians infer:

  1. Neutrality: caught between the Christian Byzantine Empire and the Muslim Caliphate, adopting either faith meant subordination to a rival. Judaism — respected by both as an older revelation, owned by neither empire — preserved independence. (The most common explanation; note it is an inference from geopolitics, not a documented motive.)
  2. Prestige and antiquity: Judaism offered a scriptural, monotheistic, law-based identity older than both rivals' faiths — the logic the Bulan story itself dramatizes.
  3. Existing Jewish presence: Jewish merchants (Radhanites), refugees from Byzantine persecution, and Crimean communities were already inside Khazaria and at court.
  4. The 737 trauma: Marwan's forced conversion of the khagan to Islam (immediately renounced) may have pushed the elite toward a definitive non-Muslim monotheism.
