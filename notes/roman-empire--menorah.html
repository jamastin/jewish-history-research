The menorah — the Temple's golden lampstand

Researched 2026-11-19 — search list item 5: "What is the menorah?". Picture already in this folder: images/menorah-arch-of-titus.jpg — the Arch of Titus relief, Rome. Sources: Exodus 25:31–40 and 37:17–24; 1 Kings 7; 1 Maccabees 1 and 4; Josephus (Antiquities 3.139–146; Jewish War 5 and 7); Procopius (Wars, Vandalic War).

What it was

  • The menorah was the great lampstand of the Tabernacle and then of the Temple in Jerusalem: seven branches, three curving up on each side of a central stem, every branch topped with a lamp.
  • Its pattern is given in Exodus as shown to Moses on the mountain: "And thou shalt make a candlestick of pure gold: of beaten work shall the candlestick be made" (Exodus 25:31). It was hammered from a single piece of gold — a talent of it, per Exodus 25:39 (a talent is roughly 30 kg; the figure is the Bible's own).
  • Every surface was worked with almond-blossom decoration — cups, buds and blossoms along the branches (Exodus 25:33–34). Josephus, who had seen the Second Temple's menorah, counts seventy ornaments in all and reads them as symbols of the sun, moon and planets.
  • Size: no ancient text gives its height directly. From the scale of the figure carrying it on the Arch of Titus relief, scholars usually estimate about five feet (roughly 1.5–1.6 m) — flag that as an estimate from the carving, not a measurement from a text.

The lamps

  • The seven lamps burned olive oil, tended by the priests morning and evening; the westernmost lamp was kept burning continually — the "perpetual light" (Exodus 27:20–21; Leviticus 24). To stand before it was to stand in the Holy Place, outside the veil of the Holy of Holies, alongside the table of showbread and the altar of incense — the three great furnishings of the sanctuary.

First Temple, Second Temple

  • Solomon's Temple had the menorah — and more: 1 Kings 7:49 lists ten lampstands of gold, five on each side. The single seven-branched menorah belongs to the Tabernacle pattern and to the Second Temple.
  • Babylon carried off the Temple vessels in 586 BC; whatever Solomon's gold became, the trail of that first set ends there.
  • The Second Temple's menorah had its own wounds: Antiochus IV looted it in 169 BC (1 Maccabees 1:21–23), and the Maccabees, cleansing the Temple in 164 BC, "made… a new candlestick" among the restored vessels (1 Maccabees 4:49–50). Herod's rebuilt Temple housed a menorah of the same pattern — the one Rome took.
  • Pompey saw it in 63 BC and, famously, left it untouched. Titus did not.

Carried to Rome (AD 71)

  • After Jerusalem fell in 70, the menorah was carried in the joint triumph of Vespasian and Titus through Rome — Josephus lists it among the spoils, "made wholly of gold." The Arch of Titus (c. AD 81) carved the procession in stone: soldiers shouldering the menorah on its stepped base, the silver trumpets beside it. That carving — the photo in this folder — is the most famous picture of the menorah ever made, and the model for the menorah in Israel's modern state emblem.
  • Josephus says Vespasian deposited the Temple treasures in his new Temple of Peace in Rome (dedicated AD 75). A second-century tradition has Rabbi Simeon ben Yohai seeing the menorah in Rome. The Temple of Peace burned in AD 192; the treasures were evidently rescued or moved, because the trail continues — but from here on, honestly, it runs on one late source.

The trail after Rome — and where it goes cold

  • AD 455 — Carthage. The Vandals under Genseric sacked Rome for two weeks and shipped its treasures to Carthage. Procopius, our source, says the Jewish Temple treasures Titus had brought to Rome were among what the Vandals held.
  • AD 534 — Constantinople. Justinian's general Belisarius destroyed the Vandal kingdom and brought the recovered treasure to Constantinople, where it was paraded in his triumph — Procopius: "and among these were the treasures of the Jews, which Titus, the son of Vespasian… had brought to Rome after the capture of Jerusalem."
  • The curse, and the sending back. Procopius tells that a Jew warned Justinian the treasures had ruined every place that held them since they left Jerusalem — and that Justinian, frightened, sent them back to Jerusalem, to the sanctuaries of the Christians there (later tradition names the Nea Church). Whether they truly arrived, and what exactly was among them, Procopius does not itemise — flag the detail as his story, not an inventory.
  • Then silence. Persians sacked Jerusalem in 614 and Arabs took it in 637/638; either upheaval could have swallowed the treasures. No source after Procopius reliably reports seeing the Temple menorah anywhere.

The legends — labelled as legends

  • The Vatican holds it: a persistent later story that the menorah lies in Vatican storerooms. No evidence supports it; the Vatican has repeatedly said it does not have it.
  • The river Tiber: a Roman folk tale that it was thrown or lost in the Tiber.
  • Buried in Jerusalem: various tales of it hidden before one of the city's falls.
  • All three are legends. The honest end of the record is Procopius, in the 530s, and after that — nothing verifiable.
