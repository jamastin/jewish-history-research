The Khazar Empire — Rise, Territory, Trade, Wars, and Fall

Map of the Khazar khaganate, about 820. Source: Wikimedia Commons

Research file, compiled 2026-11-19. Labels used throughout: [PRIMARY] = words from a period source (quoted exactly, in translation); [CONSENSUS] = mainstream scholarly position; [DEBATED] = scholars disagree. Nothing in the album files was changed by this research.

1. Who the Khazars were

[CONSENSUS] The Khazars were a semi-nomadic Turkic people. Their state grew out of the breakup of the Western Turkic (Göktürk) Khaganate in the mid-7th century. They probably spoke an Oghur (Lir-Turkic) language, related to Bulgar and modern Chuvash, but almost no words of Khazar survive — there is no surviving record written in the Khazar language except possibly one word on the Kievan Letter (see khazar-primary-sources.md). The khaganate was never ethnically uniform: it ruled Alans, Bulgars, Magyars, Slavic tribes, Burtas, Crimean Goths, and others.

Around the 650s–670s the Khazars defeated Old Great Bulgaria (Khan Kubrat's state); some Bulgars moved north and became the Volga Bulgars, who later paid tribute to the Khazars.

2. Territory at its height

[CONSENSUS] For roughly three centuries (c. 650–965) the Khazars dominated the steppe between the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea. At maximum extent, Khazaria included:

  • southern Russia and eastern Ukraine,
  • the northern Caucasus,
  • the Crimea (most of it, outside Byzantine Cherson),
  • western Kazakhstan and northwestern Uzbekistan,
  • control reaching up the Volga and west to the Dnieper.

One modern summary (Roman K. Kovalev, Encyclopedia of Russian History): at its height in the first half of the 9th century, Khazar power ran "from the middle Dnieper to the Volga–Ural steppe and from the middle Volga to the Crimea."

So completely was the Caspian associated with them that it is still called the "Khazar Sea" in Arabic, Persian, and Turkish usage (Bahr al-Khazar).

Capitals

  • Balanjar — early capital in the north Caucasus foothills (Dagestan).
  • Samandar (Semender) — on the Caspian coast of Dagestan, moved there in the 720s under Arab pressure.
  • Itil (Atil) — from about 750, in the Volga delta near modern Astrakhan; remained the capital for 200+ years. Arabic geographers describe a twin city on both banks of the Volga: the western part (Itil proper) held the khagan's brick palace and court; the eastern part (Khazaran) was the trading town. Itil's exact site has never been confirmed — everything said about it rests on texts, not excavation; the Samosdelka site near Astrakhan is a leading candidate. [DEBATED: the identification of the site]

Sarkel

The fortress of Sarkel ("White House / White Abode") on the Don River, built in 834/835 with Byzantine engineers and materials sent by Emperor Theophilus, guarded the Volga–Don portage — the choke point where goods were carried overland between the two river systems. It was the one famous stone/brick structure in a largely stoneless steppe.

3. Government: the dual kingship

[PRIMARY/CONSENSUS] Khazaria was ruled by a dual kingship (diarchy):

  • The Khagan — a sacred, largely ceremonial ruler, hedged about with ritual (Arab writers report he was shown to the public rarely, and one report gives his reign a fixed term of forty years, after which he was to be killed — a report scholars treat cautiously).
  • The Beg (Bek) — the deputy who actually governed, commanded the army, and administered justice.

Ibn Fadlan's report preserves the ritual atmosphere around the khagan; al-Masudi describes the standing army maintained by the king — he says 7,000 (al-Istakhri says 12,000) armored archers and lancers rode with the king, and that no other king in that region kept a regular standing army.

4. Trade — the economic base of Khazar power

[CONSENSUS] The khaganate sat where the Volga trade route crossed the northern branches of the Silk Road. Goods moving between Scandinavia, the Rus' lands, Baghdad, Central Asia, and Byzantium passed through Khazar customs posts.

  • The Khazars charged a tithe (about 10%) on goods passing through Itil.
  • Southbound: furs, honey, wax, amber, swords, and slaves, brought by Rus' and Volga Bulgar traders.
  • Northbound: huge quantities of Arab silver dirhams — the coin hoards of Scandinavia and Russia are the archaeological footprint of this trade.
  • Jewish Radhanite merchants, Muslim traders from Khwarazm, and Rus' boatmen all operated under what is sometimes called the Pax Khazarica.

Religious pluralism was built into the system. Al-Masudi (writing in the 940s) describes the capital's courts in his Meadows of Gold:

[PRIMARY — al-Masudi] "The custom in the Khazar capital is to have seven judges. Of these two are for the Muslims, two are for the Khazars, judging according to the Torah (Mosaic law), two for the Christians, judging according to the Gospel and one for the Saqualibah [Slavs], Rus and other pagans, judging according to pagan law…. In his [the Khazar King's] city are many Muslims, merchants and craftsmen, who have come to his country because of his justice and the security which he offers."

There is no evidence of the Khazars engaging in religious persecution, before or after the conversion to Judaism.

5. Wars and relations

The Arab–Khazar wars (c. 642–799)

[CONSENSUS] The Umayyad Caliphate's attempt to push through the Caucasus produced roughly a century of war, usually divided into the First Arab–Khazar War (c. 642–652) and the Second (c. 722–737), with raiding before, between, and after.

  • 642–652: Arab forces took Derbent (the Caspian Gates pass) but were defeated outside Balanjar in 652, where the Arab commander Abd ar-Rahman ibn Rabi'a was killed.
  • 730 — Battle of Ardabil: a Khazar army under Barjik, a son of the khagan, invaded deep into the Caliphate, defeated and killed the Arab governor al-Jarrah ibn Abdallah al-Hakami, and briefly took Ardabil in northwestern Iran — one of the worst defeats the Umayyads ever suffered from the Khazars. Barjik was killed the next year near Mosul.
  • 737 — Marwan's campaign: Marwan ibn Muhammad (later Caliph Marwan II) marched a huge army all the way to the Volga, briefly reached Itil, and forced the khagan to accept Islam and submission. As soon as the Arab army withdrew, the khagan gave both up. The 737 campaign ended large-scale war: Derbent remained the northernmost outpost of Islam, and the Caucasus line between the Caliphate and Khazaria held. Some historians see the forced conversion of 737 as the immediate background against which the Khazar elite later turned to Judaism — choosing a faith that subordinated them to neither Baghdad nor Constantinople. [Interpretation, widely held but an inference]
  • The last major clash came in 799/800, when a Khazar invasion of Azerbaijan and Arran was driven back.

Net result [CONSENSUS]: the Khazars did to the Arab advance in the Caucasus what Tours/Poitiers (732) did in the west — they stopped it, and held the line for a century.

Byzantium — ally, in-law, and rival

[CONSENSUS] For most of its history Khazaria was Byzantium's most important northern ally, against Persia first and then the Caliphate:

  • 627: Khazar/Turkic forces under the commander the Byzantines called Ziebel campaigned with Emperor Heraclius against Sasanian Persia.
  • 695–711 era: the deposed Emperor Justinian II took refuge with the Khazars and married a Khazar princess, who took the name Theodora.
  • 732: to seal the alliance against the Umayyads, Emperor Leo III married his son, the future Constantine V, to the Khazar princess Tzitzak, daughter of Khagan Bihar. Baptized Irene, she brought the Khazar ceremonial robe — the tzitzakion — into Constantinopolitan court fashion. Her son, born 25 January 750, reigned as Emperor Leo IV "the Khazar" (775–780). A Byzantine emperor was thus half-Khazar.
  • 834/835: Byzantium sent engineers to build Sarkel for the Khazars.
  • The alliance frayed in the early 900s over Crimea and Cherson, and by the 940s Byzantium was working against Khazaria — the Schechter Letter says Emperor Romanus I Lecapenus bribed a Rus' prince to attack the Khazars (see khazar-primary-sources.md).

Kievan Rus' — tribute-payer turned destroyer

[PRIMARY — Russian Primary Chronicle] In the 9th century the East Slavic tribes — Polianians, Severians, Viatichians, Radimichians — paid tribute to the Khazars (the Chronicle preserves the famous scene of the Polianians paying a sword per household). Kiev itself was founded in the Khazar orbit and had a Khazar Jewish community; the Kievan Letter (c. 930) is a Hebrew document of that community.

Through the 10th century the Rus' princes chipped away: Oleg and Igor campaigned against Khazar interests, Rus' fleets raided through Khazar territory to the Caspian (921–922 era raids; the great raid of 943/944), and Rus' traders increasingly bypassed Khazar customs.

6. The fall (964–969)

[CONSENSUS on the outcome; DEBATED on dates and sequence] The end came at the hands of Sviatoslav I of Kiev, with Oghuz (and possibly Pecheneg) allies, in a campaign or series of campaigns between 965 and 969:

  • The Russian Primary Chronicle, under the year 965, says:

[PRIMARY — Primary Chronicle] "Svyatoslav sallied forth against the Khazars. When they heard of his approach, they went out to meet him with their Prince, the Kagan, and the armies came to blows. When battle thus took place, Svyatoslav defeated the Khazars and took their city of Bela Vezha [Sarkel]. He also conquered the Yasians and the Kasogians."

  • The Chronicle never mentions Itil. For the capital's fate we depend on the Arab geographer Ibn Hawqal, who dates the Rus' sack of Bulgar, Khazaran, Samandar, and Itil to AH 358 = 968/969. Ibn Miskawayh dates a Rus'/Oghuz attack to AH 354 (965).
  • A visitor's line preserved in the Muslim accounts sums up the sack of Itil:

[PRIMARY — quoted in Arabic sources] "The Rus attacked, and no grape or raisin remained, not a leaf on a branch."

  • Muslim sources also claim that after the disaster the Khazar ruler accepted Islam and Khwarazmian overlordship in exchange for help — a rump Khazar state may have limped on a few years. The end of the khaganate as a power is usually set in 969.

Why it fell [DEBATED]: the standard political story (Byzantine–Khazar estrangement, Rus' military rise) is now supplemented by economic arguments — notably Alex M. Feldman's case (2022) that the collapse of the Islamic silver-dirham flow undermined the khaganate's ability to pay its soldiers. Treat the economics thesis as a serious recent argument, not settled fact.

7. Aftermath — what happened to the Khazars

[CONSENSUS] Khazaria did not vanish overnight, but it disintegrated:

  • 1016: a joint Byzantine–Rus' fleet subdued a remaining "Khazaria" in the Crimea/Taman region ruled by Georgios Tzoulos — often treated as the last Khazar polity.
  • 1023–1083: Khazars still appear in Rus' sources as military auxiliaries in Rus' princely conflicts.
  • 1106: after one more clash with the Cumans (Polovtsi), the Khazars fade from the historical record as a distinct people.
  • Survivors were absorbed into the peoples around them — the successor steppe powers (Pechenegs, Oghuz, Cuman-Kipchaks), Kievan Rus', and the towns of the Crimea and Caucasus.

Claims of Khazar descent [DEBATED / mostly rejected by specialists]: Khazar ancestry has been claimed for, among others, the Hungarians (the Kabar tribes who joined the Magyars are a documented case of Khazar tribal absorption, per Constantine Porphyrogenitus), Kazakhs, Kumyks, Cossacks, Mountain Jews, the Subbotniks — and the Crimean Karaites and Krymchaks.

On the Crimean claims specifically, be careful:

  • Some Crimean Karaites have claimed Khazar origin; specialists in Khazar history question the connection, and scholarship is likewise sceptical that the Tatar-speaking Krymchak Jews descend from Khazars.
  • Much of the "evidence" once used for a Crimean Khazar kingdom came from the 19th-century Karaite collector Abraham Firkovich — and the documents and tombstone inscriptions he produced are now regarded as forgeries (Dan Shapira's work is the standard exposure). Graetz and Dubnow repeated some of this material before it was debunked, which is why it still circulates.
  • Petachiah of Regensburg, traveling in the late 12th century through what he called "Khazaria," found only minim (sectaries — apparently Karaites) "living amidst desolation in perpetual mourning." That is a traveler's impression, not proof of descent.

Honest bottom line: individual Khazars and Khazar groups were certainly absorbed into neighboring peoples — that is what happens to defeated steppe confederations — but no modern people has demonstrated descent from the Khazars, and the Crimean Karaite/Krymchak link in particular rests substantially on forged 19th-century material.
