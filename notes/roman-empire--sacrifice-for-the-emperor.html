The sacrifice for the emperor

Researched 2026-11-19 for James's search list (item 2: "What was the sacrifice for the emperor?"). Sources: Philo (Embassy to Gaius 157, 317, 356–357), Josephus (Jewish War 2.197, 2.409–417; Against Apion 2.77), Ezra 6 for the older pattern.

What it was

  • Twice every day in the Jerusalem Temple, alongside the regular daily offerings, the priests offered a sacrifice for Caesar and the Roman people: one bull and two lambs, burnt whole on the altar (Philo, Embassy 157 and 317; Josephus, War 2.197).
  • It ran for roughly a century — set up under Augustus at the start of Roman rule (the practice of sacrificing for a foreign ruler was older still: Ezra 6:8–10 has Darius funding offerings "for the life of the king and his sons").

The key distinction: FOR him, not TO him

  • The sacrifice was offered to the God of Israel, on the emperor's behalf — for his health, his reign, and Rome's welfare. It was never offered to the emperor as a god.
  • This was the great compromise of Roman Judea. Everywhere else in the empire, subject peoples proved loyalty by sacrificing to the emperor's image. Jews could not do that without breaking their Law — so Rome accepted this instead: the emperor's name honoured daily in the Temple, without emperor worship. Josephus stresses how unusual the concession was.
  • Philo records the emperor Caligula himself spotting the difference and hating it: "You have not sacrificed to me" — which is why Caligula's demand for a statue in the Temple (AD 40) was such a crisis. The compromise only worked while Rome let it.

Who paid for it? The sources disagree — honestly flagged

  • Philo says the offerings were made at the emperor's expense — Augustus himself endowed them.
  • Josephus (Against Apion 2.77) says they were offered at the common expense of all the Jews — i.e., out of Temple funds raised from the Jewish people.
  • Both cannot be simply right as stated. Modern scholars split the difference: the money may have come from imperial funds drawn on the province's own taxes, or the endowment lapsed into Temple funds over the century. What is not disputed: the animals were offered daily, in the emperor's name, for about a hundred years.

How it ended — and why Josephus calls it the true beginning of the war

  • In the summer of AD 66, after Florus's massacres, Eleazar son of Ananias — captain of the Temple, and himself the son of a former high priest — persuaded the priests to accept no gift or sacrifice from any foreigner at all. That ended the sacrifice for Caesar at a stroke.
  • The chief priests and leading Pharisees begged them to reconsider, pointing out that the Temple had always accepted foreign offerings. Eleazar's party held the Temple mount and would not move.
  • Josephus is blunt about what the act meant: stopping the sacrifice was a formal, public withdrawal of loyalty — the ancient equivalent of tearing up the treaty. He writes that this decision laid "the foundation of the war with the Romans" (War 2.409–417). Battles had already happened; this was the moment peace became impossible, because Rome could read it only one way: rebellion, declared in God's house itself.
