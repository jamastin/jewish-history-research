The rival Jewish groups — Pharisees and Sadducees

Researched 2026-11-19 for James's search list (item 4: "What were the two rival Jewish groups, and what were the differences between them?"). Sources: Josephus (Jewish War 2.162–166; Antiquities 13.171–173, 18.12–17), Acts 23:6–8, the Gospels, Jewish Encyclopedia.

The two main rivals

Josephus, who was himself a Pharisee, lays out the parties of his day like schools of philosophy. Two of them ran Jewish life between them in the century before the war — and they disagreed about almost everything that mattered.

The Sadducees

  • Who they were: the priestly aristocracy — the families who ran the Temple, including many chief priests, plus wealthy landowners. Their name is usually traced to Zadok, the high priest of Solomon's time.
  • What they believed:
  • Only the written Torah (the five books of Moses) bound them. The Pharisees' oral tradition — generations of interpretation handed down by teachers — they rejected as man-made.
  • No resurrection, no afterlife, no immortal soul — when a man died, that was the end of him.
  • No angels or spirits as the Pharisees taught them (Acts 23:8 states the contrast flatly: "The Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, and no angel, nor spirit: but the Pharisees confess both").
  • Free will, nearly total: God does not intervene; men choose good or evil and take the consequences.
  • In court they were the harsher party — strict, literal penalties.
  • Their power base: the Temple and the high priesthood. Everything they were stood on that building.

The Pharisees

  • Who they were: a movement of scholars and teachers, mostly not priests — the rabbis' ancestors. Their name likely means "the separated ones," set apart by their strict observance.
  • What they believed:
  • Torah plus the oral tradition — the interpretations handed down from teacher to teacher, which they held were given alongside the written Law. This let the Law adapt to daily life.
  • Resurrection of the dead and a world to come, with reward and punishment after death.
  • Angels and spirits are real.
  • Fate and free will together: some things are God's doing, but a man's choices are his own.
  • Their strictness ran the other way from the Sadducees': they were the more lenient party in judgements, and far more popular.
  • Their power base: the synagogues, the schools, and the people. Josephus says the masses followed them, and even Sadducee officials had to rule by Pharisee interpretations in public or face the crowd.

Head to head

Question Sadducees Pharisees
Scripture Written Torah only Torah + oral tradition
After death Nothing — no resurrection Resurrection, reward and punishment
Angels/spirits Denied Affirmed
God's hand vs man's Man is on his own Both — God ordains, man chooses
Who Priests, aristocracy, the rich Scholars, teachers, the common people
Power centre The Temple Synagogue and school

Paul, himself a Pharisee, used the split as a courtroom weapon: on trial before the council he cried out that he was being judged "concerning the hope and resurrection of the dead" — and the Pharisees and Sadducees fell to arguing with each other instead of trying him (Acts 23:6–10).

What happened to each after AD 70

  • The Sadducees vanished. When Titus burned the Temple, their whole world — priesthood, sacrifices, their office and income — burned with it. Within a generation they simply disappear from history.
  • The Pharisees became Judaism. With the Temple gone, the Pharisees' portable religion — prayer, study, synagogue, a Law interpreted for daily life — was the version that could survive anywhere. Their oral tradition was written down as the Mishnah (c. AD 200) and grew into the Talmud. Rabbinic Judaism, the Judaism of the last two thousand years, descends from them.

The other two, briefly — to complete the picture

  • The Essenes: a separatist movement of perhaps 4,000 (Josephus) who held property in common, prized ritual purity and celibacy (mostly), believed in the soul's immortality and a strong fate, and withdrew from a Temple they considered corrupt. The Dead Sea Scrolls community at Qumran is widely — though not universally — identified with them. They too disappear after the war.
  • The Zealots and Sicarii: not a theology but the armed resistance. Josephus calls their root idea the "fourth philosophy" (Judas the Galilean, AD 6): no master but God — paying Rome's tax was slavery and apostasy. The Sicarii were the knife-men of that movement, named for the sica, the curved dagger they used to assassinate collaborators in crowds. It was Sicarii who held Masada (see masada.md), and Zealot factions who fought Rome — and each other — inside besieged Jerusalem.
