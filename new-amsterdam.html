New Amsterdam, 1654 — The 23

The first Jewish community in North America arrived on one boat — and the old papers remembered it. Researched 2026-11-22.

In September 1654, twenty-three Jews — men, women, and children — reached New Amsterdam from Recife, Brazil, aboard the St. Catrina, after the Portuguese retook Recife and the refugees were robbed by Spaniards on the way north. Governor Peter Stuyvesant asked to expel them; the Dutch West India Company overruled him in April 1655 and let them stay. (Jacob Barsimson had arrived separately, weeks earlier, on the Peereboom.)

The newspaper source — found

Evening Star (Washington, D.C.), Sunday, May 21, 1933, page 73 — a book review that retells the arrival:

"Her ancestors, Spanish Jews, victims of the persecution of the Inquisition, went to Holland, England, and, finally, to the Dutch colony of New Amsterdam. In September, 1654, a band of 23 Jews came over on the St. Charles. 'Surely the descendants of these Jewish Pilgrims are entitled to consider themselves 100 per cent Americans equally with the descendants of the Mayflower Pilgrims.'"

Evening Star, May 21, 1933, p. 73

Read the page at the Library of Congress

Two honest cautions: the paper's text names the ship "St. Charles" where historians usually say St. Catrina / Sainte Catherine — the printed word should be checked against the page image before citing either way. And this item matches the boat, the 23, and September 1654 exactly, but it does not mention anyone hiding or changing names. That part of the story is still unverified.

Changed names, hidden names — what the papers show

Four finds from Chronicling America, verified on the scanned pages:

1852 — "Old Rothschild"

The Grand River Times (Grand Haven, Mich.), September 1, 1852: Isaac changed his name to D' Isaacs to rid himself of "the stigma of being known as a Jew" — and Rothschild answers, "Mister Isaacs may change his name, but he cannot change his nose." A period joke at the changer's expense.

Grand River Times, Sept. 1, 1852 — Library of Congress

1909 — "Styles in Names"

The Evening Star, February 28, 1909, tells three in one article: David Levy became David Levy Yulee and re-entered Congress as a Senator; Sidney Lee, "having been born Lazarus, made the change when he entered Oxford"; and Francis Palgrave, son of Meyer Cohen, changed his name by royal permission in 1823.

Evening Star, Feb. 28, 1909 — Library of Congress

1912 — "Was Columbus a Jew?"

The Daily Kennebec Journal (Augusta, Maine), August 6, 1912: "Columbus had changed his name and concealed his true religion, the rabbi declared, because in those days his race was persecuted." The closest find yet to the hidden-identity memory — Columbus, not the boat group.

Daily Kennebec Journal, Aug. 6, 1912 — Library of Congress

1949 — the change in reverse

The Phoenix Jewish News, April 8, 1949: "Why Does a Man Change His Name?" — Ambassador Eliahu Elath, born Epstein, Hebraized his name to be known as a Jew, not to hide it.

Phoenix Jewish News, Apr. 8, 1949 — Library of Congress
