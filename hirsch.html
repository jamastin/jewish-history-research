Baron Hirsch & the Colonies

Baron Maurice de Hirsch (born December 9, 1831, Munich; died April 21, 1896, at Ógyalla, Hungary) made a fortune in railroads — Turkish, Russian and Austrian lines — and turned to philanthropy. In 1891 he set up the Baron de Hirsch Fund in the United States for Jewish immigrants from Russia and Romania (the papers of the day put the fund at $2,400,000–$2,500,000 — they disagreed, and each figure is quoted as printed), and founded the Jewish Colonization Association (JCA) the same year, to settle Russian Jews as farmers — above all in Argentina.

After his only son died in 1887 he is quoted: “My son I have lost, but not my heir; humanity is my heir.”

In his own words

“What I desire to accomplish... is to give a portion of my companions in faith the possibility of finding a new existence, primarily as farmers and also as handicraftsmen, in those lands where the laws and religious tolerance permit them to carry on the struggle for existence as noble and responsible subjects of a humane government.”

“My Views on Philanthropy,” North American Review, July 1891.

The four colonies of the 1894 report

An 1895 newspaper summary of the JCA's 1894 report says four colonies were established — 200,000 acres, 563 families — but does not name them. Identified from the Jewish Encyclopedia and JewishGen records (a strong reconstruction, labelled as such in the research files):

  1. Moïseville (Moisés Ville), Santa Fe — the oldest; founded 1890 by Russian immigrants who arrived on the Weser in August 1889, reorganized by the JCA in 1891.
  2. Mauricio, Buenos Aires province — set apart in 1891, named for the Baron himself; about 62,000 acres.
  3. Lucienville, Entre Ríos — founded October 1893, named for the Baron's son Lucien, who died in 1887.
  4. Clara, Entre Ríos — established 1894, named for Baroness Clara; its “ten groups, of about forty families each” match the 1895 article's 1894 settlement figures one for one.
