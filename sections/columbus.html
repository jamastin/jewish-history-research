Columbus — Was He Jewish?

The century-old claim that Columbus was a Jew who changed his name and hid his faith — the papers that printed it, the Spanish historian behind it, the 2024 DNA announcement, his parents, the Inquisition world behind the whole question, and his diary in Spanish and English. Researched 2026-11-22.

What the papers printed — 1912

The claim was in James's newspapers by 1912. The Daily Kennebec Journal (Augusta, Maine), August 6, 1912, under the headline "WAS COLUMBUS A JEW?":

"His true name, he said, was Christoval Colon, which was a Jewish name. Columbus had changed his name and concealed his true religion, the rabbi declared, because in those days his race was persecuted."

Daily Kennebec Journal, Aug. 6, 1912, p. 11

Chicago's Jewish weekly The Sentinel worked the same claim over on July 19, 1912 — and argued against it: "…that the faith, which was thus to be spread, was the Jewish one there is not a scintilla of evidence."

Daily Kennebec Journal, Aug. 6, 1912 — Library of Congress

The Spanish historian

Salvador de Madariaga, in his Christopher Columbus (1940), argued that Columbus's parents were Spanish Jews who had departed for Genoa in the face of persecution and "remained faithful to the language of their country of origin" — on that reading, the Genoese Cristoforo Colombo was the cover identity of a Spaniard whose real name and tongue were Cristóbal Colón and Castilian. An earlier Spanish claimant, Celso García de la Riega, argued from 1898 for a Jewish Colón family of Pontevedra, Galicia; the American historian Henry Vignaud refuted him in 1913.

The 2024 DNA announcement — Spanish and Italian news

On October 12, 2024, Spain's RTVE aired "Colón ADN," presenting José Antonio Lorente's University of Granada study: DNA from the Seville remains and from Columbus's son Hernando showed "traits compatible with Jewish origin," and the team concluded Columbus was a Sephardic Jew from the Valencia coast. Italy's ANSA wire carried it the next day: "Cristoforo Colombo non era genovese ma spagnolo e ebreo" — and carried the pushback too: specialists noted the documentary "does not at any moment show Columbus's DNA." Status: the study has never been published in a peer-reviewed journal and its data has not been released; the claim is not accepted as settled.

The name question

Nobody disputes that Cristoforo Colombo of Genoa became Cristóbal Colón in Spanish service — names were routinely translated in that century — and that he signed himself "Xpo Ferens," Christ-bearer. The disputed claim is the stronger one: that the change was a concealment. His surviving writing is in Spanish and Latin, essentially none in Italian — the fact Madariaga built on. His own 1498 deed of entail names Genoa as his homeland.

His parents — Domenico and Susanna

The documented record: Domenico Colombo, a Genoese master weaver, in the notarial archives in ordinary detail; Susanna Fontanarossa of the Bisagno valley, named in a Genoa deed as his wife. The claims: that a Jewish Colón and Fonterossa of Pontevedra were the real parents (Vignaud, 1913: "the documents… establish nothing of the kind"); that Susanna's Hebrew name is itself evidence (it was also a normal Christian name in Liguria); and Madariaga's Spanish-converso parents, for whom no document has ever been produced. The 2024 study named and tested neither parent.

The Spanish Inquisition — the world behind the question

Why would anyone hide a name? The 1391 massacres drove mass conversions; the converts' descendants — conversos — rose, and were then hunted by the Inquisition founded in 1478 under Ferdinand and Isabella, with "purity of blood" rules from 1449. Columbus sailed for those monarchs, financed with converso help (Luis de Santángel, Gabriel Sánchez), with a converso interpreter, Luis de Torres — and he left on August 3, 1492, the morning after the expulsion of Spain's Jews took effect.

The Diary — original and translated

Columbus's original diary is lost; what survives is Las Casas's abstract of it, part quotation, part summary. The full file sets the key passages side by side — the Spanish and the English — including the prologue on the expulsion ("So after having expelled the Jews from your dominions, Your Highnesses, in the same month of January, ordered me to proceed with a sufficient armament to the said regions of India") and the diary's own description of his interpreter Torres: "avía sido judío" — "had been a Jew."

Record vs. claim

The Jewish-Columbus theory is a real, documented, century-old claim with named claimants — and James's papers were printing it by 1912. It remains a minority claim, not the historians' consensus: the Genoese documents, and Columbus's own naming of Genoa, stand on the other side, and the 2024 DNA announcement has not changed that status.
