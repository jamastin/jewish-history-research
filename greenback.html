The Greenback Find

“THE NEW DEPARTURE” — The Anti-Monopolist, Saint Paul, Minnesota, April 4, 1878

One clipping in the collection showed a fragment of old print about greenbacks and gold. The question was simple: where did it actually come from? After a long hunt through books and pamphlets that turned out not to be the source, the passage was found — verified in the page's own text — in a Saint Paul newspaper of 1878, written by Stephen D. Dillaye.

The passage, exactly as printed

“The Greenback is the new departure. It is America for America. It is the American financial policy. It is the people for the people. It is the nation saying to all its enemies, ‘hands off, this is our money. Gold is good, but it is scarce, foreigners control it. Greenbacks are better. They are from the people for the people. They are the nation for the nation. They are Americans.’ To be free, a nation must control its own money. To be slaves to gold is to be slaves to the Rothchilds and German Jews. With Greenbacks for our currency and a money policy of our own free from all banking interference, (and above and beyond all foreign interference) which shall furnish an adequate supply for a perfect equality of right, such will give labor its reward and industry its recompense—this is the American policy.”

Spelling “Rothchilds” as printed, with no second s.

The Policy Behind the Greenback

The greenback was born of the Civil War. By the end of 1861 the war was costing more than taxes and bond sales could pay for, and on December 30 of that year the banks and the Treasury stopped paying out gold and silver coin. Congress answered on February 25, 1862, with the Legal Tender Act. The Treasury would print its own paper money — United States Notes, green on the back — and the law declared the notes a legal tender: a creditor had to take them at face value for a debt, though they were backed by no gold and could not be exchanged for coin. Only customs duties and the interest on the national debt still had to be paid in coin. Two more acts raised the total authorized to $450 million. The Secretary of the Treasury who set the policy in motion, Salmon P. Chase, is the man whose portrait is printed on the $1 note.

The war ended; the argument over the money did not. Creditors and banks wanted the notes called in and burned, and the dollar put back on gold — every note retired made the remaining dollars scarcer and dearer, and debts heavier in real terms. Farmers and debtors wanted the notes kept in circulation, for the same reason in reverse. The Supreme Court first ruled the legal tender law unconstitutional as to earlier debts (Hepburn v. Griswold, February 7, 1870), then reversed itself and upheld the law in full (Knox v. Lee, May 1, 1871).

Congress nonetheless ordered a return to gold. The Resumption Act of January 14, 1875, directed that greenbacks be redeemed in coin beginning January 1, 1879. The soft-money men organized the Greenback Party in 1874, and in the congressional elections of 1878 the Greenback-Labor ticket polled more than a million votes and elected fourteen members of Congress. That same winter, on February 28, 1878, Congress passed the Bland-Allison Act over President Hayes's veto, ordering the Treasury to buy and coin two to four million dollars' worth of silver every month.

It was into that fight — five weeks after the silver act, with gold resumption nine months off — that the St. Paul article on this page was printed in April 1878. "Slaves to gold" was its name for the hard-money policy: contract the paper currency, redeem in gold alone, and let debtors carry the cost.

What gave it value. A greenback could not be cashed in for gold, yet it was never mere paper, and it never became worthless. The first support was the law itself: every debt in the country, public and private, could be paid in greenbacks, so every debtor needed them and every creditor had to take them. The second was the government's own counter: the Treasury accepted the notes for taxes and for everything owed to it — everything, that is, but customs duties. Those were kept payable in gold on purpose, so that the government always had coin coming in to pay the interest on its bonds in gold and keep its credit sound. The third was a door that stood open at the start: until July 1, 1863, any holder could trade his notes at full face value for six-per-cent government bonds, which set a floor under the paper. The fourth was the fence Congress built around the printing press: $450 million was the legal limit, and not a dollar more could be issued. The last support was confidence — the belief that the Union would survive, tax, and someday pay in coin. That belief was priced every day in the New York gold market. On July 11, 1864, with a Confederate army at the edge of Washington, it took $285 in greenbacks to buy $100 in gold, and a paper dollar was worth about thirty-five cents. By December 1878 the notes had climbed back to even money, and on January 1, 1879, the Treasury began redeeming them in gold on demand — and found few takers. Seven months before that day, on May 31, 1878, Congress had forbidden the Treasury to retire the notes at all: what came in was to be paid out again and kept in circulation. The amount then outstanding, $346,681,016, stayed the amount for more than a hundred years.

The limits, year by year. 1862 — $150 million authorized (February 25), raised to $300 million (July 11). 1863 — raised to $450 million (March 3), the most ever allowed; the most ever actually out was $447,300,203.10. 1866 — Congress ordered the notes retired, slowly (April 12); Secretary McCulloch brought the total down to $356 million. 1868 — Congress stopped the retirement (February 4). 1874 — after part of the retired notes had been put out again in the panic of 1873, Congress fixed the maximum at $382 million (June 20). 1875 — the Resumption Act ordered the total worked down toward $300 million as bank notes grew. 1878 — Congress forbade any further retirement (May 31) and froze the amount at $346,681,016, where it has remained ever since.

The law itself. None of this rested on custom or on a Treasury order. The greenback was created by an act of Congress — the Legal Tender Act of February 25, 1862, chapter 33, printed at page 345 of volume 12 of the United States Statutes at Large, where any reader can find it today. Its words made the notes "lawful money and a legal tender in payment of all debts, public and private, within the United States," excepting only customs duties and interest on the public debt. The Supreme Court struck that clause down as to earlier debts in 1870 (Hepburn v. Griswold), then reversed itself and upheld the law in 1871 (Knox v. Lee), and in 1884 upheld it for peacetime as well (Juilliard v. Greenman). The policy was argued in Congress, written into the statute book, and fought through the courts — it is all on the record.
