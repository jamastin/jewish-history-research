The Menorah & Rome

The menorah — the Hebrew word just means “lampstand” — was the great golden lampstand of the Tabernacle and then of the Temple in Jerusalem: a central stem with three curving arms on each side, seven branches, every branch topped with a lamp.

What it was

“And thou shalt make a candlestick of pure gold: of beaten work shall the candlestick be made” (Exodus 25:31).

It was hammered from a single piece of gold — a talent of it, per Exodus 25:39 — worked all over with almond-blossom decoration: cups, buds and blossoms. Josephus, who had seen the Second Temple's menorah with his own eyes, counts seventy ornaments in all. No ancient text gives its height; from the scale of the figure carrying it on the Arch of Titus relief, scholars estimate about five feet — an estimate from the carving, flagged as such.

What it was used for

It stood in the Holy Place, the outer room of the sanctuary, and it was the room's only light. Its seven lamps burned pure olive oil, tended by the priests morning and evening — “to cause the lamp to burn always” (Exodus 27:20). Josephus says three lamps were kept burning through the day and all seven at night; the Talmud calls the westernmost lamp the ner tamid, the perpetual light, from which the others were kindled. That lamp is the direct ancestor of the single eternal light that still burns in synagogues today.

What it meant

The ancient writers read it several ways, and the research files keep their readings separate:

  • A tree — trunk, branches, almond blossoms: the first tree to flower in spring, a sign of watchfulness and new life.
  • The seven days of creation — Josephus, in his Jewish War: the seven lamps were “an emblem of the seven days of creation and rest.”
  • The heavens — Josephus in his Antiquities, and Philo: the seven lamps are the sun, the moon and the five planets then known — a little universe in gold.
  • Wisdom and God's spirit — Zechariah's vision of the golden lampstand (Zechariah 4) hears the words: “Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts.”

The trail to Rome

Babylon carried off the Temple vessels in 586 BC; Antiochus IV looted the menorah in 169 BC; the Maccabees, cleansing the Temple in 164 BC, “made… a new candlestick” (1 Maccabees 4:49–50) — the relighting remembered at Hanukkah, whose nine-branched chanukiah is a descendant of the menorah, not the same object. Herod's rebuilt Temple housed a menorah of the same pattern — the one Rome took in AD 70, carried in Titus's triumph and carved on his arch in Rome. That relief — the oldest picture of the menorah that survives — became, in time, the emblem of the State of Israel.
