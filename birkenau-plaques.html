The Birkenau Monument Plaques

The row of plaques at the International Monument to the Victims of Fascism at Birkenau — and how their wording changed. Researched 2026-11-22.

The monument was chosen by international competition in 1957 (jury chaired by sculptor Henry Moore), designed by a Polish–Italian team led by Pietro Cascella, and unveiled on 16 April 1967 before some 200,000 people.

The four-million figure — the timeline

1945 — the Soviet estimate

The Soviet commission investigating right after liberation estimated four million victims — not by counting, but by multiplying the crematoria's theoretical capacity by the days they ran. Western historians never used the figure; Raul Hilberg's 1961 total for Auschwitz was about one million.

1967 — the plaques go up

The original plaques, in about nineteen languages, read:

"Four million people suffered and died here at the hands of the Nazi murderers between the years 1940 and 1945."

They did not say the victims were mainly Jews.

1990 — the old plaques come down

With communist rule over, the Auschwitz Museum removed them. The museum's own historian, Franciszek Piper, had rebuilt the toll transport by transport from deportation records: about 1.1 million people murdered at Auschwitz, about one million of them Jews.

1995 — the new wording

"For ever let this place be a cry of despair and a warning to humanity, where the Nazis murdered about one and a half million men, women and children, mainly Jews, from various countries of Europe. Auschwitz-Birkenau 1940–1945."

The plaque's "one and a half million" is rounded museum wording; the documented research figure behind it is Piper's ~1.1 million.

How many were killed at Auschwitz — and how we know

The overall numbers never rested on the plaque. They stand on the Nazis' own paperwork and the population record: the Korherr Report, the intercepted Höfle Telegram, the Einsatzgruppen reports, transport records, censuses, and Yad Vashem's millions of victims by name.
